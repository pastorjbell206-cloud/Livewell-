Matthew 5 through 7 is the longest single political speech in the New Testament. The American church has not read it as politics for two centuries. The not-reading has cost us a politics. It has also cost us the Sermon. I want to read it back.

The depoliticized reading of the Sermon on the Mount has been the dominant evangelical approach for roughly the entire history of American evangelicalism. The Sermon has been read as personal ethics, as devotional inspiration, as the ideal we will attain in heaven, as the impossible standard that drives us to grace. Each of these readings has something to say, but each of them suppresses what the text most plainly is — a public address delivered to a public audience, describing the public conduct of a public community, in language that the political imagination of the first century would have recognized as political, not private.

I want to read the text honestly, which means reading it as politics. The reading is uncomfortable. The reading is also what the text actually says.

## What the Sermon on the Mount is

The Sermon on the Mount, in Matthew's gospel, is the inaugural public teaching of Jesus' ministry. Matthew presents it in a way that deliberately parallels the giving of the Torah at Sinai — Jesus on a mountain, delivering law, with the implicit framing that this is a new Moses giving a new law to a new people. The parallel is not subtle. Matthew is announcing, through the structure, that Jesus is constituting something — a community, a polity, a new people who will live by the law he is now giving.

The law itself is delivered to *the disciples* in Matthew 5:1–2, but in the presence of *the crowds* that Matthew 4:25 has just described — crowds from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea, and beyond the Jordan. The audience is both internal and public. The disciples are being constituted as a particular people; the crowds are being put on notice that the constitution is happening.

This is the structure of a political event in the ancient world. A leader gathers his followers on an elevated place. He addresses them in public. He declares the constitution of the people he is leading. The surrounding crowds witness the declaration. The declaration tells the followers how they will live, and tells everyone else what kind of people the followers will be. The Sermon on the Mount, read with attention to its structure, is doing exactly this.

The depoliticized reading misses the structure. The depoliticized reading takes individual teachings — the Beatitudes, the antitheses, the prohibitions on anger and lust and divorce, the instructions on prayer and fasting and almsgiving — and treats them as a collection of moral counsels for the individual believer's inner life. The collection is not the Sermon. The Sermon is a constitution. The collection of teachings is the substance of the constitution. The depoliticized reading has been treating the parts as if they were the whole, and the parts as if they were private, and the result has been a Sermon that says much less than what the text actually says.

## The Beatitudes as political honor reversal

The Beatitudes in Matthew 5:3–12 are not, primarily, inspirational descriptions of inner spiritual states. They are public reversals of the political honor system of the surrounding world.

Every ancient political community had an honor system — a set of categories that named who was honored and who was dishonored, who was favored and who was disfavored, who was first and who was last. The Roman world had its honor system. The Jewish world under Roman occupation had a different honor system. The Beatitudes deliberately invert the honor systems of both.

*Blessed are the poor in spirit* — the Roman world honored the proud. *Blessed are those who mourn* — the Roman world celebrated those who triumphed. *Blessed are the meek* — the Roman world honored the strong. *Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness* — the Roman world honored those who hungered and thirsted for power. *Blessed are the merciful* — the Roman world honored the victorious. *Blessed are the pure in heart* — the Roman world honored the politically clean. *Blessed are the peacemakers* — the Roman empire was, by its own account, peacemaking through conquest; the Beatitudes describe a different peace. *Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake* — the Roman world honored the powerful, not the persecuted. *Blessed are you when others revile you and persecute you on my account* — the political community being constituted will be persecuted; its honor is precisely the honor the surrounding political world will deny it.

This is not inner-spirituality. This is the public declaration of a new political honor system, in deliberate inversion of the honor system the surrounding world recognizes. Members of the polity Jesus is constituting will be those the surrounding world refuses to honor. They will be honored anyway, in the polity Jesus is constituting, by the King who is constituting it. The political reading is not optional; the political reading is what the Beatitudes actually do.

## Salt, light, and the Sermon's political claim

Matthew 5:13–16 contains the salt-and-light passage. *You are the salt of the earth. You are the light of the world. A city set on a hill cannot be hidden.* The depoliticized reading treats these as inspirational metaphors for personal influence. The political reading reads them differently and, in my judgment, more accurately.

The salt-and-light passage is describing the public function of the polity being constituted. The polity is not merely a private community of spiritually-formed individuals. The polity is a *city set on a hill* — a public political reality, visible to the surrounding world, exercising a public function in the world. The polity preserves the world the way salt preserves food, and illuminates the world the way a city's lights illuminate the surrounding territory. The function is public. The function is political, in the sense that it is the function of a polis.

The American evangelical reading of this passage has often treated salt and light as descriptions of individual Christians' personal witness — be good people, share your faith, let your light shine in your workplace. The reading is not entirely wrong. The reading is too small. The text is describing the public function of a community, not just the personal witness of individuals. The community is *the city set on a hill*. The community, as community, is the political fact the surrounding world is being asked to reckon with.

## The antitheses as political reframing

Matthew 5:17–48 contains the antitheses — the *you have heard it was said... but I say to you...* passages. The depoliticized reading treats these as more rigorous personal moral demands. The political reading treats them as the constitution of a different kind of public community.

The prohibition on anger goes beyond the prohibition on murder. The new polity will be a community in which the conditions that lead to murder are also forbidden, because the polity is constituting itself as a community of reconciliation rather than escalating grievance.

The prohibition on lust goes beyond the prohibition on adultery. The new polity will be a community in which the conditions that lead to adultery are also forbidden, because the polity is constituting itself as a community of covenantal sexual practice rather than predatory sexual practice.

The restriction on divorce goes beyond the Mosaic provision. The new polity will be a community in which marriage is structured around covenantal permanence rather than convenience, because the polity is constituting itself as a community of household stability.

The prohibition on oaths goes beyond perjury. The new polity will be a community in which speech is reliable in itself, without the need for sworn guarantees, because the polity is constituting itself as a community of trustworthy public speech.

The restriction on retaliation — *do not resist the one who is evil... if anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also... if anyone forces you to go one mile, go with him two miles* — is one of the most political passages in the entire New Testament, and the depoliticized reading has had the hardest time with it. The passage is describing the public conduct of the polity in its interactions with the surrounding political authorities. The Roman soldier could legally force a Jewish civilian to carry his pack for one mile. The polity Jesus is constituting will carry the pack for two miles. The polity Jesus is constituting will not resist the violence of the empire with violence; it will resist it by demonstrating, in public conduct, that the empire's violence has no final claim on its members.

The love-of-enemies passage in Matthew 5:43–48 is the climax. *Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be sons of your Father who is in heaven.* The political community being constituted will love its enemies. The political community being constituted will be marked by enemy-love. Enemy-love is not a private spiritual disposition; it is the political identity of the polity.

## The verdict

The Sermon on the Mount is the politics of the people of God. It is not negotiable. It is not aspirational. It is not the ideal we will attain in heaven. It is the constitution of the polis we already belong to, the citizenship we already hold, the alternative city we have been carrying around inside the empire for two thousand years.

The American church has, on the whole, read the Sermon in ways that made it survivable. We have spiritualized it. We have privatized it. We have made it about the inner life of the individual believer. We have treated it as the ideal we cannot reach. Each of these moves has had the effect of removing the Sermon from public political force, of letting it land softly, of making it a document we can affirm without being governed by.

The depoliticized reading was a luxury Christendom afforded us. Christendom let us believe that the surrounding political order was, in some general sense, Christian, and so the public politics of the Sermon could be left in abeyance — the surrounding culture would carry the political weight, and the Sermon could be reserved for the inner formation of individual disciples.

Post-Christendom does not afford us that luxury. The surrounding political order is no longer carrying any of the weight the Sermon is calling for. The polity Jesus constituted has to constitute itself again, publicly, in distinction from the surrounding political order, in ways that the surrounding political order will increasingly resist. The Sermon is, again, the constitution. The Sermon was always the constitution. The reading of it as politics is not innovation; it is the recovery of the most plain reading of the text.

I am writing this for the American Christian reader who has been forming his political life on the assumption that the Sermon is private spirituality. The assumption has cost us a politics. The recovery of the Sermon as politics will cost us much of what we have currently been calling our political identities. The cost is real. The cost is worth it. The cost is the price of being governed by what we already say we believe.

The polity already exists. The constitution has been delivered. The question is whether we will let it govern us, or whether we will keep reading it in ways that make it survivable rather than authoritative. We have been choosing the survivable reading for too long. The other reading is harder. The other reading is right. The recovery is what the next generation of faithful Christian political life will be built from. Read the Sermon. Read it slowly. Read it as the political constitution it has always been. Then let it govern you.
