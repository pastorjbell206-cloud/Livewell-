You are angry at God and you have been told to stop.

Maybe not in those words. The church rarely says it that bluntly. It says it sideways — *be careful with that, you don't want to put God in the dock, who are you, O man, to answer back to God.* It says it with a verse fired like a warning shot. And the grieving person, already carrying the loss, now carries a second thing: the suspicion that the rage rising in their chest is itself a sin, a failure of faith, evidence that they were never as devoted as they thought. So they swallow it. They go quiet. They say the right things at the funeral and they stop talking to God altogether, because the only thing they have to say to him is something they have been told they are not allowed to say.

I want to tell you what the prayer book of the Bible actually does with that anger. It is not what you were taught.

## A third of the songbook is complaint

Open the Psalms. This is the hymnal Israel sang, the prayers Jesus prayed, the words the church has carried for three thousand years. And roughly a third of them are laments — not gentle, not composed, but raw complaints flung directly at God by people who believed he had wronged them or abandoned them or fallen asleep at the post.

*How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?* That is Psalm 13, and notice the accusation buried in it. The psalmist is not asking whether God has forgotten him. He is asserting it and demanding to know how much longer it will go on. *Rouse yourself. Why do you sleep, O Lord.* That is Psalm 44, and it is the prayer of people who have been faithful and been crushed anyway, and they say so to his face — *all this has come upon us, though we have not forgotten you.* They are not confessing sin. They are filing a grievance.

This is in the Bible. God did not leave it out. He could have given us a songbook of pure praise, sanitized, untroubled, the kind of worship music that never asks an awkward question. Instead he gave us a songbook with the complaints left in, and he called it Scripture, and his Son sang from it.

## Brueggemann and the prayers we stopped praying

Walter Brueggemann spent his life on these psalms, and he noticed something about the modern church that should disturb us. We have edited them out. Quietly, without a vote, the contemporary church has stopped praying the laments. Our worship runs almost entirely in what he called the psalms of orientation and new orientation — the everything-is-good and the everything-is-restored. The psalms of disorientation, the ones for the season when life has come apart and God seems to have done it, we have dropped from the rotation.

Brueggemann's point was sharp. By cutting the laments, we did not make our faith more reverent. We made it dishonest. We created a worship in which the only permitted speech to God is gratitude, so the person whose life is in ruins has no sanctioned language and must either fake the gratitude or fall silent. We taught people that the way to be close to God is to lie to him about how they feel. And a relationship in which one party is not allowed to say the hard thing is not a close relationship. It is a managed one.

The lament psalms refuse the management. *You have made us like sheep for the slaughter. Rise up; come to our help.* That is covenant speech. It is the speech of someone who is angry precisely because they still believe God is supposed to be on their side, and the anger is the proof that the relationship is still load-bearing. You do not rage at a God you have given up on. You rage at one you are still holding to the promise.

## The accusation that is still prayer

Here is the line we keep missing. The lament is always addressed to God.

The psalmist does not write God off. The psalmist does not turn to the congregation and complain about God, the way we complain about a boss in the parking lot. The psalmist turns to God's face and complains to him. *Why have you forsaken me? Why do you hide? How long?* Every accusation is a second-person accusation. You. You did this. You are absent. You are too slow. The anger is not a turning away. It is the most intense possible turning toward.

This is the difference the church flattened. There is a way to be angry at God that is unbelief — the anger that walks out, slams the door, and never addresses him again. And there is a way to be angry at God that is faith holding on with bloodied hands — the anger that stays in the room and keeps yelling at the one it refuses to stop believing in. The Psalms are full of the second kind. The Psalms canonize it. They make it liturgy. They hand it to the whole people of God as the right way to pray when the world has broken and God appears to have allowed it.

Jesus prayed it from the cross. *My God, my God, why have you forsaken me.* He did not pray *I accept this in serene submission.* He prayed Psalm 22, the lament, the why, the accusation. The sinless one, in his last conscious words, reached for the complaint. If the lament were a failure of faith, the most faithful human who ever lived would not have died with one on his lips.

## The imprecations we are most afraid of

There is a darker corner of the Psalter, and the church has quarantined it almost entirely. The imprecatory psalms. The ones where the praying person asks God to do violence to those who have harmed them. *Break the teeth in their mouths.* *Let death take them by surprise.* The hundred and thirty-seventh psalm, written by exiles weeping by the rivers of Babylon, ends with a line so terrible we never print it on a coffee mug — a blessing pronounced on whoever would dash the infants of Babylon against the rocks. We read past it. We pretend it is not there.

I am not going to defend the sentiment as a moral instruction. It is not one. But notice what the praying person is doing with the rage, and notice what they are not doing. They are not picking up a stone. They are handing the rage to God. The most savage impulse a wounded human being can feel — the desire to see the one who hurt them destroyed — is being carried into the presence of God and set down at his feet rather than acted out with their hands. The psalm is not a permission slip for vengeance. It is the alternative to vengeance. It is what you do with the murderous part of grief instead of murdering.

Grief produces rage. Real grief, the kind that comes from real loss, often comes with a fury that frightens the person feeling it — fury at the drunk driver, fury at the doctor, fury at the disease, fury at God for standing by. The church's instinct is to shush that fury, and the shushing drives it underground, where it festers into something worse. The Psalms do the opposite. They give the fury a direction. They say: bring even that to God. Especially that. Do not clean it up first. Do not make it presentable. The God of the Psalter would rather have your honest rage in his hands than your composed silence in the next room.

## What this does to the relationship

Consider what it means that God put these prayers in the book on purpose.

It means he is not fragile. The God who canonized the laments and the imprecations is not a deity who needs to be managed, flattered, or protected from your real condition. We treat him as though he were — as though a hard word might wound him, or anger him, or finally exhaust his patience and send him away. The Psalms reveal a God who invited the hard word, who built a whole liturgy out of it, who handed his people the vocabulary of accusation and said, in effect, *here, use this, this is how you stay close to me when you cannot feel me.*

A God you must lie to is a small god. A God you can rage at and remain in covenant with is the living God of Israel, the one who wrestled Jacob through the night and let him keep the limp and the blessing both. The intimacy is in the honesty. The closeness is precisely in the freedom to say the unspeakable thing and find that he is still there in the morning.

## What you can do with the anger tonight

I am not going to tell you the anger is a stage you will pass through on a schedule, and I am not going to promise you it resolves into peace by a date certain. I have watched it last. I have sat with people whose anger at God was still raw in the tenth year, and I did not consider them faithless. I considered them honest, which is rarer and harder.

What I will tell you is this. You do not have to choose between your faith and your fury. That was always a false choice, sold to you by people who could not handle a God big enough to be yelled at. Take the anger to him. Say the actual words. *How long. Why. Where were you.* Pray the thirteenth psalm with your teeth clenched if that is all you have.

That is not the failure of your faith. That is your faith, refusing to let go of a God it is furious with.

The opposite of faith was never anger. It was the silence of walking away. You have not walked away. You are still yelling at him.

So was his Son.
