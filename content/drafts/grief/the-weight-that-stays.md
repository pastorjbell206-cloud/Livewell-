They told you it would lift. It did not lift.

The literature of grief is full of stages, and the stages imply a destination. Denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance — laid out like a hallway you walk down, with a door at the end marked *recovery*, on the other side of which the weight is gone and you are yourself again. People wait for that door. They count the months. At the one-year mark they expect to have arrived, because someone implied that the first year was the hard one and the second year was the better one — and when the second year turns out to weigh exactly what the first one did, they conclude that something has gone wrong with them. They grieved incorrectly. They failed the assignment. The grief that doesn't lift becomes, on top of everything else, a verdict against the griever.

I want to tell you that when grief doesn't lift, it is not your failure. I have watched it stay in faithful people for decades. It is not a malfunction of grief. It is what grief is.

## The lie of the closed wound

Somewhere we got the idea that the goal of mourning is to return to the person we were before. To close the wound so completely that the scar disappears and the loss stops registering, and we are, in some sense, restored to factory condition.

We speak of *getting over it*, *moving on*, *finding closure* — language borrowed from the marketplace and from the therapeutic, neither of which has any business legislating how long a mother may miss her son. Listen to where the words come from. We close a deal. We move on to the next thing. We process and finish. The whole vocabulary is the vocabulary of transactions completed and tasks discharged, and we have laid it over the one experience in human life it fits least.

Closure is a real estate term. It means the deal is done and the parties walk away. It has no place at a grave. The people you love are not deals to be closed. The relationship did not end when the breathing did; it changed its form, but you are still in it — still oriented toward someone who is no longer where you can reach them — and the ache of that orientation is not a problem to be solved. It is love with nowhere to go. It is love that has outlived its object and refuses to die on schedule.

So of course it stays. **It stays because the love stays, and you would not want the love to stop — which means you cannot honestly want the grief to stop either, because they turn out to be the same substance.** To ask for the grief to end completely is to ask to stop loving the one you lost. No one who has truly loved will pay that price for relief. You would not trade the ache away if the price of the trade were the love that aches. Nobody would. And so it stays, and the staying is not the wound failing to heal. The staying is the love refusing to die.

## Wolterstorff and the wound that becomes you

Nicholas Wolterstorff, after his son Eric died on a mountain in 1983, wrote one of the few honest things ever published about this. *Lament for a Son* came out in 1987, and it refuses the closure on every page.

*I shall look at the world through tears,* he wrote. *Perhaps I shall see things that dry-eyed I could not see.* He did not expect the tears to stop. He expected to see differently because of them, permanently, for the rest of his life. He was not describing a phase. He was describing a new and permanent way of having eyes.

And then the line I return to most. He wrote that grief does not get smaller; we get bigger around it. The loss stays the same size — it was always the full size of the person who died, and that does not shrink. A child is not a smaller loss in year ten than in year one. But a life can grow up around the wound, the way a tree grows around an iron stake driven into its trunk, the bark slowly enfolding the metal without ever expelling it. The stake is still there. It is inside the wood now, grown over, carried. The tree is larger, and it is also, permanently, a tree with iron in it. That is healing — but it is not the healing we were sold. Nothing was removed. Something was incorporated. The tree did not get its old shape back. It built a new shape around what it could not get rid of.

This is a truer picture than the hallway with the door. There is no door. There is only the slow widening of a life around a thing it will carry to its own grave.

## Why the resurrection does not erase the grief

I am a Christian, and I believe in the resurrection of the dead, and I need to say carefully what that does and does not do — because the church has badly misused it on the grieving, and I have watched the misuse leave a second wound beside the first.

The resurrection is not a reason to stop grieving now. Notice that Jesus, who is the resurrection and the life, wept at the tomb of Lazarus before he raised him. He had the resurrection in his own hands, minutes away, the power to undo the whole catastrophe already coiled in his chest — and he grieved anyway. He did not treat the coming joy as a cancellation of the present sorrow. He let both be fully real at once, in the same body, at the same grave. The Christian is not asked to skip the weeping because Sunday is coming. The Christian is asked to weep as one who weeps toward a morning, which is a different thing entirely than not weeping at all.

Paul wrote it precisely, and we misquote it constantly. We do not grieve *as those who have no hope.* He did not write that we do not grieve. He wrote that our grief has a horizon the hopeless do not have. The grief is the same grief. The tears are the same tears, the same salt, the same exhaustion. What changes is the direction we are facing while we shed them. The resurrection does not lift the weight in this life — it promises that the weight is not the final word, which is exactly not the same as promising you will stop feeling it on a Tuesday in the tenth year. You may feel it on that Tuesday. Many faithful people do. The hope is not that you will stop. The hope is what waits on the far side of all the Tuesdays, after the last of them.

This is why the cheap version of resurrection hope wounds. When we wave the empty tomb at a fresh grief as though it should end the crying, we are not preaching the resurrection. We are preaching against the tears of Christ. He cried at the grave of a man he was about to raise. If the coming resurrection did not stop his weeping, no one should be telling you it must stop yours.

## The widow at the second Christmas

I have learned to distrust the first Christmas after a death. Everyone is watching it. The family closes ranks, the church remembers, the casseroles are still arriving, and the grieving person is carried through the day on a current of attention.

It is the second Christmas I have learned to fear. By then the world has decided the grief should be over. The attention has moved on to a newer sorrow, a fresher crisis, someone else's hard year. And the person is left to discover that the second one is, very often, worse than the first — because now they are grieving alone, without the current to carry them, and they are grieving a second loss on top of the first: the discovery that the grief did not keep its promise to leave.

I have sat with people in that second year and watched them apologize for still being sad. Apologize. As though they had broken a contract they never signed. Someone, somewhere, had taught them that a year was the allotment, and they had gone over, and now they were ashamed of a love that would not shrink to the size the calendar permitted. I have spent more of my ministry talking people out of that shame than almost anything else I do. The grief is not the problem. The lie that it should be gone — that lie is the problem, and it does more quiet damage in the second year than the loss did in the first.

So I have learned to do the opposite of what the calendar wants. I have learned to come back in the second year, and the third, when no one else is coming. To say the name of the dead out loud, on purpose, because the grieving are starving to hear it and everyone around them has decided that saying it would only stir things up. The name was already stirring. It never stopped. The only question is whether they have to carry it alone or whether someone will say it with them.

There is a quiet ministry in that, and the church has nearly lost it. To remember out loud. To mark the anniversary the rest of the world has forgotten. To send a note in March, when the casseroles stopped in January, that says only: I was thinking of him today. The grieving do not need to be reminded of their loss — they have not forgotten it for an hour — they need to be told that someone else has not forgotten it either, that the one they lost still has a place in the memory of more people than the one carrying the grief. That is a thing love can do for the long haul, and it costs almost nothing, and almost no one does it past the first month.

## The faith always made room for the long grief

We act as though lifelong grief were an embarrassment to faith, a thing to be hidden in the back pew. The Scriptures never treated it that way.

Walter Brueggemann spent his life recovering the lament tradition the modern church had quietly abandoned, and his central observation lands directly here: a faith that only knows how to praise has no language for the person whose grief did not end on schedule. Israel kept the laments in the songbook precisely because grief outlasts the funeral. Jacob mourned Joseph for years and refused to be comforted — *I shall go down to my son to the grave mourning* — and the text does not rebuke him for it. David wept over Absalom until his soldiers were ashamed of him, and the wail he made, *my son, my son*, has never stopped being read aloud at gravesides three thousand years later. The Bible is full of people whose grief did not lift, recorded without apology, because the people who assembled it knew what the calendar-keepers forgot.

The longest griefs in Scripture belong to the most faithful people in it. That ought to settle something. If lifelong grief were a failure of trust, the patriarchs and the king after God's own heart would be the great failures. They are not. They are the witnesses. Their tears are in the book on purpose, the way Psalm 88 is in the book on purpose — to tell the person grieving in year ten that they have not wandered outside the faith. They have wandered deeper into it, into the part the brochures left out.

## What you carry, and what carries you

So here is what I will not say. I will not say time heals all wounds, because it does not, and you already know it does not, and you would stop trusting me the moment I said it. I will not give you a stage you are about to graduate from. I will not promise that next year is lighter. I do not know your next year, and I will not lie about it to make this end gently.

What I will say is that the staying of the weight is not a sign that your faith is weak or your grief is broken. It is the shape that love takes once death has had its turn. The people who told you it would lift were trying to be kind, but they were describing a country none of us has ever actually visited — and you are not failing to reach it. You are doing the harder and more faithful thing, which is to keep carrying what cannot be set down, and to keep walking anyway, toward a morning you have been promised and have not yet seen.

The weight stays. I will not pretend otherwise.

But you do not carry it alone, and you do not carry it nowhere. There is a hand under the load that has held heavier ones, and a grave already emptied that tells you which way the dark finally breaks. Not yet. Not on your schedule. But that way, and not the other.

The weight stays. So does he.
