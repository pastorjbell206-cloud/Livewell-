The reasons start arriving before the casket is in the ground.

Someone tells you it was God's plan. Someone tells you that God needed another angel, which is not even good theology, but they mean well and you nod. Someone tells you everything happens for a reason, and you can see in their face that they need this to be true more than you do — that they are not comforting you so much as steadying themselves against a world where a thing like this could happen to a person like you for no reason at all. Someone, eventually, tells you that God will use it. Maybe he will. But you notice that all of them, every one, has arrived with a reason. As if the unbearable thing about your loss were the absence of an explanation, and a good enough sentence might close the wound.

It will not. And the oldest book in the Bible on the subject agrees with you. If you have ever asked, into the dark, why God allows suffering — and gotten back only the sound of people filling the silence — you are in older company than you know.

## Thirty-seven chapters of being wrong

The book of Job is long, and it is long on purpose, and the length is the argument.

A righteous man loses everything in an afternoon — his wealth, his servants, and then, in a single sentence the narrator refuses to slow down for, all ten of his children, killed when the house fell on them at a feast. Job sits in the ashes. He tears his robe. And then his friends arrive, and they do the one thing in the whole book that anyone does right: they sit with him on the ground for seven days and seven nights, and they say nothing, because they see that his suffering is very great.

Then they open their mouths, and for the next thirty-some chapters they explain. They are not villains. They are orthodox. Everything they say is the conventional theology of their day, and much of it sounds like the theology of ours. God is just. God does not punish the innocent. Therefore Job must have sinned, and if Job will only confess, the suffering will lift and the equation will balance. Suffering is the effect; sin is the cause; find the cause and the universe makes sense again and the friends can go home to their own children with the world still safe.

They are wrong. The book tells us at the very start that they are wrong — we, the readers, were shown in the first chapter that Job is blameless, before the friends ever spoke a word. So we watch thirty-seven chapters of confident, articulate, theologically respectable people explaining a man's suffering, and we know the whole time that every explanation is false. The book is, among other things, a sustained portrait of religious people doing harm with their certainty. They came with reasons. The reasons were a second affliction laid on top of the first, and the second one they delivered with their own mouths.

## When God finally speaks

Then God answers. After all the human explaining, the voice comes out of the whirlwind, and here is the thing that should stop us cold. God does not explain.

He does not tell Job about the scene in heaven from chapter one — the wager, the accuser, the reason behind it all. Job never learns why. He dies, at the end of a long restored life, still not knowing why his first ten children were taken. Instead God asks questions — dozens of them, vast and strange and beautiful. *Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth? Have you entered the storehouses of the snow? Can you bind the chains of the Pleiades? Do you give the horse his might? Who provides for the raven its prey?* It is not an argument. It is a tour of a creation too large and too wild for Job to have authored or even to comprehend. God does not say *here is the reason.* God says, in effect, *you are speaking about things larger than you knew — and I am here, and I am not absent, and I am not asleep.*

And Job is satisfied. Not because he got the explanation — he did not — but because he got God. *I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you.* The encounter did what the explanation could not. He had wanted a reason, and he received a presence, and the presence turned out to be the thing he had actually been after the whole time, underneath the demand for an answer.

Then comes the verdict the church loves to skip. God turns to the friends, the orthodox explainers, and tells them he is angry with them — *for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has.* The men who defended God are rebuked. The man who accused him, who demanded an audience, who refused the easy reasons and hurled his complaint at the sky — he is the one who spoke rightly. **Let that land on every comforter who ever arrived with a verse and a theory: God preferred the honest complaint to the tidy defense.** He was angrier at the men who explained his ways than at the man who shouted at him for them.

## Why the explanation would not help anyway

Here is what I have come to believe after years of sitting in these rooms. Even if you could have the reason, it would not do what you imagine it would do.

Suppose God pulled back the curtain and showed you the entire chain of causes — the whole architecture of how this loss serves some good you cannot now see. Suppose you understood it completely, the way Job's friends were so sure they did. Your child would still be gone. The understanding would not return what was taken. It would only convert your grief into a transaction — you lost this in order to gain that — and a person you love is not a price paid for a benefit received. To accept the explanation would be to agree that the trade was worth it. And there is no parent alive who would make that trade, and God, mercifully, does not ask you to. The reason, if you had it, would ask you to sign off on the death of the one you love as a fair exchange. You would refuse. You would be right to refuse.

The free-will defense, the soul-making theodicy, the greater-good argument — the philosophers have built these, and they are not nothing, and I have read them carefully and even taught them to students with a clear conscience. But every one of them works at the altitude of the argument, and grief does not live at that altitude. Grief lives in the kitchen at six in the morning, with one fewer place to set at the table, with the coffee mug that is still in the cabinet because you cannot make yourself give it away. No syllogism reaches that kitchen. No proof has ever set a fourth place. Only a presence reaches the kitchen.

## The ones who stopped explaining

The people who have written most truly about loss are the ones who had built explanations first, and then watched a death take the explanations apart.

C.S. Lewis wrote *The Problem of Pain* in 1940 — a clear, ordered, persuasive account of how suffering fits inside a good God's world. It is a fine book. It is a book written from the outside, by a man who had thought hard about grief without yet being inside one. Then his wife Joy died in 1960, and he wrote *A Grief Observed* (1961), and it is the same man and not the same man at all. The later book does not explain. It records. It refuses the consolations the earlier book had offered so calmly, and the refusal is not a loss of faith. It is faith arriving in the kitchen, where the proofs do not reach. Lewis did not retract *The Problem of Pain.* He simply discovered that a true thing said from a safe distance does nothing for the person under the rubble — and he had become the person under the rubble.

Nicholas Wolterstorff was a philosopher by trade, a man whose whole working life was argument, when his son Eric died on a mountain in 1983 at twenty-five. He wrote *Lament for a Son* (1987), and a philosopher's book it is not. It is fragments, refusals, a flat insistence on the size of the thing. *Don't say it's not really so bad,* he wrote. *Because it is. Death is awful, demonic.* He would not let the theodicies he himself could have constructed shrink the loss to a manageable size. And what he found, where the explanation should have been, was not a reason but a revised picture of God: *God is not only the God of the sufferers but the God who suffers.* He did not get an answer. He got a companion in the ash heap, which Job got too, and which turns out to be the only thing the book of Job ever promised.

## The harm the reasons do

I want to be honest about something the church does not like to admit. The reasons we hand the grieving are not neutral. They are not merely unhelpful. They wound, and they wound in a particular way, and we should name it.

When you tell a grieving mother that God took her child for a reason, you have told her that God is the agent of her child's death and that he had a purpose worth her child's life. You may not have meant that. You almost certainly did not. But that is the sentence she now has to live with, and she will turn it over at three in the morning, and it will curdle. Either God is cruel — having traded her son for some greater good she is not permitted to see — or the comforter was talking nonsense to fill a silence they could not bear. Those are the only two places the sentence leads, and both are worse than the honest *I don't know* the comforter was too frightened to say.

Job's friends did this. They were not malicious. They were anxious. A man was suffering in front of them with no apparent cause, and the absence of a cause threatened their whole picture of an orderly, just universe — so they manufactured a cause and laid it on him. *You must have sinned.* They did it to keep their own world intact. The explanation served the comforter, not the sufferer. It always does. When we explain a grief that is not ours, we are most often steadying ourselves — building a small wall of reasons between us and the terrifying possibility that this could happen to anyone, including us, for nothing. The reasons are not for the bereaved. They are for the frightened.

The most faithful thing the friends ever did was the seven days of silence at the beginning, before they opened their mouths. If they had simply stayed in the ashes and said nothing, the book of Job would be a story about good friends. It became a story about bad theology the moment they decided that the silence needed filling. Most of us cannot leave the silence alone either. We should learn to. The silence, held with someone, is closer to the presence of God than any reason we could invent to break it.

## The God who entered it instead

So the Christian claim is not finally a reason. It is a person.

When God moved to address the suffering of the world, he did not send down a fuller explanation. He did not transmit a better theodicy from a safe distance. He came. He took a body, and the body could be hurt, and it was. He stood at the grave of his friend Lazarus, knowing he would raise him within the hour, knowing the happy ending was minutes away — and the shortest verse in the Bible records what he did first. *Jesus wept.* He did not skip to the resurrection. He did not tell Mary and Martha to cheer up because the explanation was coming and the good ending was already loaded. He stood in the stink of death, among people who were furious he had come too late, and he cried. The God who weeps at a grave he is about to empty is the only God worth having when you are standing at one that will not be emptied this side of the world's end.

That is the answer the Bible gives. Not the reason you asked for. The presence you actually needed. A God who came down into the suffering rather than narrating it from a safe height. A God who, in Job, refused to explain, and in Christ, refused to stay away. The two refusals are the same mercy from two directions.

I cannot give you the reason. I have stopped trying, and I have come to think the stopping was obedience and not defeat. What I can tell you is that the absence of a reason is not the absence of God. He has been in the ashes with Job. He wept at the grave with the sisters. He never told Job why, and he was not, for one moment of that whole long book, absent from him.

He showed up instead. He is still doing it. He has not explained a thing.
