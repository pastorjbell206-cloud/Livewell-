We are very good at Friday and Sunday. We have nothing for Saturday.

Walk into almost any church and you will find a theology of the cross. We can preach it. We have the atonement worked out in five competing models, and we can argue them at conferences. Walk into the same church on Easter and you will find a theology of the resurrection, with trumpets, with lilies, with a sanctuary so full the ushers bring out folding chairs. The two great days of the Christian story are covered. We have books on them. We have hymns for them.

There is a third day, and we have almost nothing.

Saturday. The day the body lay in the tomb. The day after God died and before God rose. The disciples lived a whole day inside it, a real day with real hours, and the Gospels say next to nothing about it because there was nothing to report. No miracle. No appearance. No word from heaven. Just a sealed stone and a dead teacher and a group of people whose entire understanding of the world had collapsed on Friday afternoon and would not be rebuilt until a morning they had no reason to expect.

That is the day most of us are actually living in.

## The day with no content

Notice what Saturday is not. It is not Friday. Friday is catastrophe, and catastrophe at least has shape. Friday you can name what is happening — he is dying, it is finished, the worst is occurring before your eyes. There is a terrible clarity to Friday. The grief has an event to attach itself to.

Saturday has no event. The catastrophe already happened. It happened yesterday, and now there is only the long flat stretch of after, when the casket is in the ground and the relatives have gone home and the dishes have been returned to their owners and you are left with a Tuesday, and another Tuesday, and the slow horror of discovering that life intends to keep being ordinary around a hole it refuses to acknowledge.

Saturday is not Sunday either. This is the part we will not sit still for. We rush the grieving toward Sunday — *he's in a better place, you'll see him again, God works all things together.* All of it may be true. None of it is Saturday. On Saturday the resurrection has not happened yet, and to a person living inside their Saturday, our Sunday talk sounds like someone narrating the ending of a film to a man who is still watching, in the dark, the part where the hero dies. We are not comforting him. We are refusing to be in the theater with him.

The disciples did not have Sunday on Saturday. They had only the dead body and the dark. And the faith they were given, the faith we inherit from them, includes that day. It is on the calendar. The church, in its wisdom, did not skip from Good Friday to Easter. It left Saturday standing, empty, unprogrammed, a whole liturgical day with almost nothing in it but waiting.

## Lamentations does not look away

The Old Testament has a Saturday book. We do not read it.

Lamentations. Five poems written after Babylon destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC, after the temple burned, after the covenant people watched the visible center of their faith reduced to ash and rubble and corpses in the street. The poems do not soften it. *How lonely sits the city that was full of people.* They describe mothers boiling their own children in the famine. They name God himself as the one who did this — *the Lord has become like an enemy.* This is in the Bible. The people of God kept it, the way they kept Psalm 88, because they knew that a faith with no language for the ruin is a faith that goes mute exactly when its people need words most.

And here is the thing the famous middle of the book is doing. In chapter three, in the dead center of the rubble, comes the line everyone quotes — *his mercies are new every morning, great is your faithfulness.* We have made it a chorus. We have sung it at sunrise with our hands raised. But read where it sits. It sits buried in the middle of the most relentless grief in the canon, and the poems do not stop after it. The lament resumes. Chapter four is more ruin. Chapter five ends not in triumph but in a question left open — *unless you have utterly rejected us, and you remain exceedingly angry with us.* That is the last verse. The book ends on a Saturday. The hope in chapter three did not end the grief. It was spoken from inside the grief, and the grief went on around it.

That is the only kind of hope that is any use on Saturday. Not the hope that ends the waiting. The hope you can say in the middle of the waiting without the waiting being over.

## The Christ who waited

There is a line in the old creed we say without noticing it. *He descended to the dead.* On Saturday, the church confessed, Christ did not skip ahead. He went down. He entered the realm of the dead and was, for a day, among them — God himself on the wrong side of the grave, keeping company with everyone who had ever waited in the dark for a rescue that had not come.

Hans Urs von Balthasar, the Swiss theologian, wrote that Holy Saturday is the day God knows what it is to be dead — not merely to die, which is Friday's work, but to be dead, to lie in the silence with no power moving, no light, no word. He called it the day of God's solidarity with the lost. I do not need you to follow the whole theology. I need you to hear the center of it. Whatever Saturday is, God has been in it. Not narrating it from Sunday. In it. On the inside of the stone.

## The disciples did not know it was Saturday

Here is the part that should change how we read the whole story. The disciples did not know they were living in Saturday. We know. We have the book. We have read to the end, and so we cannot fully feel what that day was, because we cannot unknow the morning that is coming. But they could. To them it was not the middle day of a three-act drama. It was simply the day after the end of everything. The story, as far as they could tell, was over, and it had ended in defeat, and there was no act three because there was no reason on earth to expect one.

That is the condition of grief exactly. You do not get to read ahead. When you are inside the loss, you do not experience it as a chapter with more chapters coming. You experience it as the end of the book. The mind cannot manufacture a Sunday it has no evidence for, and the well-meaning friend who insists the Sunday is coming is asking you to feel something the disciples themselves could not feel on the original Saturday. They had walked with him for three years. They had watched him raise the dead. And on Saturday they still sat in a locked room and grieved as people with no hope, because the hope had been crucified the day before.

So if you cannot feel the resurrection right now, you are not less faithful than the apostles. You are in precisely their position. They could not feel it either. Faith on Saturday is not the ability to feel Sunday. Faith on Saturday is staying in the room, keeping the body company, refusing to leave even though the story appears to be over. That is all they did. It turned out to be enough.

## Against the rush to Sunday

I want to say something plainly to the church, because we are the worst offenders. We cannot stand Saturday. It has no program. There is nothing to do on it, no victory to claim and no catastrophe to mourn dramatically, only the flat unbearable middle, and so we hurry people through it. We schedule the grief. We expect the bereaved to be functioning by a certain Sunday, smiling by a certain holiday, *back to normal* by some unspoken deadline we would never say aloud but enforce with our discomfort whenever they are not.

We do it because Saturday frightens us. A grieving person who is not recovering on our timeline is a standing reminder that our theology has a gap in it, that we built a faith of Fridays and Sundays and forgot the long middle, and rather than sit in the gap with them we push them across it. We mistake our own impatience for their healing. We call it encouragement. It is abandonment with a Bible verse attached.

To sit with someone on their Saturday is to do the one thing the disciples managed and we mostly cannot — to stay in the room with the dead and the silence and the absence of any timeline, and to ask for nothing, and to fix nothing, and to simply not leave. That is harder than a casserole. It is harder than a verse. It is what love requires, and most of us run from it.

## What you carry out of this

I am not going to promise you that your Sunday is coming this week. I do not know your calendar and I will not pretend to. Some Saturdays last a season. I have walked beside people whose Saturday lasted years, and a few whose Saturday, as far as I could see, did not break this side of their own grave.

What I can tell you is that Saturday is not godforsaken, even when it feels like the very definition of forsaken. The day with no miracle in it is still a day the Lord was in. He lay in the dark of it. He kept the silence of it. He waited the length of it, with no more evidence of Sunday than you have now.

So you are not waiting alone, and you are not waiting outside the faith. You are waiting inside the one day we forgot to write about. The stone is still sealed. The body is still cold. The morning has not come.

It is Saturday. He is in the tomb with you.
