The advice industry tells couples that marriage is hard but worth it. The advice industry lies.

Marriage is not hard the way running a marathon is hard. Marriage is hard the way being slowly carved by another person is hard. It is the long dismantling of two false selves, and most of us — most of us, if we are honest — flinch at the carving and call the flinching virtue and call the marriage broken when it is finally doing the only thing it was ever supposed to do.

## What marriage actually is

Tim Keller wrote that marriage is the hardest thing because it is the closest thing on earth to what the gospel does to a person. He did not mean that as inspiration. He meant it as warning.

Marriage in the older tradition was not romance. It was covenant. The Hebrew word for it carries the weight of two people swearing an oath that does not depend on how they feel about it on a given Tuesday. The marriage that depends on feelings on a Tuesday is not a marriage. It is a long, slow disappointment with a wedding ring in the middle of it.

The Reformation broke this in ways the Reformers did not intend. Luther and Calvin, who themselves married late and well, recovered marriage from celibate medievalism and re-imagined it as a vocation — a school of sanctification, a place where God forms two people into one body that can carry weight. But the recovery was incomplete. By the time evangelicalism inherited the doctrine three centuries later, marriage had drifted into something closer to companionate romance with a sacramental gloss: two best friends who pray together. The story we tell ourselves about marriage in the American church is this companionate story, baptized.

The story is exhausted. Look at the divorce statistics. Look at the data on marital satisfaction among the generation that bought the companionate story most completely. Look at the way couples in your own circle talk about the marriages they are inside. They are not talking about covenant. They are talking about whether they are happy.

The covenant marriage is not primarily about happiness. It is about formation. The happiness, when it comes, is the byproduct of the formation, not the goal of it.

## What the carving feels like

The first seven years are about discovering that your spouse is not the person you thought you married. The next seven are about discovering that you are not, either. The years after that — if you make it through the second seven — are about discovering what you actually married, which is not a person but a vow, and the vow is doing something to you that nothing else in your life will ever do.

This is not poetic. It is operational.

Your spouse will fail you. You will fail your spouse. The failure is not the end of the marriage; the failure is where the marriage begins to be real. Eugene Peterson called marriage "a long obedience in the same direction," borrowing the phrase from Nietzsche on purpose. The borrowing is the point. The shape of obedience inside a marriage is the same shape it takes everywhere else: showing up after you said you would, after you no longer want to, after you have nothing left to give to it.

Conflict is not a sign the marriage is broken. Conflict is the friction of the carving. The marriages that survive are not the ones with the least conflict; they are the ones in which conflict has been honored — fought through, repaired from, learned from. The marriages that end are usually the ones in which conflict was avoided until the avoidance was the whole relationship and there was nothing left underneath.

Most of what you fight about is not what you are fighting about. The dishes are never the dishes. The money is never the money. The schedule is never the schedule. Underneath the surface argument is the deeper one — the one about whether you are seen, whether you are honored, whether you matter to this person in the way you needed to matter. The deeper argument is the one God is interested in. The deeper argument is the one the marriage is for.

## I have failed her

I have failed Susanna in twelve specific ways I can name and at least thirty more I cannot. I will not list them here. The marriage we have now is the marriage we have because she stayed. The staying was costly. I have started to understand the cost only in the last seven years, and I am forty-six. The marriages I would point to as the strongest ones in my circle of pastors and friends are not the marriages with the least scarring. They are the marriages that did not pretend the scars were not there. They are the marriages in which someone — usually the wife, in the marriages I have watched up close — refused to leave even when leaving would have been justified.

This is not a sermon about staying when you should not stay. There are marriages that should end. There are marriages in which the staying is the abuse. I am not writing about those. I am writing about the marriage that is not abusive but is also not yet what it could be, and you are tired, and you are wondering if you married the wrong person, and the cultural ambient noise tells you that you should follow your heart out of it.

The cultural ambient noise is wrong.

## What the practice actually is

Here is what the marriages that get better actually do, in my watching. Five things, no more, in no particular order, all hard.

They speak the things they would rather not speak. Not in conflict — in calm. Not when they are angry — when they are not. The marriages that fail are usually the marriages in which the hard thing was never named until the not-naming had become the marriage.

They keep doing the small repairs. The repair after the fight matters more than the absence of the fight. A marriage with daily small repairs holds together; a marriage in which fights are avoided and never repaired comes apart all at once, often years later, after everyone has stopped noticing.

They protect time. Not date nights, primarily — though date nights help. They protect the daily fifteen minutes of conversation in which nothing has to be decided. They protect the bed as a place where two bodies remain present to each other. They protect the practice of being known, which is the only thing marriage really gives.

They tell the truth about money. The marriages that come apart over finances are not the marriages without enough money. They are the marriages in which one of the two was hiding something, or in which the money decisions had been made under cover of one person's preferences and called shared. Money is the canary in this coal mine. Watch it.

They do not have the same marriage every year. The marriage in year three is not the marriage in year ten or twenty or thirty. The couples who treat the marriage as a thing that should stay the same end up disappointed; the couples who let the marriage grow, who renegotiate the unspoken contract as they themselves change, end up with a marriage that has carried real weight.

## The verdict

The marriage that gets better is the marriage you stay in long enough to be carved by. You will want to leave. Stay anyway. You will want to fix it; the fixing is mostly waiting. You will want to be understood; you will be understood eventually by the only person whose understanding could ever have been enough, but he is not your spouse. He is the third party to every marriage, and the marriages that last are the ones that figure this out — that there are not two of you in the room but three, and the third is the one who keeps the vow going when the other two have run out of reasons.

The advice industry will tell you that you can have a great marriage in ninety days. You cannot. You can have a great marriage in thirty years. Most of the work is in the waiting. The waiting is the practice. The practice is the marriage.

It will get worse before it gets better. The getting worse is the getting better. You are not broken. You are being carved.
