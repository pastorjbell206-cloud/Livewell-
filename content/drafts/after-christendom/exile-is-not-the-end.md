There is a particular grief that has settled over the church in the West, and most of us have never named it correctly. We call it decline. We call it secularization. We call it the culture turning against us. But underneath all those words is something older and more specific, something the Bible has a name for and a great deal to say about. The name is exile. **The church in the West is in exile, and the worst thing we can do is treat exile as the end of the story when Scripture treats it as one of the most fertile chapters in it.**

I mean exile in a precise sense, not a melodramatic one. Christians in the West have not been deported or imprisoned. What we have lost is something subtler and, in its own way, just as disorienting: we have lost the home-field advantage. For seventeen centuries the culture carried the faith along, assumed it, defended it, made it the default. Now the culture has moved on, and believers find themselves strangers in a place that used to feel like home — holding convictions the people around them no longer share, speaking a language the wider world has stopped understanding. That is exile. And the people who first lived it were not defeated by it. They were forged by it.

## A letter to people who wanted to go home

In 587 BC the Babylonians took Jerusalem, burned the temple, and marched the leading citizens of Judah a thousand miles east into captivity. Everything that had organized their faith — the temple, the land, the king, the whole apparatus that told them God was with them — was gone. And in that wreckage, a confusion took hold that should sound familiar. The exiles assumed this could not last. Surely God would not leave his people in Babylon. Surely the captivity was a brief interruption before the swift return to the way things were.

Into that assumption the prophet Jeremiah sent a letter, and it is one of the most bracing documents in Scripture. Build houses and live in them, he wrote. Plant gardens and eat what they produce. Take wives, have children, let your children marry and have children, so that you multiply there and do not decrease. And then the line that breaks every expectation: seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the Lord on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare (Jeremiah 29:5–7).

Sit with how strange that is. God is not telling them to mount a resistance. He is not telling them to keep their bags packed for the imminent return. He is telling them to settle in, to invest, to seek the flourishing of Babylon itself — the very empire that destroyed their home. The exile would last seventy years. An entire generation would live and die there. And God's instruction was not *endure until you escape.* It was *build, here, now, among these people who are not your own.*

Walter Brueggemann, in *Cadences of Home* (1997), reads this as the founding posture of faith under the conditions we now inhabit. The church in the West, he argues, has to stop imagining itself as the chaplain of the culture and start imagining itself as a community in exile — one that holds its identity without the support of the empire, that sings the Lord's song in a strange land without pretending the land is home. The exiles had to learn to be the people of God without the temple. We have to learn to be the people of God without Christendom. The task is the same.

What Brueggemann saw, and what makes the comparison more than a metaphor, is that exile was the most theologically productive period in Israel's entire history. Almost everything we now treat as the heart of the Old Testament took its final shape in or around the exile. The Torah was edited and compiled by a people who had lost the land it described. The great prophetic books were collected by a community trying to understand why God had let the unthinkable happen. The synagogue — that portable, decentralized, building-light form of gathered worship that would later make the spread of both Judaism and Christianity possible — was an exile invention, born of necessity when the temple was gone. The exiles did not merely survive Babylon. They produced, under the pressure of having lost everything, the most durable religious legacy in human history. Loss did not impoverish their faith. It forced their faith to become portable, and portable faith outlasts empires. That is the promise hidden inside our own dispossession, if we have the nerve to receive it.

## The temptation in exile is to stop being yourself

Exile presents two temptations, and they pull in opposite directions, and the church in the West is being torn between them right now.

The first temptation is assimilation: to quietly become indistinguishable from Babylon. To keep the name Christian while letting the actual content dissolve into whatever the surrounding culture already believes — to seek the welfare of the city by becoming the city. This is the temptation of the exile who is tired of being strange, who wants to belong so badly that he forgets he was sent. Psalm 137 captures the ache underneath it: by the rivers of Babylon we sat down and wept, and our captors demanded songs of Zion, and the question that escaped them was, *how shall we sing the Lord's song in a foreign land* (Psalm 137:1–4). The assimilationist answer is to stop singing it, or to sing whatever Babylon requests.

The second temptation is the opposite: a hostile, defensive separatism that treats the surrounding culture as nothing but the enemy. This exile keeps his identity but loses his mission. He builds his walls high, prays for Babylon's downfall, and waits for the return that will let him punish everyone who made him a stranger. Jeremiah forbids this too. Seek the welfare of the city. Pray for it. The exile is not permitted to hate the place he has been sent.

The book of Daniel is the working model of the narrow path between these two. Daniel and his friends serve in the Babylonian court, learn its language, rise in its administration — they are not separatists hiding in the hills. And yet there is a line. When the food of the king's table would compromise them, they decline it (Daniel 1:8). When the worship of the king is demanded, they refuse it, and accept the furnace. **Daniel shows us that the faithful exile is neither the one who burns Babylon nor the one who becomes Babylon, but the one who serves it, loves it, and will not bow to it.** That is the hardest posture in the world to hold, and it is exactly the posture the church is being asked to recover.

## Exiles, sojourners, and the people we always were

Here is the part the New Testament makes impossible to dodge: exile was never supposed to be a temporary emergency for the people of God. It is the normal condition.

Peter writes his first letter to scattered believers across Asia Minor and opens by calling them exiles of the dispersion (1 Peter 1:1). A few sentences later he urges them, as sojourners and exiles, to abstain from the passions of the flesh (1 Peter 2:11). He is not describing a crisis. He is describing their identity. The church, in its truest self, is a colony of strangers — citizens of a kingdom that is not yet visible, living in a world that runs on other loyalties. Christendom obscured this for so long that we came to believe the opposite, that we were the natives and everyone else was the stranger. That was always the illusion. The collapse of Christendom has simply returned us to the truth Peter assumed from the beginning.

Lesslie Newbigin, who spent decades as a missionary in India and then returned to a Britain that had quietly abandoned the faith, understood this better than almost anyone. He came home to find the West had become a mission field — and that the church there had no idea, because it still imagined itself at the center. His whole later work is an argument that the church in the West must recover the posture of a missionary minority, a community that does not assume the culture and therefore actually has to live the gospel in a way the culture can read. Newbigin saw that losing the center was not the disaster the institutions feared. It was the recovery of an honesty the church had lost when it sat down on the throne.

There is a discipline in this that the comfortable church never had to learn. A missionary cannot assume his hearers share his premises. He cannot quote a verse and expect it to carry authority, cannot invoke a value and expect agreement, cannot trade on a reputation the culture no longer extends. He has to translate, to demonstrate, to earn a hearing he is not automatically granted. Newbigin understood that this is harder work than chaplaincy and also more honest, because it forces the church to know what it actually believes and why, rather than coasting on inherited assent. The exile is a missionary, whether or not he ever leaves home. The land changed around him, and now he must speak to it as a stranger speaks — carefully, lovingly, without the lazy shortcut of assuming he is understood.

To live as an exile is to live without the props. No assumed agreement, no cultural deference, no momentum carrying you along. You build your house and plant your garden in soil that is not your own, and you do it anyway, because the God who sent you into exile is the same God who promised that the exile was not the last word.

## What an exile actually does on a Tuesday

It is easy to talk about exile in the grand register of empires and centuries. It is harder, and more useful, to ask what Jeremiah's letter requires on an ordinary day, because the letter is relentlessly ordinary. Build houses. Plant gardens. Marry. Raise children. Seek the welfare of the city. There is nothing heroic in that list. There is no call to a dramatic last stand, no instruction for a glorious confrontation with Babylon. There is only the slow, unglamorous business of living a faithful life in a place that does not share your faith.

This is, I think, the most overlooked instruction in the whole prophetic literature, and it is the one our moment most needs. The temptation under pressure is always toward the dramatic gesture — the culture-war battle, the defiant stand, the symbolic victory that feels like resistance and accomplishes nothing. Jeremiah forbids the drama and commands the ordinary. The exile changes Babylon not by conquering it but by being a good neighbor in it: by raising children who know who they are, by building things that bless the people around them, by seeking the genuine welfare of a city that is not his home. The witness is in the gardens. It is in the houses. It is in the quality of a life lived faithfully where no one is watching for it. A church that learns to seek the welfare of its city — its actual city, its schools and streets and struggling families — without demanding the city's allegiance in return is a church that has understood the letter. Most of our energy goes the other direction, toward demanding the allegiance and neglecting the welfare. The exiles were told to do the opposite, and they were told it would be enough.

## What is on the far side of seventy years

Jeremiah's letter does not end with the instruction to settle in. It ends with a promise, and the promise matters precisely because it comes only after the long obedience.

For I know the plans I have for you, declares the Lord, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope (Jeremiah 29:11). That verse has been printed on a million coffee mugs and stripped of everything that gave it weight, because it has been torn out of its setting. It is not a promise of a comfortable life. It is a promise made to people who have just been told they will die in Babylon — that beyond the seventy years they cannot see, beyond their own lifetimes, God has not abandoned the story. The exiles had to build a life they would never see vindicated. They had to plant gardens whose harvest would feed grandchildren they could only imagine. The hope was real. It was also long.

I find this clarifying rather than discouraging. The church in the West may be in for a long exile. The recovery, if it comes, may not come on my watch or in the lifetime of anyone reading this. We may be the generation that plants gardens for a harvest we will not eat. **But exile is not the end of the people of God. It is the place where the people of God remember that their hope was never in the empire to begin with.** The temple fell, and the faith did not. Babylon rose and Babylon fell, and the faith outlived it. The exiles who built houses and sought the welfare of the city were not the ones who lost. They were the ones who kept the story alive long enough to hand it back.

So build the house. Plant the garden. Seek the welfare of the city that no longer agrees with you, and pray for it, and refuse the furnace, and do not pack your bags for a return to a Christendom that is not coming back.

There is a strange freedom in accepting the exile rather than fighting it. The energy the church spends trying to claw its way back to the center is energy it cannot spend on the work in front of it — the neighbor, the city, the slow formation of a people who know who they are in a place that has forgotten. Acceptance is not surrender. The exiles who built houses in Babylon were not surrendering; they were refusing to waste their lives in a bitterness that accomplished nothing. They were free to live, because they had stopped pretending they were somewhere else. The church that accepts its exile is freed in the same way — freed from the nostalgia that drains it and the panic that distorts it, freed to do the only thing it was ever finally asked to do, which is to be faithful in the place it has actually been sent. That place is no longer the center. The center was never the point.

The exile is long. It is not the end. It was never the end.
