A man I know turned seventy this year, and he can remember when the stores in his town closed on Sunday. Not the religious stores. All of them. The hardware store, the bank, the diner on the corner — closed, because the assumption underneath the whole arrangement was that Sunday belonged to God and you did not need to argue the point. Nobody voted on it. Nobody had to. It was simply the air. **That air is gone, and its disappearance is the most important fact about the church in the West, more important than any election, any scandal, any worship style we keep fighting about.** What is ending is not the Christian faith. What is ending is Christendom, and the two were never the same thing, though we spent seventeen centuries confusing them.

The word *christendom* is worth slowing down on, because most people use it to mean "a lot of Christians" and that is not what it means. Christendom is a civilizational arrangement in which Christianity is the default — the assumed center, the unquestioned background, the faith you were born into rather than the faith you chose. Stuart Murray, in *Post-Christendom* (2004), defines it as the system in which Christian beliefs and the institutional church occupied the center of Western culture, with the state's coercive power standing behind them. Under Christendom you did not become a Christian. You simply were one, the way you were English or French or, later, American. The church did not have to make its case. The case had already been made for you by the time you could speak.

That arrangement had a beginning. It will have an end. We are living in the end.

It is worth saying at the outset why this matters beyond the walls of the church, because the death of Christendom is not only a religious event. It is a civilizational one. For most of Western history, the rhythms of ordinary life — the calendar, the holidays, the moral vocabulary, the very shape of the week — were organized around a Christian center that almost no one questioned. When that center dissolves, it does not simply leave a hole where God used to be. It changes what a society assumes about meaning, about death, about the worth of a human life, about whether there is any story large enough to belong to. The people who feel the loss most acutely are often not the devout but the merely cultural — the ones who never thought much about faith and are now discovering, with a vague unease, that the floor they stood on was held up by something they had stopped believing in. That unease is the texture of our moment. It is worth understanding where it came from.

## How a persecuted minority became the establishment

To see what is dying, you have to see how it was born, and the birth was not gentle.

For its first three centuries the church had no power at all. It was illegal, intermittently hunted, socially suspect — a movement of slaves and women and tradesmen meeting in houses, refusing the incense the empire demanded. And it grew anyway. Alan Kreider, in *The Patient Ferment of the Early Church* (2016), spent a career asking how, and his answer is unsettling to a results-driven age: the early church grew slowly, without strategy in any sense we would recognize, through the sheer strange attractiveness of transformed lives. It fermented, like dough. It did not campaign. It had no access to the levers a modern church reaches for first, and that absence turned out to be the engine.

Then came the turn. In 312, on the eve of a battle at the Milvian Bridge, the emperor Constantine claimed a vision and won, and Christianity went from hunted to favored almost overnight. The decisive blow came later. In 380, the emperor Theodosius issued the Edict of Thessalonica and made Nicene Christianity the official faith of the Roman Empire. What had been a confession you might die for became a status you were born into. Within a century the same church that had buried its martyrs was prosecuting heresy with the machinery of the state.

This is the hinge of the whole story, and I want to be fair to it before I name its cost. The Constantinian settlement was not simply a fall. Tom Holland, no apologist when he began, argues in *Dominion* (2019) that the moral assumptions the modern West treats as self-evident — the dignity of the weak, the equal worth of every person, the strange instinct that the victim might be in the right — are not natural to humanity at all. They are the slow sediment of Christian teaching, dripped into the groundwater of civilization over a thousand years of Christendom until even the atheist drinks them without knowing the source. Christendom built hospitals, universities, the very concept of human rights. It was not nothing. It was, in many ways, a gift.

But here is what the gift cost.

The cost was hidden inside the gift, the way it usually is. When the church gained the power of the state, it gained the ability to enforce belief, and the ability to enforce belief slowly eroded the difference between believing and complying. A faith you can be punished for refusing is a faith that no longer needs to persuade. Over the centuries that followed, the machinery of Christendom grew impressive and the conviction underneath it grew thin, because the machinery did the work the conviction used to do. Heretics were tried, dissenters silenced, and the borders of Christendom defended with armies — and at every step the church looked stronger while becoming, in the deepest sense, weaker. The martyrs had died for a faith no one could compel. Their descendants ruled over a faith no one could escape. These are not the same thing, and the distance between them is the distance the church has been falling ever since.

## The water we were swimming in

When a faith becomes the default, something happens to it that is almost impossible to see from the inside.

It stops being chosen. And a faith that is never chosen slowly stops being believed, even while everyone keeps attending. Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), describes the long shift from a world in which it was nearly impossible *not* to believe in God to a world in which belief is merely one option among many, and often not the easiest one. Under Christendom, unbelief was almost unthinkable. The whole social order assumed God. To doubt was to step outside the community itself. That is a powerful thing — and it is also a trap, because it means the faith was being held up by the scaffolding of culture rather than by the conviction of the heart.

Take away the scaffolding and you find out what was load-bearing.

That is precisely what the last two centuries have done. The scaffolding has come down — the Sunday laws, the prayer in the schools, the assumption that a respectable person attended somewhere. And as it came down, what looked like a vast Christian society turned out to be, in large part, a vast Christian habit. The people who genuinely believed kept believing. The people who had merely inherited the habit let it go, and they are letting it go faster every decade. **We are not watching the death of Christianity. We are watching the death of the habit of Christianity, and we keep mistaking the corpse of the habit for the corpse of the faith.**

This is why the panic in much of the church is misdirected. The numbers that frighten us — the empty pews, the rising count of those who claim no religion — measure the collapse of Christendom far more than they measure the collapse of belief. The default is dying. Something always dies when a default dies. The question is whether we have the courage to ask what is actually in the coffin.

Stuart Murray pressed exactly this question, and his answer reframes the whole grief. Post-Christendom, he argued, is not the territory after Christianity but the territory after Christianity's privilege. It is the condition in which the church operates from the margins rather than the center, as one voice among many rather than the assumed authority, without the support of the state or the deference of the culture. Murray listed the shifts plainly: from the center to the margins, from a majority to a minority, from settlers to sojourners, from privilege to plurality, from control to witness. Read that list and notice how much of it describes a loss of *standing* rather than a loss of *truth*. The church is being moved from the head table to the edge of the room. The gospel it carries to the edge of the room is precisely the same gospel it carried to the head table. Only the seating has changed. We are grieving the seating as though it were the supper.

## The kingdom was never the empire

Scripture saw this coming, and said so plainly, long before Constantine was born.

When Daniel interprets Nebuchadnezzar's dream in Daniel 2, he describes a succession of empires as a great statue — gold, silver, bronze, iron — magnificent and doomed. And then a stone, cut from a mountain by no human hand, strikes the statue and shatters it, and the stone becomes a mountain that fills the whole earth (Daniel 2:34–35, 44). The kingdom of God enters history not as another empire in the sequence but as the thing that outlasts every empire in the sequence. It is not made by hands. It does not need the iron of Rome. Christendom, for all its grandeur, was an attempt to make the stone behave like the statue — to give the unmade kingdom the machinery of the made empires. It worked, for a while. The statue always works for a while.

Jesus was even blunter. Standing before Pilate, before the full weight of Roman power, he said that his kingdom was not of this world, that if it were, his servants would fight (John 18:36). The whole logic of Christendom was an effort to make his servants fight after all — to give the kingdom the soldiers it had declined. And whenever the church has reached for that power, it has won territory and lost the very thing the territory was supposed to protect.

There is a pattern worth naming here, because it recurs every time the church touches the empire. The faith gains the world's power and loses its own. It gains the ability to compel and loses the ability to compel by love alone. It gains cathedrals and loses house churches, gains professional clergy and loses the priesthood of ordinary believers, gains the calendar and the schools and the laws and loses the strange, unrepeatable thing that made pagans say of the early Christians, *see how they love one another.* This is not an argument that the church should have refused Constantine, which is a question no one living can usefully relitigate. It is an observation that the trade had a cost, the cost was always coming due, and the bill is the thing we are now holding in our hands and calling decline. The decline is, in part, the long-deferred invoice for the throne.

Look at how Jesus actually described the kingdom's growth. A mustard seed, the smallest of seeds, becoming a tree. Leaven, hidden in the dough, working invisibly through the whole lump (Matthew 13:31–33). Not an army. Not an edict. Not an emperor's vision before a battle. A seed and a hidden ferment — exactly the slow, powerless, unstrategic growth Kreider found in the first three centuries, before the church traded the ferment for the throne. The images Jesus chose are images of a minority that works from below. Christendom made the church a majority that ruled from above. We have spent so long as the leaven baked into the loaf that we forgot leaven is supposed to be hidden, and small, and other than the bread it changes.

## What the end actually requires of us

So the story is ending, and I will not pretend I know exactly what comes next, because no one living has stood here before.

We are the first generations of Western Christians in seventeen hundred years to face a world where the faith is not the default — and that means we cannot simply repeat what worked for our parents, because what worked for our parents was, in part, the borrowed momentum of a culture that no longer exists. The pastor who keeps preaching to the building as though the town outside still assumes the gospel is preaching to a Christendom that has already left the room. I have done this. I have stood in my own pulpit and addressed an audience of the imagination — a town that nodded along to the basics — when the actual town outside my doors no longer knew the story at all.

Here is what I have come to believe the end requires, and it is not what the anxious voices say.

It does not require that we recapture the culture, seize the institutions, and rebuild the scaffolding by force. That is Christendom's own logic, and it will fail, and it deserves to fail, because the seed was never meant to be the statue. What the end requires is that we learn, again, to be the kind of church that grew when it had nothing — no Sunday laws, no favored status, no assumption working in its favor. A church that persuades rather than presumes. A church whose lives are strange enough to provoke the question the early church provoked: *why are these people not afraid.* We are being handed back the conditions of our own beginning. That is not a punishment. It may be a mercy.

The man who remembers the closed stores grieves something real, and I will not mock his grief. A world is ending, and it had genuine goods in it. But Christendom was the scaffolding, not the building. The scaffolding is coming down. **The question is no longer whether the West will be Christian by default. It will not be. The question is whether the church will remember how to be the church when nothing in the air agrees with it anymore** — whether, stripped of the empire, we can still be the seed.

I think often of what the early church did not know it had. It did not know it was at the beginning of anything. It had no sense that Constantine was coming, no inkling that its houses of worship would one day be cathedrals and its bishops would advise emperors. It simply lived, and suffered, and buried its dead, and held to a story the world found absurd, and the holding turned out to be the thing that mattered. We are at the other end of the arc now, watching the cathedrals empty and the bishops lose their seats at the table. But the church before Constantine and the church after Christendom are closer to each other than either is to the long middle, and that kinship is a gift. We are not the first Christians to live without the empire's help. We are only the first in a very long time to have to remember how.

The stone was never the statue. It was always the stone. And the stone outlasts the gold.
