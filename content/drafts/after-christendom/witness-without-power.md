By the year 300, before any emperor had lifted a finger to help it, the Christian movement may have numbered in the millions across the Roman world. It had no army. It held no offices of consequence. It controlled no schools, no courts, no press, no levers of the imperial machine. Its members were disproportionately the people Rome counted as nothing — slaves, women, the urban poor, the kind of people whose deaths did not trouble the historians. And yet within a few generations this powerless movement had so saturated the empire that an emperor would find it expedient to join it rather than crush it. **The early church grew not despite having no power but, in a sense we have nearly lost the ability to understand, because of it.** That is the fact a declining Western church most needs to sit with, because it is being handed back the exact conditions under which the faith first spread like fire.

We have spent so long assuming that influence requires position — a voice in the room, a seat at the table, a hand on the wheel — that we read the loss of those things as the loss of the faith's future. The first three centuries say otherwise. They say the future of the faith was never tied to its position. It was tied to its life.

## The math does not work, and that is the point

Rodney Stark, a sociologist rather than a theologian, took up the question with cold-eyed numbers in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996), and his answer is more unsettling than any sermon. The church did not grow through mass conversions or dramatic public victories. It grew at a steady, almost modest rate — somewhere in the range of forty percent per decade, Stark estimated, the same rate by which modern movements like the early Mormons grew. Compounded over three centuries, that ordinary rate produces an extraordinary result. A few thousand at the death of the apostles becomes a few million by Constantine. The growth was exponential precisely because it was relational and unhurried — person to person, household to household, friend to friend.

But Stark's most provocative finding is *why* people stayed. When plague swept the cities of the empire — and it did, catastrophically, more than once — the pagans fled, abandoning even their own sick to die alone in the streets. The Christians stayed. They nursed the dying, their own and their neighbors', often catching the disease and dying themselves. The simple fact that basic care raises survival rates meant that Christian communities came through the plagues stronger, with more survivors bound by gratitude and awe. The watching world drew the obvious conclusion: these people have something we do not. They are not afraid of death the way we are afraid of it.

That is witness without power. Not an argument won in the forum. A body, fevered and dying, refusing to leave the bedside of a stranger. The math of the empire said the Christians should have died out. They multiplied, because the thing they had could not be killed by the thing the empire used to kill everything else.

Stark pressed the point further, into territory that should make a modern church uncomfortable. The early Christians, he showed, were disproportionately attractive to the people the empire treated worst. They welcomed women in a culture that discarded infant girls on rubbish heaps to die — and so their communities had more women, more marriages, more children, more growth. They cared for widows and orphans the wider society ignored. They crossed the ethnic and class lines that everyone else policed. The result was that the church became a refuge for exactly the people who had nothing to gain from the existing order, and a great deal to gain from a community that treated them as though they mattered to God. This was not a marketing strategy. It was the simple outworking of believing that every person bore the image of God, applied without exception in a world that made endless exceptions. The empire ran on a hierarchy of worth. The church ignored the hierarchy, and the people at the bottom noticed first.

## Patience was a strategy nobody chose

Alan Kreider, returning to him from a different angle, argues in *The Patient Ferment of the Early Church* (2016) that the early church's defining trait was patience — and that this was not a tactic but a character, formed in people who genuinely believed God was at work and they did not have to force the outcome.

This matters because it cuts against everything a results-driven, growth-obsessed church instinctively reaches for. The early Christians did not evangelize the way we do. They did not, for the most part, stand in public and preach to crowds; that was rare and often dangerous. They did not run programs. What they did, Kreider shows, was live in a way that was patiently, persistently strange — and that strangeness raised questions in their neighbors that the Christians were then ready to answer. The candidates for baptism were often made to wait years, formed slowly into a new way of being before they were admitted. The church was not trying to maximize conversions per quarter. It was trying to produce people whose lives were the argument.

A church that has lost its power is freed, whether it wants to be or not, from the illusion that it can force the harvest. It can only be patient. It can only ferment. **When you have no power to coerce belief, the only thing left is to live in a way that makes belief plausible — and that, it turns out, is the only thing that ever truly worked.** Christendom let us forget this, because Christendom gave us levers, and levers are faster than lives. The levers are being taken away. We may discover that we were always meant to ferment, not to push.

There is a cost to this recovery that I do not want to soften. Patience is agonizing for a church trained to measure itself by quarterly results. Fermentation cannot be reported on a chart. The early church's slow formation of disciples over years, its refusal to inflate its numbers by lowering the bar, its willingness to let a person mature before declaring them a member — all of this runs against every instinct of a movement anxious about its survival. When you are afraid you are dying, the temptation is to grasp for the fastest growth available, and the fastest growth is always the shallowest. Kreider's point is that the early church grew deep precisely because it refused to grow fast, and that the depth was the durability. The candidate who waited three years for baptism was a candidate the next persecution could not easily frighten away. We have largely inverted this, building wide and shallow, and the shallowness is now draining out the same door it came in. The early church's patience was not passivity. It was a confidence that the work was God's and the timing was God's, and that confidence is the precise thing a panicked church most needs and least has.

## God chose the foolish thing on purpose

None of this is an accident of history that the New Testament merely records. It is the announced strategy of God, and Paul states it with a bluntness that still scandalizes.

The word of the cross is folly to those who are perishing, he writes, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God (1 Corinthians 1:18). Then he drives it further than comfort allows. God chose what is foolish in the world to shame the wise. God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong. God chose the low and despised in the world, even things that are not, to bring to nothing things that are — so that no human being might boast in his presence (1 Corinthians 1:27–29). Read that as a description of the church's social makeup in the first century and it is simply accurate. The movement really was composed of the foolish, the weak, the low. But Paul is not apologizing for this. He is claiming it is the design.

The center of the faith is an instrument of imperial execution. The cross was Rome's way of displaying its power — the most public, most humiliating death the empire could inflict, reserved for slaves and rebels, a billboard of who was in charge. And the church took that symbol of total powerlessness and made it the throne. The God of the Christians did not overcome the empire by becoming a greater empire. He overcame it by being crucified by it and rising anyway. The method is in the message. You cannot preach a crucified Lord and then insist that the kingdom advances by strength.

This is why Paul could write, of his own weakness, the sentence that should govern every church now learning to live without clout: God's grace was sufficient for him, for God's power is made perfect in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9). Not tolerated in weakness. Not despite weakness. Made perfect in it. The thinning of the church's cultural power is not, on this logic, the frustration of God's work. It may be the precondition of it.

Notice that Paul had asked God three times to remove the weakness, the thorn in his flesh, and three times the answer was no. He wanted strength and was given grace instead, and he came to understand that the two were not the same and that the grace was better. This is the prayer the Western church is praying right now, mostly without realizing it. Lord, restore our power. Lord, give us back the culture, the influence, the deference, the thorn-free strength we used to have. And it is possible — I think it is likely — that the answer is the same answer Paul received. No. My grace is enough. My power works through what you are calling your weakness. The church that keeps demanding its strength back may be asking God to undo the very condition in which his power is most clearly his own and not ours. The thorn was not Paul's failure. It was the thing that kept the power from being mistaken for his.

## Hauerwas and the temptation we keep failing

Stanley Hauerwas has spent a career insisting that the church's first political task is not to seize the levers of the state but to be the church — a community whose common life is itself the witness, a people who do not need to run the world in order to show the world another way to live. The book of Acts is his evidence, whether he cites it or not. Look at what the church actually does in those first chapters: it devotes itself to teaching and fellowship and the breaking of bread; it holds its possessions loosely and meets the needs of its own; it bears witness boldly and is hauled before the authorities for it; and when the authorities threaten, it does not organize a faction to take power but prays for boldness to keep speaking (Acts 2:42–47; 4:23–31). The early church's response to opposition was never to grasp for control. It was to ask God for courage and go back to the work.

There is a deep consistency in this that Hauerwas presses harder than most are comfortable with. The church does not first have a message and then, separately, a way of life that may or may not match it. The way of life is the message. A community that forgives because it has been forgiven, that shares because it has received, that welcomes the stranger because it was once a stranger to God — that community is making an argument no sermon can make, an argument the watching world can examine and not refute, because the evidence is a set of actual people living an actual life. When the church has political power, it is tempted to let the power accomplish what the life was supposed to accomplish, to enforce by law what it could only ever commend by witness. And every time it makes that trade, it weakens the very thing that made it credible. The kingdom advances by being a contrast, not a coercion. A church that rules looks like every other ruler. A church that serves, forgives, and suffers looks like its Lord, and that resemblance is the only argument that has ever finally persuaded anyone.

We keep failing this test, and I include myself and my own tribe in the failure. Every time the church senses its power slipping, the loudest voices call for more power — capture the institutions, win the elections, reclaim the culture by force of numbers and law. It is the most natural instinct in the world, and it is the precise instinct the early church did not have, because it had no power to be tempted by. Now that ours is genuinely going, we will find out whether we believe what we say about the cross, or whether Christendom taught us to trust the sword after all.

The early church conquered Rome by refusing to fight it on Rome's terms. It out-loved, out-suffered, and out-died an empire that had every advantage. **The question for the church after Christendom is not how to get our power back. It is whether we ever needed it, or whether the power was the thing that nearly cost us the gospel in the first place.**

I want to name what this asks of pastors in particular, because we are the ones most tempted to measure our faithfulness by our reach. For a long time the pastor in the West was a person of standing — consulted, deferred to, given a microphone in the public square by default. That standing is going, and its going feels, from the inside, like a loss of effectiveness, even a loss of calling. It is neither. The early church had no pastors with public standing; it had elders who were as likely to be arrested as honored. What it had instead was a community whose ordinary members lived in a way that made the gospel visible, so that the witness was carried not by a few professionals with platforms but by the whole body, scattered through the city, loving in ways the city could not explain. A pastor after Christendom is not a public figure managing the church's influence. He is a person forming a people whose shared life is the argument. That is a smaller role and a truer one, and I am still, most days, learning to want it.

A dying man held the hand of a stranger in a plague-emptied city, and an empire was changed. That is the method. It was always the method. We just got rich enough, for a while, to forget it.
