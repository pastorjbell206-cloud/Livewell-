When something large dies, the first grief is indiscriminate. You mourn everything at once — the good and the rotten, the essential and the accidental, all of it heaped together in the loss. Only later, when the shock thins, can you begin the harder work of sorting: this part I will carry forever, that part I am quietly relieved to be free of. The church in the West is in the first stage. Christendom is dying, and we are mourning all of it at once, the way you do at a funeral before you have had time to think. **But not everything that is dying deserves to be mourned, and the most important spiritual work of this generation is learning to tell cultural Christianity apart from the faith it was wrapped around — because they are dying together, and only one of them is supposed to.**

This is a distinction the comfortable years let us blur. When the culture and the faith point the same direction, you never have to ask which one you actually serve. The default did the sorting for you. Now the default is gone, and the question can no longer be avoided. What, exactly, are we afraid of losing? And is all of it worth keeping?

## The difference cultural Christianity was built to hide

Cultural Christianity is the version of the faith you can hold without it costing you anything, because everyone around you holds it too.

It is Christianity as respectability, as social glue, as the assumed background of a decent life. It fills pews at Christmas and Easter and weddings and funerals. It says grace at the table out of habit and votes its tribe out of loyalty and would be offended to be called anything but Christian, while carrying almost none of the actual weight of following a crucified Lord. It is not hypocrisy exactly — most of the people holding it are sincere. It is something subtler: a faith that has been so thoroughly merged with a culture that no one can tell anymore where the gospel ends and the customs begin. Under Christendom this version flourished, because the culture rewarded it and demanded nothing in return.

The death of Christendom is, first and most visibly, the death of *this.* The nominal believer is leaving. The person who attended because attendance was expected, who identified as Christian because identifying otherwise was unthinkable — that person is melting away with the social pressure that produced them. And here is the part the church struggles to say out loud: the loss of the nominal believer is not, in itself, the loss of anything the gospel ever promised to keep. Jesus never recruited the nominal. He kept thinning his own crowds. When the teaching got hard in John 6, many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him, and he did not chase them; he turned to the twelve and asked if they would leave too. Christendom reversed that dynamic for seventeen centuries, swelling the crowds by lowering the cost. The collapse of Christendom is simply the cost returning.

I want to be careful here, because this is the point where the argument can curdle into something ugly — a smug satisfaction at the departure of the lukewarm, a relief that the crowd is thinning so the remnant can feel pure. That is not the spirit of any of this, and Jesus weeping over Jerusalem forbids it. The nominal believer leaving is still a person, often a person the church failed — handed a faith too thin to hold, never shown the real thing, never given a reason to count the cost because no one ever told them there was a cost. We do not get to be glad they are gone. We get to ask why the only Christianity many of them ever encountered was the cultural counterfeit, and whether the church that handed them the counterfeit was not itself the problem. The death of cultural Christianity indicts the church before it indicts the people who are leaving it. They are setting down what we gave them. The question is why we gave them so little.

## What Bonhoeffer saw from a prison cell

Dietrich Bonhoeffer saw all of this coming from inside a Nazi prison, and his letters from those months contain one of the strangest and most prophetic phrases in modern theology.

Writing to his friend Eberhard Bethge in 1944, in the letters later gathered as *Letters and Papers from Prison*, Bonhoeffer began sketching what he called a "religionless Christianity." He saw that the world was becoming what he named *mündig* — come of age, no longer needing God as the explanation for the gaps in its knowledge or the prop for its morality. The old religious scaffolding, he argued, was collapsing, and good riddance to much of it. He had watched the respectable, churchgoing Christianity of his own nation bless the regime that was murdering its way across Europe. He had seen what cultural Christianity was worth when the test came: nearly nothing. The pastors who had the most religion had the least faith. Bonhoeffer was not mourning the death of religious Christendom. He was asking what kind of faith could possibly survive its death — a faith stripped of its cultural supports, a Christianity that followed Christ rather than merely invoking him.

He paid for that faith with his life, hanged at Flossenbürg weeks before the war ended. And the witness of his dying is the answer to his own question. **What Bonhoeffer proved is that when cultural Christianity dies, the faith that remains is smaller, costlier, and immeasurably more real** — the faith that will follow Christ to a gallows because it was never propped up by anything but Christ in the first place.

Charles Taylor names the modern condition Bonhoeffer was living into. In *A Secular Age* (2007) he describes the "cross-pressures" of our moment — the way belief and unbelief now press on everyone, so that the believer feels the pull of doubt and the unbeliever feels the haunting of transcendence, and no one gets to rest in an unexamined default. Cultural Christianity cannot survive cross-pressure, because cultural Christianity was always an unexamined default. Real faith can survive it. Real faith was always examined; it was always chosen against alternatives. The cross-pressured world is brutal to nominal belief and oddly clarifying for the genuine kind.

## Two things are buried in the same grave

So let me say plainly what I think dies, and what does not, because the grief needs sorting and no one else seems eager to do it.

What dies: the assumption that a respectable person is a Christian. The political reflex that fused the faith to a flag and a party and a nostalgic vision of a nation. The crowds that came for belonging and stayed for nothing. The cultural prestige of the pastor, the automatic deference, the reserved seat at the civic table. The mistaking of church membership for discipleship. The whole apparatus by which the culture did the work the Spirit was supposed to do. These are dying, and when I am honest, I cannot fully grieve them, because much of this was the very thing that hollowed the church from the inside while it looked strong from the outside. A church that has to be defended by social pressure is already dead; it just has not been told.

What does not die: the gospel. The cross and the empty tomb. The presence of God with his people. The Scriptures, which were written largely by and for a persecuted minority and lose nothing when their readers become a minority again. The communion of saints across twenty centuries. The Spirit, who was not granted by Constantine and cannot be revoked by a census. None of this is tied to Christendom. None of this depends on a culture's agreement. The faith that raised the dead in a hostile empire does not need a friendly one to survive.

There is a second list worth making, harder than the first, because it concerns the genuine goods that Christendom carried and that we will miss when they are gone. The instinct to care for the weak, which Tom Holland traced to Christian roots, was real, and a culture that forgets its source may forget the instinct. The shared moral vocabulary that let strangers argue about right and wrong because they assumed a common frame — that is going, and its loss is not nothing. The hospitals and universities and charities that grew from Christian conviction, the assumption that mercy is a duty and not a weakness, the strange Western reflex that the powerful owe something to the powerless — these were genuine fruits, and they may not survive the death of the tree that bore them. I do not want to pretend the only thing dying is the rot. Real goods are dying too. The honest position grieves them while refusing to confuse them with the gospel itself. The goods were downstream of the faith. They were never a substitute for it, and they cannot be preserved by a culture that has discarded their source. That is the sober part of this essay, and I will not soften it: some good things are going to be lost, and the way to keep them is not to defend the culture but to be the church that produced them in the first place.

Lesslie Newbigin understood that the danger was never the death of Christendom but the church's failure to distinguish it from the death of faith. He came home to a post-Christian Britain and found a church paralyzed by grief over the wrong corpse — mourning its lost status as though its lost status were its life. He spent his last decades trying to wake it to the difference: the West was now a mission field, and that was not the end of the church but the recovery of its true work, which Christendom had let it forget.

## The seed has to go into the ground

Jesus gave us the only frame that makes sense of a death like this, and he gave it just days before his own.

Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit (John 12:24). He was speaking of himself, of the cross that was days away. But he stated it as a law of the kingdom, not a one-time exception. Life comes through death. The seed that refuses to die stays a single seed — preserved, intact, and utterly barren. The seed that goes into the ground and is buried and broken open is the only one that multiplies. Christendom was a seed that refused to fall, a Christianity preserved so carefully by the culture that it slowly stopped bearing fruit. Its death is terrible to watch. It may also be the only way back to a harvest.

Jesus said the same thing in the form of a paradox we have domesticated past recognition: whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake will find it (Matthew 16:25). This is true of churches and not only of souls. The church that spends its energy trying to save its life — its position, its prestige, its cultural power — will lose the thing it was protecting. The church willing to lose all of that for Christ's sake will find what it was actually for.

This is the test that separates the two things dying in the same grave, and it is a test we can actually run. Take any feature of the church's life that feels threatened right now and ask of it: is this the gospel, or is this Christendom? Is this the seed, or is this the husk that grew around the seed? The reserved seat at the civic table — husk. The conviction that Jesus is Lord whether the civic table agrees or not — seed. The expectation of cultural deference — husk. The call to love the neighbor who does not defer — seed. The political reflex to win — husk. The willingness to lose everything and follow anyway — seed. Run that test honestly across the whole inventory of what we are afraid to lose, and a strange thing happens. Most of what frightens us turns out to be husk, and most of what we will actually keep turns out to be the part no census can touch. The grief is real. It is also, on inspection, mostly grief for the husk, and the husk was never what kept us alive.

Hebrews promises that this sorting is, in the end, God's own work. He has promised to shake the heavens and the earth once more, the writer says, and that phrase, *once more,* signals the removal of what can be shaken, so that what cannot be shaken may remain (Hebrews 12:27). Christendom can be shaken; it is being shaken; it is, by the evidence, coming down. **The shaking is not the enemy of the kingdom. The shaking is how you find out what the kingdom actually was, underneath everything the culture had built on top of it.**

The fear underneath all our grieving is that the shaking will not stop until there is nothing left — that the seed in the ground is simply dead, not buried, and no spring is coming. I cannot prove otherwise from a chart, and I will not pretend to. But the witness of the church's own history is that it has died and risen more times than anyone counting has expected. The faith was supposed to be finished when Rome fell, and it outlived Rome. It was supposed to be finished when the Enlightenment dismissed it, and it outlived the philosophers who wrote its obituary. It has been buried in more soils than I can name, and the burying has a way of preceding a harvest no one predicted, because the God of this faith specializes in the kind of life that only comes after a death. That is not optimism. Optimism is a reading of the trend lines. This is hope, which is a reading of the character of God, and the two are not the same, and only one of them survives a funeral.

When the dust settles, the church will be smaller. It will also be unmistakable, in a way it has not been able to be for a very long time. The people who remain will be there because they chose it against the grain, not because the culture handed it to them at birth. That is not a defeat. That is a seed in the ground.

Grieve what is worth grieving. Bury the rest, and do not dig it back up. Something is supposed to grow here.
