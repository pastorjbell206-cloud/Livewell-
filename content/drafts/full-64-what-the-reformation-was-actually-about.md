The Protestant Reformation is one of the most consequential events in Western history — religious, cultural, political, economic. It is also one of the most misunderstood by the people who claim its inheritance, including me for many years. We tend to remember it as a clean story: a corrupt church, a brave monk, a door, and the recovery of the gospel. The real thing was richer than that, and more double-edged.

What the Reformation actually recovered was real, and worth recovering. At its center was a question about how a guilty person stands before a holy God, and the answer the Reformers pressed — *justification by grace through faith*, not by the purchase of merit — was a genuine return to Paul after centuries of accretion. [cite — Luther, 1517, the Ninety-Five Theses, if dated.] Alongside it came the conviction that Scripture, not the institution's later traditions, holds final authority, and that an ordinary believer could read it in his own language. These were not novelties. They were excavations — the clearing away of debris from something the church already possessed and had half-buried.

That much, I will defend. The recovery of grace is not a sixteenth-century opinion; it is the gospel, and the church needed it back.

But here is the part the triumphal version skips. The Reformation also set loose something it did not fully intend, and we are still living inside it. When it placed the Bible in every hand and made the individual conscience the seat of authority, it could not also supply the thing the old structure had provided: a way to stay together. The principle that each believer, with Scripture, could discern the truth had no built-in brake. And so the movement that began with one division kept dividing — Lutheran from Reformed, and then again, and again, until the present reality of tens of thousands of denominations, each certain it has read the text rightly. [cite — confirm any denomination-count figure rather than asserting it.]

I do not say this to undo the Reformation. I say it because its children have inherited both halves of its legacy and tend to claim only the flattering one. We celebrate the recovery of grace and the open Bible. We rarely reckon with the fragmentation, the individualism, the quiet assumption — so deep in the Protestant water that we no longer taste it — that *me and my Bible* is the basic unit of Christian truth. That assumption would have alarmed the Reformers themselves, who were churchmen, not spiritual freelancers. But ideas outrun their authors, and the idea that the conscience is sovereign did not stop where Luther and Calvin wanted it to stop.

This is the missed thing: the Reformation recovered the gospel of grace and underprovided for the unity of the church. It was strong on *truth against corruption* and weak on *how the body holds together once everyone can read for himself*. The result is a Protestantism magnificent in its access to Scripture and chronically unable to stay in one room — a tradition that produced both an extraordinary flowering of biblical seriousness and an extraordinary talent for splitting.

The point is not to relitigate the sixteenth century or to romanticize what came before it; the medieval church had real corruptions the Reformers were right to name. The point is maturity about our own inheritance. A movement can recover something essential and lose something essential at the same time, and the faithful response is not to pick a half. It is to hold the recovery of grace with gratitude and to take seriously the wound the recovery left — to be Protestants who still ache for the unity our own founding made harder, and who refuse to treat our endless dividing as though it were a virtue rather than a cost.

The Reformation was about grace, and Scripture, and the conscience set free before God. It was also about a fracture that has not healed. Own both. The first is a gift you should not surrender. The second is a debt the church is still paying — and pretending otherwise is how we keep paying it.
