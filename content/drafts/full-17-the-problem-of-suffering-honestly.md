The problem of suffering is not a debating question. It is the condition we live inside.

Cancer kills children. Tsunamis kill villages. The Holocaust happened, and the Christians of Germany mostly did not stop it, and that fact alone should disqualify the easy apologetic answers most of us have been taught to give. I want to give a different answer. It will not satisfy you intellectually. It is, I think, the only honest answer the Christian tradition has, and I am going to take three or four times as many words as I would normally use to make sure I am giving it carefully.

I want to start by being honest about what the question is. The classical formulation, going back at least to Epicurus, runs like this. If God is all-good, he would want to prevent evil. If God is all-powerful, he would be able to prevent evil. Evil exists. Therefore, either God is not all-good or not all-powerful or both, or God does not exist. The argument is clean. The argument has, over the centuries, persuaded a great many honest thinkers. The argument is the single most persistent reason serious skeptics give for not being Christian, and I take it seriously. I took it seriously at twenty. I take it seriously now.

The standard Christian responses are well known. The free-will defense, articulated most rigorously in our time by Alvin Plantinga: God's willingness to give created beings genuine freedom necessitates the possibility of evil, because freedom that cannot choose evil is not freedom. The soul-making theodicy, associated with John Hick: a world in which suffering is possible is a world in which moral and spiritual growth is possible, and the kinds of growth God wants in his creatures require a world like ours. The greater-good frame, in various versions: the suffering we observe contributes to outcomes that are, in the eyes of an all-knowing God, more valuable than the alternatives. Each of these has something to say. Each, in my honest reading, has limits when it meets actual suffering — the suffering of a child with cancer, the suffering of a victim of genocide, the suffering of a single person you know whose pain has no apparent point.

I want to acknowledge the limits before I go further. The pop Christian apologetic that confidently solves the problem of suffering by deploying the standard arguments is not doing justice to the question. The question is harder than the standard arguments admit. The skeptic who reads the standard arguments and remains unpersuaded is not being unreasonable. The skeptic is responding accurately to the fact that the arguments are intellectually incomplete in the face of actual suffering. I want to be honest about this. I want to say, as a pastor and a writer and a former skeptic, that I have spent twenty years thinking about this question, and the standard arguments are not what finally moved me. Something else did. The something else is what I want to describe.

## What the Bible actually does

I want to start with what the Bible actually does with the problem of suffering, because the answer is interesting and is not, on the whole, what the pop apologetics has trained us to expect.

The Bible does not, on the whole, answer the problem of suffering with reasons. The Bible answers the problem with a sufferer.

The book of Job is the longest and most rigorous biblical treatment of the question, and the answer Job gets is striking. Job is afflicted unjustly. His friends spend chapters offering the standard theodicies — sin, soul-making, the inscrutable wisdom of God — and Job rejects them all, insisting that his suffering is unjust and demanding an audience with God. God eventually grants the audience. The audience is in chapters 38 through 41. God does not give Job reasons. God does not explain. God does not tell Job why the suffering was permitted. God speaks to Job out of a whirlwind and asks Job a series of questions about the structure of the cosmos — *where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth, when the morning stars sang together?* Job's response is not satisfaction with an argument. Job's response is surrender to a presence. *I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you.*

The book ends with Job's vindication and the restoration of his fortunes, but the structural argument of the book is that the answer to suffering is not a theodicy. The answer is the encounter with God himself, and the encounter is what makes the suffering bearable, even when the suffering has not been explained. The Lord did not give Job reasons. The Lord gave Job himself. Job, in the end, says that was enough.

Lamentations does something similar in a different register. The book is a series of poems about the destruction of Jerusalem and the temple in 586 BC. The poems are unflinching. They do not soften the suffering. They name God as the agent of the suffering. They do not, in the end, explain the suffering or offer a theodicy. What they do is keep speaking to God — even accusatorially, even angrily, but to God, not about God. The whole structure of Lamentations is the structure of a person addressing God from inside the suffering, not a person trying to justify God from outside the suffering. The form is the answer. The form says: in suffering, the practice is not to explain it but to address God from within it, and the addressing is itself the participation in something that makes the suffering bearable.

The Psalms — the largest single book of the Old Testament — are saturated with the same pattern. The lament psalms make up roughly a third of the Psalter. Psalm 88 is the most extreme: a complete cry of suffering with no resolution at the end. The psalm just ends, *you have caused my beloved and my friend to shun me; my companions have become darkness.* The Bible includes this psalm. The Bible canonizes the unresolved lament. The Bible does not require the sufferer to wrap up his suffering with a theological bow. The Bible just keeps speaking to God, even when no answer comes.

The pattern climaxes in the New Testament with the cross. Christ on the cross prays the opening of Psalm 22 — *my God, my God, why have you forsaken me?* He does not, in that moment, deliver a theodicy. He prays the lament. The lament becomes the moment of redemption. The God who is on the cross is the God who has entered the suffering personally. The God who is on the cross does not stand outside the suffering explaining it. The God who is on the cross suffers it, and by suffering it, redeems it, and by redeeming it, makes the suffering finally bearable to those who suffer.

This is what the Bible does. The Bible does not, on the whole, answer the problem of suffering by giving reasons. The Bible answers the problem by giving us a sufferer — by showing us a God who has entered the suffering himself, who has died on the cross, who has been raised from the dead, who is present in the Spirit with those who suffer now, and who has promised the eventual consummation in which the suffering will be put right.

## Why this is different

I want to be careful here, because the move I have just made is often heard as a dodge. The skeptic says: *but the problem is logical. The problem is that there is a contradiction between God's goodness and the existence of evil. You are not addressing the contradiction. You are pivoting to pastoral comfort.*

I want to respond honestly. The skeptic is partly right. I am not, in this essay, resolving the logical problem of evil in a way that would satisfy a strict philosopher. The free-will defense and the various theodicies attempt to do that, and I think they have something to say, but I do not think any of them finally compels intellectual surrender.

What I am doing is something different. I am claiming that the kind of answer the question actually needs is not the kind of answer the standard theodicies attempt to give. The standard theodicies attempt to give reasons. The reasons, even when they work logically, do not actually help the sufferer. The sufferer with cancer is not consoled by the free-will defense. The grandmother whose grandchildren died in the burning is not consoled by the soul-making theodicy. The Holocaust survivor is not consoled by the greater-good frame. The reasons are not what the sufferer needs. The sufferer needs a presence — someone who has been in the suffering, who is in it now, and who will see it through to the end.

The Christian claim is that we have that presence. The Christian claim is that God himself, in Christ, has entered the suffering of his creatures, has died on a Roman cross, has been raised on the third day, and is present with the sufferer now through the Spirit. The Christian claim is not that this resolves the logical problem of evil. The Christian claim is that this answers the deeper question — the question the sufferer is actually asking, which is not *why does this make sense?* but *am I alone in this?* The answer the Christian tradition gives is *no, you are not alone, because God himself is in it with you.*

This is, I think, the only answer that finally works. It is not an intellectual answer. It is a presence. The presence is what scripture offers. The presence is what the saints have testified to for two thousand years. The presence is what I have personally encountered in the lives of suffering people I have pastored, and what I have personally encountered in seasons of suffering in my own life. The presence is not a substitute for the intellectual question. The presence is what the intellectual question has been driving at all along.

## What the alternative offers

I want to be honest about what the alternative — the strong secular response to the problem of suffering — offers, because it is worth examining.

The strong secular response is that there is no problem of suffering, because there is no expectation that the universe should be otherwise. The universe is indifferent. The universe is not the kind of thing that could have intentions. The suffering is the result of physical and biological processes operating according to their natures. There is nothing to explain. There is only the suffering itself, and the question of how human beings should respond to it.

This is a coherent response. It is not, I think, finally adequate to the moral weight of the suffering. The strong secular response leaves us with the suffering and with no resources for redeeming it. The strong secular response, if you take it seriously, has to grant that the Holocaust was, in some final sense, unredeemable — that nothing can be done to give meaning to the deaths of those who died in the camps. The strong secular response, if you take it seriously, has to grant that the death of a child from cancer is, in some final sense, only a tragedy with no further meaning.

This may be true. The strong secular response is not refuted by the fact that we find it uncomfortable. But the strong secular response is, in my reading, less adequate to the moral weight of the suffering than the Christian response is. The Christian response says: the suffering is real, and is unjustly suffered, and is being suffered with by God, and will be put right at the consummation. The strong secular response says: the suffering is real, and there will be no putting right, and the moral weight has nowhere finally to go. The two responses are different. The Christian one is, I think, the one that takes the moral weight of the suffering more seriously, because it refuses to let the suffering be the final word.

## The verdict

The problem of suffering is not a problem to be solved. The problem of suffering is the condition in which we live, and the question is what kind of God, if any, is present in it with us.

The God I have come to believe in is the God who is present in it with us. He does not, on the whole, give us reasons. He gives us himself. The giving was the cross. The presence is the Spirit. The promise is the consummation. None of these is intellectually conclusive against the strong secular alternative. All of these are, in my experience and in the testimony of the church across two thousand years, more adequate to the actual condition of the actual sufferer than the alternatives.

I came to faith from atheism partly because of this. The intellectual arguments were necessary but not sufficient. The sufficient thing was the recognition that the Christian story takes the suffering more seriously than the secular story does — and that the God of the Christian story is the only God I have heard of who has been willing to enter the suffering himself, rather than standing outside it explaining it. That was finally what moved me. That is what continues to move me. That is what the saints across the centuries have testified to. That is, I believe, what is actually on offer.
