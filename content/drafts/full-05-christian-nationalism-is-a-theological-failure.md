I want to start by saying what most pastors I know will not yet say from a pulpit. Christian nationalism is a heresy. It is not bad politics that happens to have Christian language attached to it. It is bad theology that has happened to take political form, and treating it as politics first is precisely what has let it survive every critique the church has tried to lodge against it. The critique has been political. The defense has been theological. The asymmetry has been fatal.

I have watched, over twenty years of ministry, the slow movement of Christian nationalism from the fringe of American evangelical life to its center. I have watched it become respectable in churches that would have rejected it a generation ago. I have watched pastors I respect adopt its framings while telling themselves they have not. I have watched it absorb the imaginations of the younger men in the churches I serve, and I have failed to give them an alternative as fast as the manosphere and the nationalist intellectuals have given them their version. This essay is part of the alternative. It is overdue.

## What it actually is

I want to be honest about the definition before I am honest about the critique. The strongest version of Christian nationalism, as articulated by its most serious proponents — Stephen Wolfe's *The Case for Christian Nationalism* (2022) is the document of record now — is the position that the political order of a nation should be explicitly Christian, that the political community should be defined by a shared Christian identity, and that the state should privilege the Christian church over other religious bodies and over secular institutions.

The popular version, the one most of you have encountered in your own churches, is thinner. It is the assumption that America is or was a Christian nation in some constitutive sense, that this constitutive Christianity gives it a unique relationship to God's purposes in history, that protecting this Christian identity is a primary duty of American Christians, and that the political activity of Christians should aim at the recovery and defense of this identity.

Both versions, the academic and the popular, share a common theological move. They identify a nation-state — or in the popular version, a quasi-mythical version of one — with the redemptive work of God. They make the political community the carrier of the gospel. They make the church a function of the nation rather than a community drawn out of every nation.

That move is the theological failure. The rest of the critique follows from it.

## The pattern is old

I am not the first pastor to notice this. The church has met this move before and named it, and the church has, on the whole, been right.

It met it in the Roman imperial cult, which conscripted the worship of the divine Caesar as the binding of the empire, and which the early Christians refused at the cost of their lives. Polycarp was killed in 155 because he would not say *kyrios kaisar* — Caesar is lord. He would only say *kyrios christos*. The Roman authorities did not understand the distinction; the early Christians understood it perfectly. There was only one Lord, and the Lord was not the empire.

The church met the same move in the establishment of Christianity under Constantine, and the gradual shift of imperial language into ecclesial use. Augustine wrote *City of God* in part to refuse the conflation. He had watched what political identification did to the gospel, and he wanted the church to remember that its citizenship was elsewhere.

The church met the same move in the German Christians of the 1930s — the *Deutsche Christen* movement, which sought to align Lutheran identity with Nazi national identity, which removed the Old Testament from German Bibles because the Old Testament was Jewish, which baptized Hitler's racial ideology with Christian vocabulary. The Confessing Church, which arose in opposition under Karl Barth and Dietrich Bonhoeffer and the Barmen Declaration of 1934, named the *Deutsche Christen* what they were — a heresy. The Confessing Church paid for the naming. Bonhoeffer paid for it with his life.

The church met the same move in Slovakia under Jozef Tiso, a Catholic priest who became a fascist head of state and led his country into collaboration with the Holocaust. It met it in the *Reichskirche* under Wilhelm II. It is meeting it now in Russian Orthodoxy under Kirill, whose alignment of the Russian Orthodox Church with Putin's regime has produced, among other things, a theological defense of the invasion of Ukraine.

The pattern is not new. The pattern has a name. The pattern has been condemned by the church every time the church has been awake enough to recognize it. The pattern is what we are seeing now in American evangelical life, and the church is, on the whole, not awake.

## What it violates

I want to name the doctrines Christian nationalism violates, because the critique has to be theological if it is going to be honest.

It violates the catholicity of the church. The church is, by definition and confession, drawn from every nation, every tongue, every tribe — Revelation 7:9. The church identified with a particular nation is no longer the catholic church. It has become a national church, and the national church is, in scriptural terms, no longer the church at all in its full sense. It is a tribal idol with Christian vocabulary.

It violates the eschatological frame of the New Testament. The Kingdom of God is *not yet*. It is being inaugurated by Christ's resurrection, it is operating in the world through the Spirit, and it will be consummated at Christ's return. No nation-state in the meantime is the Kingdom. No nation-state can be. To call any nation-state Christian in the constitutive sense is to collapse the eschatology and pretend the Kingdom has come to a particular polity. It has not. It will not. That collapse is what we mean, theologically, by *realized eschatology* misapplied — and it is precisely the move Lutheran political theology learned to refuse after 1945, at enormous cost.

It violates the doctrine of the cross. The Christian community is supposed to live cruciform, by suffering rather than by inflicting suffering, by the surrender of power rather than its accumulation. Paul makes this explicit in 1 Corinthians 1 and 2. The cross is foolishness to the Greeks and a scandal to the Jews precisely because the Lord of glory comes to defeat the principalities and powers by being killed by them. Christian nationalism reverses this. It accepts political power as a means to Christian ends, and in accepting the means, accepts the principalities and powers as legitimate carriers of the Kingdom. The cross is the judgment on this acceptance. The cross is precisely the refusal of this kind of victory.

It violates the missiology of the church. The church is *sent*. It is sent to every nation. It is sent as the body of Christ in the world, to bear witness to Christ in the world, to suffer with the world as Christ suffered with the world. The church identified with one nation has become its envoy rather than its missionary. The position is incoherent. You cannot be sent to a nation you are. You can only be sent to a nation you are not.

These are four doctrinal violations. Each is sufficient to disqualify the position. Together, they are devastating. Christian nationalism is not a position serious Christian theology can hold without ceasing to be serious Christian theology.

## What the political critique missed

The reason American evangelicalism has not yet, on the whole, recognized this as the heresy it is, is that we have tried to critique it politically. We have said: it is dangerous, it has bad outcomes, it is illiberal, it will end badly. All of which is true. None of which has been theologically sufficient.

The political critique can be answered politically. *Yes, it is illiberal — but liberalism failed.* *Yes, it is dangerous — but the status quo is also dangerous.* *Yes, it may end badly — but the alternative is ending worse.* The political critique enters the political debate on the political debate's terms, and the political debate is endless, and the Christian nationalist position has the advantage of clear conviction in a debate where most of its opponents have only managed to manage.

The theological critique is different. The theological critique does not say *Christian nationalism will produce bad outcomes.* It says *Christian nationalism is not Christian.* That is a different move. It does not engage the political debate on the political debate's terms. It refuses to enter the political debate at all, and forces the question back into the church, where it belongs.

That refusal is what we have not done. Most of the pastors I know who oppose Christian nationalism have opposed it politically. We have not yet opposed it theologically, from our pulpits, with the seriousness the Barmen Declaration showed in 1934. We have made the same mistake the German liberal Protestants made in the early thirties — we have argued politically against a movement that is, at its core, theological, and the argument has not landed because the argument has been in the wrong key.

## What it would cost to say this honestly

I have not said this from my own pulpit yet, at least not with the directness I am saying it now. I want to be honest about that. I have alluded. I have hinted. I have preached against the symptoms while leaving the cause unnamed. The reasons have been pastoral, and the pastoral reasons have been real, and the pastoral reasons have also been, in retrospect, partly cowardice.

There are men in my church who believe versions of this. They are good men. They love their families. They give to the work. They show up. They serve in the children's ministry. They would be wounded by being told that a position they hold is a heresy, and the wound would be real, and the wound would damage relationships I have spent twenty years building. The pastoral instinct is to spare the wound by softening the language. The pastoral instinct, in this case, is wrong.

Bonhoeffer wrote in *Discipleship* that cheap grace is the deadly enemy of the church. He meant something specific by it. He meant grace that does not require the convert to leave behind the gods he had before he came. The conversion from American civic religion to actual Christianity requires leaving behind the civic religion. It requires the recognition that the prior faith was an idol. That recognition is wounding. The wound is the conversion.

I have a duty to inflict the wound, gently and clearly, from the pulpit. I have not been doing it. I am repenting of it here, in writing, before I do it from the pulpit. I will do it from the pulpit too. The doing will cost.

## The verdict

Christian nationalism is a theological failure. The political form is the surface; the theological collapse is the cause. The church that recognizes this can refuse it. The church that does not has, in some important sense, already left the catholic church and joined a national one, regardless of what the sign on the building says.

The Confessing Church in 1934 was small. It was always going to be small. It was right anyway. The faithful church in America will, on this question, also be small. It will be right anyway. The cost of being right will be friendships, congregations, livelihoods. The cost of being wrong will be larger and slower to recognize and more terrible to repent of when the repenting comes due.

I am writing this for the pastor who has not yet preached against this from his own pulpit. I have been him. I am repenting of being him. The preaching is the work. The preaching costs. Do it anyway. The faithful church in America in 2026 is the church that did the costly thing while there was still time to do it. Most of us still have time. Some of us no longer do. The window is closing. Preach.

The God who blessed Polycarp's refusal will bless ours. He blessed the Barmen signers. He blessed the Confessing Church. He has been blessing the saints who refused this move for two thousand years. He will bless us too. Not in the way American civic Christianity has trained us to expect. In the way the cross trains us to expect — through suffering, through smallness, through being known by God when we have been forgotten by the empire we refused.

That is enough. That has always been enough. The faithful church is the church that has remembered this. The Christian nationalist church has forgotten it. The forgetting is the heresy. The remembering is the way back.
