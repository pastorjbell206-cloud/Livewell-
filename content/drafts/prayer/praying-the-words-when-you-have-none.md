There is a kind of suffering that does not produce eloquence. It produces silence, or worse, it produces a single ugly syllable repeated — *why, why, why* — and then nothing. People imagine that great grief moves a person to pray. More often it does the opposite. It strikes you dumb. You sit down to talk to God in the worst week of your life and discover that the machinery has seized, that the words you used to reach for are gone, and that the silence where they used to be feels like one more proof that God has left the building.

I want to tell you what the church has done about this for three thousand years, because it is the best-kept secret in plain sight. The answer is not to summon better words. The answer is that God anticipated this exact moment and gave you a book of words to borrow.

**When grief or rage burns your own prayers to ash, the Psalms are the words God wrote for you to pray back to him — and praying borrowed words in a borrowed voice is not a lesser prayer. It is often the only honest one left.**

## The strange fact at the center of the Bible

Sit with something odd. In the middle of the Scriptures — books that are mostly God speaking to us — there is one long book that is us speaking to God. The Psalms are prayers. A hundred and fifty of them, collected over centuries, and they were placed in the Bible, which means God saw fit to inspire and preserve not only his words to us but our words back to him. He gave us, inside his own book, a record of how to talk to him.

Athanasius, the fourth-century bishop who fought half the empire to defend the deity of Christ, noticed what makes the Psalms different from everything around them. He wrote, in a famous letter to a man named Marcellinus, that most of Scripture speaks *to* us, but the Psalms speak *for* us — that whoever takes up this book finds in it the motions of his own soul set to words, so that he reads them as if they were his own and prays them as if he had written them. The grief is already there. The rage is already there. The confusion, the longing, the praise that will not come — all of it is already in the book, waiting, so that when your own well runs dry you do not have to dig a new one. You lower a bucket into one that has held water for three thousand years.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer pressed this further than anyone, and he pressed it from prison. In his small 1940 book *Psalms: The Prayer Book of the Bible* — written in the shadow of the Nazi regime that would eventually hang him — he made a claim that overturns how most of us think about prayer. We assume prayer means saying to God what is in our hearts. Bonhoeffer says no. Often what is in our hearts is confused, poisoned, or simply empty, and if prayer depended on the quality of our hearts we would be lost. The Psalms teach us to pray not out of our own poverty but out of the richness of the word God has given. We learn to pray, he says, by praying back to God the words he has spoken. The strange grace of it is that the words come first and teach the heart what it should have felt.

## The permission you were never given

Here is where the Psalms do something the modern church almost never does. They let you be furious with God to his face.

We have built a Christian culture that polices the prayers of the suffering. *Don't say that.* *You don't really mean it.* *Have more faith.* And so the grieving learn to lie in church, to perform a serenity they do not have, to leave their rage at the door because they have been taught it is unspiritual. Then they open the Psalms and find this, prayed by David and sung by Israel and quoted by Jesus from the cross: *My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?* (Psalm 22:1). That is not a polite prayer. That is an accusation, hurled upward, and God put it in the songbook of his people and then, on a Friday afternoon, the Son of God made it his own dying words. The most God-forsaken sentence in human language turns out to be Scripture, turns out to be prayer, turns out to be on the lips of God himself.

Read further into the dark and it gets darker. Psalm 88 is the one nobody preaches, because it does not resolve. It begins in trouble and it ends — read it — with the line *darkness is my closest friend.* No turn. No sunrise in the last verse. No tidy lesson. Just a man telling God that he is drowning and that God has not shown up, and then the psalm simply stops, in the dark, where the man actually is. And God preserved it. He let it into the canon without a happy ending, which means he is not afraid of your prayer that has no happy ending either. C. S. Lewis, in his 1958 *Reflections on the Psalms*, admitted he was at first scandalized by the violence and bitterness in these prayers — the cursing, the raw vindictiveness — and only slowly came to see that their refusal to be polite was itself a kind of honesty the religious are forever tempted to lose. The Psalms do not flinch from what is in a person. They drag it into the open, into the presence of God, which is the only place it can be healed.

So if you have been carrying rage at God and hiding it because you thought faith required the hiding — the book at the center of your Bible was written to give you permission. The opposite of faith is not anger. The opposite of faith is walking away. The angry psalmist is still talking to God. That is the whole point. He has not left. He is shouting, but he is shouting *at God*, which means he still believes there is Someone there to shout at.

## Whose voice you are borrowing

There is a deeper reason the borrowed words fit your mouth, and Bonhoeffer is the one who pressed it hardest, against the instinct of his own age.

He asked the obvious question. How can a man pray Psalm 22 — *my God, why have you forsaken me* — or the psalms of innocence, or the psalms that cry for a justice the pray-er has not earned? How can a sinner honestly pray words of righteousness, or a comfortable man honestly pray the agony of the abandoned? Bonhoeffer's answer reorders everything. The Psalms, he said, are first of all the prayers of Jesus Christ. They are the words of the one true man who could pray them all without exception — the righteous one who was truly forsaken, who truly thirsted for justice, who truly walked through the valley. And when we pray them, we do not pray them alone or in our own name. We pray them in Christ, who prayed them first, so that even the words too high or too low for our actual condition become honest on our lips because they were honest on his. Jesus did not merely teach prayer from a distance. He prayed the prayer book of Israel as one of us, and on the cross he reached for Psalm 22 because it was already his. When you cannot pray, you climb into a prayer he has already prayed, and your weak voice joins a voice that never falters.

This is why the church bound the Psalms to the daily rhythm of its life and never let go. The monasteries prayed through all hundred and fifty in the course of each week, some of them all hundred and fifty in a single day, not because the monks felt every line but because the praying was never only about what they felt. It was the body of Christ lending its voice to the prayer of its head, the whole choir carrying every part so that no single voice had to carry the impossible alone. Bonhoeffer's own underground seminary at Finkenwalde prayed the Psalms together daily in the years before the Gestapo shut it down, and he insisted that a Christian who abandoned the Psalter had cut himself off from the prayer of the whole church across all time. He was not romanticizing it. He was a man learning to pray words of deliverance from inside a Germany he could see was going to kill him, and the Psalms were how he did it.

## How the words carry you

But the Psalms do more than vent. They move, and praying them moves you, and this is the deeper gift.

Pray Psalm 130 and feel the architecture. *Out of the depths I cry to you, O LORD.* It begins at the bottom, in the depths, with no pretense of being anywhere else. But it does not stay. It moves — through a plea for mercy, through a confession that no one could stand if God kept a record of sins, into a strange, dogged waiting: *I wait for the LORD, my soul waits, and in his word I hope; my soul waits for the Lord more than watchmen wait for the morning.* The psalm carries the pray-er from the depths to the dawn-watch, not by denying the depths but by walking through them with words to hold onto. You may begin praying it without an ounce of hope. By the end your mouth has confessed hope your heart could not yet feel — and Bonhoeffer's insight lands exactly here. The word teaches the heart. You pray your way toward a faith you did not have when you opened your mouth.

This is why praying borrowed words is not dishonest when your own heart lags behind. Some Sundays you will sing a psalm of praise from inside a depression and feel like a hypocrite, and you are not one. You are doing what the body of Christ has always done — lending your voice to the prayer of the whole people, trusting that what you cannot feel today, someone in the family feels for you, and that the words are true even on the day you cannot rise to them. When you pray Psalm 88 in your own darkness you are also praying it on behalf of everyone in that darkness who has no words, and someone praying Psalm 23 in green pastures is, at the same hour, praying it for you. The Psalter is a choir, and you do not have to carry the whole song.

## The Spirit and the borrowed word

And here the New Testament reaches back and meets the Psalms in the dark.

Paul says we do not know what to pray for as we ought, and that the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words (Romans 8:26). Set that beside the Psalms and see what God has done. On one side, the Spirit prays *underneath* your wordlessness, carrying upward the groan you cannot form. On the other, the Psalms hand you words *from above*, the very prayers God authored, ready for your dry mouth. From below the Spirit groans; from above the word is given; and in between sits you, with nothing of your own — and you are not, it turns out, alone in the silence. You are surrounded. The God who knows you have no words supplies them from both directions at once.

There is an old practice worth recovering here, and Athanasius all but laid it out in that letter to Marcellinus. He went through the Psalter telling his friend which psalm answered which condition of the soul — this one for when you are persecuted, this one when you have sinned and need to repent, this one when you would give thanks, this one when fear has hold of you. He treated the book as a kind of dispensary, a shelf of remedies, each labeled for an ailment of the heart. You can read it the same way. When the bottom drops out, Psalm 130 — *out of the depths.* When you feel abandoned by God, Psalm 22 — *why have you forsaken me.* When there is no light at all and you need a prayer that will not lie about it, Psalm 88. You do not have to manufacture the right prayer for your particular ruin. The right prayer has already been written, indexed, and preserved, and it is waiting on the shelf for the hand that has nothing of its own to bring.

This is the thing I most want the grieving and the raging to hear. Your inability to pray is not the end of your prayer life. It may be the beginning of a deeper one — the day you stop generating prayer from your own resources and start receiving it. The strong, articulate, self-sufficient prayer of your good years was always a little too pleased with itself. The broken prayer, the borrowed prayer, the prayer that is only Psalm 130 read aloud through tears with no feeling behind it — that prayer has stopped trusting your eloquence and started trusting God's word. That is not a downgrade. That is growing up.

## What to do tonight

So here is the counsel, plain. When you cannot pray, do not wait to feel like praying. Open the Psalms and read one out loud — slowly, in the first person, as your own — and do not stop because you do not mean it yet. Pray Psalm 22 when you feel forsaken. Pray Psalm 88 when there is no light and you need a prayer that will not lie to you about it. Pray Psalm 130 when you are at the bottom and need words that will climb when you cannot. Let the borrowed words be your words. Let the dead king and the imprisoned pastor and the dying Christ have taught you the syllables your grief could not invent.

The day will come — it has come for me, it has come for everyone who prays these prayers long enough — when you reach for the Psalms with nothing in your hands and find that the words were waiting for you, written down centuries before your particular sorrow, as though Someone had known it would come and had left the words by the door.

He did know. He left the words. Pick them up.
