At some point, if you pray for years and not weeks, the warmth goes out of it. The mornings that once felt like sitting with a friend become mornings of sitting in an empty room talking to the furniture. You read the same Scripture that used to move you and feel nothing. You sing the songs and your chest stays flat. And the cruelest part is that nothing has obviously gone wrong — you have not committed some scandal, lost your beliefs, walked away from the church. You are doing everything you did before. It just stopped working, and no one prepared you for this, and the silence makes you wonder whether the whole thing was ever real or only ever a feeling that has now, like all feelings, faded.

The modern church has almost nothing to say to you here, because the modern church sold you prayer as an experience, and an experience that stops being experienced looks, by that logic, like a dead relationship. But the great masters of prayer — the people who prayed so deeply that the church remembers their names four hundred years later — would look at your dry season and tell you something that sounds insane the first time you hear it. They would tell you that you have not regressed. You may have advanced. The dryness is not the failure of prayer. It is, often, the place prayer was always heading.

**Feeling was never the engine of prayer, and when the feeling goes, what remains is the thing prayer was building toward all along — a faithfulness to God that no longer depends on what God is currently doing for your nervous system.**

## The mistake underneath the panic

To see why dryness is not catastrophe, you have to see the mistake that makes it feel like catastrophe.

We were trained to read the presence of feeling as the presence of God and the absence of feeling as the absence of God. It is an understandable mistake. Early in prayer, God often does give a beginner real sweetness — a warmth, a sense of nearness — and we naturally conclude that the warmth *is* the relationship. So when the warmth withdraws we conclude the relationship has withdrawn. But this is exactly backward, and Teresa of Ávila spent a whole book explaining why. In *The Interior Castle*, written in 1577, she describes the soul's life with God as a series of rooms moving inward toward the center where God dwells, and she warns repeatedly that the consolations — the felt sweetness — are gifts God gives in the outer rooms to draw beginners in. They are the candy a parent uses to coax a toddler across the room. And precisely because they are for beginners, God withdraws them as the soul matures, not to abandon it but to teach it to walk toward him for his own sake rather than for the candy. The dryness, in Teresa's map, is not the soul falling out of the castle. It is the soul being moved into a deeper room, where the lights are dimmer and the love is realer.

John of the Cross, her younger contemporary and fellow reformer, gave the experience the name we still use — the dark night. And he made a distinction that has rescued more dry Christians than any other in the tradition. The dryness that comes from sin or laziness, he said, leaves you indifferent to God; you do not pray and you do not care. But the dryness that comes from God's deeper work leaves you anxious *because* you cannot pray, grieved at the distance, longing for a God you can no longer feel. The very pain of the dryness is the proof of its goodness. If you did not love God you would not miss him. The ache is the love, still there, now stripped of its rewards so that it can become love and not merely pleasure. If you are reading this in distress over your dry prayers, John of the Cross would tell you the distress itself is the evidence that God has not left. The indifferent do not suffer over prayer. You are suffering. That is a sign of life, not death.

## What actually carries you

So if feeling cannot carry prayer through the dry season, what does. The answer is the most unfashionable word in modern spirituality. Discipline.

Jesus assumed this. The parable I keep coming back to — the widow and the unjust judge — exists because Jesus said we *should always pray and not lose heart* (Luke 18:1). Always. Not when moved. Not when warm. Always, including the long stretches when prayer feels like talking to a wall, which is the only condition under which the command not to lose heart makes any sense. You do not need to be told to persist in something that is rewarding you. You need to be told to persist in something that is not, and Jesus told us, which means he knew the not-rewarding seasons were coming and built the instruction for them.

This is why the church has always anchored prayer to fixed times and fixed words rather than to mood. The monastery rose at fixed hours to pray whether the monks felt like it or not, and the genius of that was never legalism — it was the recognition that a prayer life governed by feeling will collapse the moment feeling does, and feeling always does. James K. A. Smith has spent his career arguing that we are shaped not chiefly by our thoughts or our feelings but by our habits, by what we do over and over until it does something to us. Apply that to prayer and the dry season transforms. The prayer you pray when you feel nothing is not the inferior prayer. It may be the more formative one, because it is the prayer that is purely an act of the will toward God, untainted by the question of what you are getting out of it. The warm prayer might have been partly about the warmth. The dry prayer can only be about God. There is nothing else in it for you.

Brother Lawrence understood this better than anyone, and he understood it from a monastery kitchen. A seventeenth-century lay brother who washed dishes and mended sandals, he left almost nothing in writing, but the conversations and letters his friends gathered after his death became *The Practice of the Presence of God*, one of the most beloved small books in the church. His whole method, if you can call it that, was to do everything — the cooking, the cleaning, the dull and endless tasks — in conscious company with God, turning his mind back to God's presence a hundred times a day without waiting for any feeling to authorize it. And he was honest about the dryness. He said there were long stretches when he felt nothing at all, and that he had resolved it did not matter, that he would go on practicing the presence of God whether he felt it or not, for the love of God and not for the consolations. The result, he claimed, was a settled and unshakable nearness to God that the feelings of his early years had never given him. He had stopped praying for the experience and started praying for the Person, and the Person, it turned out, had been there in the dry kitchen the whole time.

## What the dryness is doing to you

But I do not want to leave this at endurance, as though the only counsel were to grit your teeth and wait it out. Something is being done to you in the dry season, and it is worth naming so you can cooperate with it.

The dryness weans you off yourself. As long as prayer feels good, there is a subtle corruption you cannot see — you are coming to God for the way he makes you feel, which means the relationship is, underneath, still about you. The withdrawal of feeling exposes that and, if you stay, slowly cures it. You learn to love God when there is nothing in it for you, which is the only kind of love that is actually love and not disguised self-interest. This is what the marriage analogy gets right. The early infatuation, all feeling, is real but immature, and every marriage that lasts passes through the death of infatuation into something the feelings could never have built — a covenant love that holds when the warmth is gone, that chooses the person on the gray Tuesday, that is finally about them and not about how they make you feel. Prayer follows the same arc. The dry years are the covenant years. They are where love grows up.

There is a particular trap the dry season sets, and the masters all warn against it because they all fell into it. The trap is to go hunting for the lost feeling — to change churches, chase the next conference, the next worship style, the next book, the next experience that might restore the warmth. Teresa watched souls do this and called it a kind of spiritual greed, a refusal to be weaned, an insistence on the consolations of God over the God of the consolations. The hunt feels like devotion. It is actually its opposite. It is the soul trying to get back the candy instead of growing up into the love, and the more frantically you chase the feeling the further you drive yourself from the quiet, unglamorous fidelity that is the only thing that will carry you through. John of the Cross was blunt about it: in the dark night, the worst thing you can do is strive to recover the sweetness, because the dark is doing a work in you that the sweetness would only interrupt. The counsel of the whole tradition is the same, and it is maddening to a culture that cannot sit still. Do not chase the feeling. Stay where you are. Keep praying into the dark, and let the dark finish what it came to do.

Pray Psalm 42 in the dry season, because it was written from inside one. *As the deer pants for streams of water, so my soul pants for you, O God.* The image is not satisfaction. It is thirst — a thirst that has not yet been quenched, an ache for a God the psalmist cannot presently reach. And he does the thing the dry season requires: he talks to himself, he interrogates his own flat soul. *Why are you cast down, O my soul? Why so disturbed within me? Put your hope in God.* He commands himself toward a hope he does not feel, on the strength of a God he cannot presently sense. That is dry prayer in its purest form — the soul preaching to itself, choosing God against the testimony of its own deadness, refusing to let the absence of feeling become the absence of faith.

And the verse to hold above all others in the dark is from Lamentations, written by a man watching his entire world burn, who somehow managed in the middle of the rubble to say this: *The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases, his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning; great is your faithfulness* (Lamentations 3:22–23). Notice he does not say he feels the steadfast love. He is sitting in the ashes of a destroyed city; he feels almost nothing but grief. He says it anyway, as a fact about God that does not depend on his experience of it. The mercies are new every morning whether the morning feels merciful or not. That is the ground you stand on in the dry season — not your feeling of God's faithfulness, which has gone, but God's faithfulness itself, which has not, and never will, and was never yours to feel into existence in the first place.

## The chair you keep sitting in

So here is what I would tell you to do, and it is almost embarrassingly plain after all of that. Keep the time. Sit in the chair. Say the words, even the borrowed ones, even when they are ash in your mouth. Do not measure the prayer by what you felt, because you have been measuring with a broken ruler your whole life and it is the ruler, not the prayer, that is failing.

You will be tempted to quit, and the temptation will dress itself up as honesty — *I'm just not feeling it, I'd be a hypocrite to keep going through the motions.* But going through the motions is not hypocrisy when the motions are an act of fidelity to a God you have chosen to trust past your feelings. Hypocrisy is pretending to feel what you do not. Faithfulness is coming to the chair and telling God the truth, which is that you feel nothing and have come anyway. That prayer — *I feel nothing and I came anyway* — may be the most pleasing prayer you will pray in your life, because there is no candy in it. It is pure trust, offered to God for no reward but God.

The dry season ends. The masters all say it does, in God's time and not yours, and the warmth returns, usually deeper and steadier than before. But that is not the point, and if you make it the point you have learned nothing and will only be praying for the warmth again. The point is the God who was there the whole time, in the empty room that was never empty, waiting in a dimmer and deeper room of the castle for you to come and love him for himself.

Keep coming. He is not measuring what you feel. He is watching who keeps returning to the door — and you, dry and faithful and feeling nothing, are still there. That is the prayer. That was always the prayer.
