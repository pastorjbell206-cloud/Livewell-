There is a particular silence that falls in a hospital room when someone finally says, *I think we should pray*, and nobody knows who goes first. I have stood in that silence more times than I can count. A family that would speak fluently about anything else — the surgery, the insurance, the drive home — goes mute the moment the conversation turns vertical. They want to pray. They have wanted to pray for years. And when the moment arrives they discover they have no idea how the words are supposed to leave the body.

If that is you, I want to tell you something before anything else. You are not behind. You are not failing a test that other Christians passed quietly in some room you missed. The disciples lived with Jesus, watched him heal, watched him still a storm, and the one thing they asked him to teach them was not how to preach or how to perform a miracle. It was this. *Lord, teach us to pray* (Luke 11:1). The men closest to the praying life of God in human form looked at it and concluded they did not know how. So the question you are embarrassed to ask is the same question the first disciples asked out loud, to his face.

Here is what I want to give you, and it will cost you the comfort of a formula. **Prayer is not a technique you master. It is a conversation you enter with Someone who has been speaking first the entire time, and learning to pray is mostly learning to stop performing long enough to answer.**

## The lie you learned before you learned anything

Most of us absorbed an idea about prayer before we ever consciously thought about it, and the idea is poisonous. We learned that prayer is a performance evaluated by God for quality. That there is a right vocabulary, a properly reverent tone, a length below which it does not count and above which it impresses. We learned, somewhere, that prayer is something you are graded on.

Jesus attacked this directly, and it is worth sitting in how directly. In the Sermon on the Mount he tells the crowd not to pray like the people who stand on corners to be seen, who heap up empty phrases thinking they will be heard for their many words, because — and this is the line that should undo every anxious beginner — *your Father knows what you need before you ask him* (Matthew 6:5–8). Read that slowly. The performance is not just discouraged. It is pointless. You are not informing God. You are not impressing God. You are not assembling the correct phrases to pull a response from a deity who would otherwise withhold it. He already knows. The knowing came first.

Which means the entire anxiety underneath *how do you actually pray* — the fear that you will do it wrong — is built on a premise Jesus demolished in three sentences. There is no wrong vocabulary because there is no exam. The God you are praying to is not a judge scoring your fluency. He is a Father who, Jesus says, already knows, and is waiting not for your eloquence but for your face.

Tim Keller, in his 2014 book *Prayer*, names the trap with a phrase I have never been able to shake. He says most people treat prayer as a way of getting things from God, when the deepest tradition of the church understood it as a way of getting God himself. The difference is everything. If prayer is a vending machine, then technique matters enormously — you had better push the right buttons. If prayer is the way you come to know a Person, then the fumbling, the silence, the not-knowing-what-to-say is not a failure of the process. It is the process. No one knows what to say in the first weeks of love.

## Why no one is born knowing how

There is a reason the disciples had to ask, and it is worth dwelling on, because it lifts a weight off you.

Prayer is not instinctive the way breathing is. It is learned the way a language is learned — by being around people who speak it, by imitation, by years of clumsy practice. This is exactly why God did not drop a technique out of the sky; he gave a people, and the people prayed together for centuries, and a child growing up among them absorbed how it was done the way she absorbed her mother tongue. Israel learned to pray by praying the Psalms at the festivals and in the home. The disciples learned by watching Jesus slip away before dawn to pray and asking him, eventually, to teach them what they had seen. The early church learned by praying the Lord's Prayer aloud together three times a day. In every case the learning ran through a community, not a manual.

We have forgotten this, and the forgetting is most of the modern problem. We treat prayer as a private skill you are supposed to possess fully formed, in the solitude of your own room, on the first try, and when you cannot we conclude something is wrong with us. But no one learns a language alone in a room. If your prayer life is starving, the question may not be *what technique am I missing* but *who am I praying near.* Find people who pray and pray with them. Let their words give yours a shape. Pray written prayers if free prayer fails you — the church wrote thousands of them precisely so the beginner would not have to start from nothing. There is no shame in praying someone else's words until your own arrive. That is how every Christian who has ever prayed well began, and most of them never fully graduated from it, and neither will you, and that is not a deficiency. It is how the thing was always meant to be passed down.

## What it actually is

So let me try to say plainly what prayer is, stripped of the machinery.

Prayer is talking to God as though he is real, present, and listening — because he is. That is almost the whole of it. The complexity we have layered on top is mostly ours. A child does not study the grammar of address before speaking to her father. She speaks because he is there and she trusts that he hears, and the speaking is clumsy and circular and full of repetition and complaint, and it is exactly what he wants. Jesus chose the word *Abba*. Not a formal word. The word a small child uses for the parent who picks them up. He handed us the most intimate possible address and we turned it into a liturgy we are afraid to get wrong.

Now, intimacy is not informality for its own sake, and I want to be careful here, because the modern correction overshoots. The God you speak to is the God who made the galaxies and who, in the next breath, knows the number of hairs on your head. Both are true and prayer holds both. You are not chatting with a buddy. You are not performing for a distant king. You are addressing the One who is simultaneously infinitely above you and nearer to you than your own breathing, and the strangeness of that is not a problem to solve. It is the actual texture of the thing.

Teresa of Ávila, the sixteenth-century Spanish reformer who wrote more wisely about the inner life of prayer than almost anyone, defined it in a single line that has outlived four centuries. Prayer, she said, is nothing else than an intimate sharing between friends; it means taking time frequently to be alone with him who we know loves us. Notice what is not in that sentence. No technique. No required vocabulary. No threshold of feeling. Two things only — that you take the time, and that you remember he loves you. A teenager learning to pray and a mystic in an ecstasy of contemplation are, by Teresa's definition, doing the identical thing. Taking time to be alone with the One who loves them. The mystic simply has more practice.

## Then why is it so hard

Because if it is this simple, the honest reader is already objecting. If prayer is just talking to a God who already knows and already loves, why does it feel like talking into a dead phone? Why the dryness, the wandering mind, the suspicion that no one is on the other end?

I am not going to wave that away, because it is the most truthful question in the whole subject, and the tradition takes it seriously where the marketing does not. Part of the answer is that you are a body, and bodies are restless, and the mind wanders not because you are a bad Christian but because you are a human being whose attention has been trained for years by every screen you own to never rest on one thing for ninety seconds. The wandering is not a verdict on your faith. It is a verdict on the age, and the saints who never saw a phone complained of the identical wandering, which should comfort you. Teresa called her own mind a wild horse and a clattering mill. The interior chaos is not new. It is human.

But there is a deeper answer, and it is the one I most want you to carry. Paul writes that we do not even know what to pray for as we ought — and then, instead of telling us to try harder, he tells us that the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words (Romans 8:26). Read what that does to the whole anxious project. The not-knowing is not the obstacle to prayer. The not-knowing is built into the design, and God has already accounted for it. When you sit down and have no words, when the groan is all that comes, that groan is not the absence of prayer. It is, Paul says, the Spirit praying inside you, carrying upward what you could not form. The most inarticulate prayer you have ever prayed — the one that was only weeping, only *help*, only silence with your face in your hands — was not the failed version. It may have been the truest.

## So here is where you actually begin

I promised you no formula and I will keep that promise, but I will not leave you with nothing, because *teach us to pray* deserves an answer and not a fog.

Begin by telling God the truth. Whatever is actually in you — the fear, the resentment, the boredom, the specific gnawing thing you have not said to anyone — say that. Not the cleaned-up version you think he wants. The real one. He knows it already, so the only person the cleaning-up protects is you, and it protects you from the one relationship that could actually hold the real thing. The Psalms, which I write about elsewhere, are God's own evidence that he can take the unedited version, because he put the unedited version in the prayer book of his people on purpose.

Begin small and begin daily, because prayer is more like a marriage than a lightning strike. A short, honest, regular conversation will form you more than a rare and spectacular one. Five minutes you actually keep will do more than an hour you only fantasize about. Keller's counsel, drawn straight from the long tradition, is to anchor the speaking to God's own words — to read a little of Scripture, let it turn your attention toward who God actually is, and pray back what you find there. This is why the church has always prayed the Psalms and prayed the prayer Jesus gave. You are not generating prayer from an empty tank. You are answering a God who spoke first, and his words give you yours.

A simple pattern helps if you want one, and the church has used it for centuries under the old name of adoration, confession, thanksgiving, and supplication — though you do not need the labels. Tell God who he is and that he is worth your attention. Tell him what you have done and where you have failed, plainly, without groveling and without excuse. Thank him for what is actually in front of you today, however small. Then ask him for what you and the people you love need. The four motions keep you from collapsing prayer into nothing but a list of requests, which is where most of us drift if no one stops us. But hold the pattern loosely. It is a trellis, not a cage — something for the living thing to climb, not a rule you can fail. If you only manage one of the four on a given morning, you have still prayed.

And begin without waiting to feel like it, because the feeling is not the prayer and was never the point. You will have days the words are warm and days they are ash, and the God on the other end does not love you more on the warm days. The discipline of coming when you do not feel it is not hypocrisy. It is fidelity, and fidelity is what love looks like on the ordinary days that make up almost all of a life.

## What you are really asking

I have answered the question on its surface, and now I want to answer the thing underneath it, because *how do you actually pray* is almost never only a question about method.

When people ask me this, white-knuckled, in hospital rooms and across my desk, what they are usually asking is whether anyone is there. Whether the silence has a Person in it. Whether the speaking is into a void or into the ear of God. And I cannot give you that on the strength of a technique, because no technique reaches across that gap. Only the One on the other side reaches across it, and the staggering claim at the center of the Christian faith is that he already has — that the God who knows what you need before you ask became flesh, prayed with a human voice in a garden until he sweat blood, and taught a handful of confused men the words to say. He did not stand at a safe distance and grade their attempts. He got into the room.

So you do not have to pray well. You were never going to pray well. You have to pray honestly, to a Father who already knows, in the company of a Spirit who prays the words you cannot find. The disciples started exactly where you are starting, with a request that admitted they did not know how. That admission was not the thing that disqualified them. It was the first true prayer they prayed.

Start there. Say *I do not know how to do this.* You will have begun.
