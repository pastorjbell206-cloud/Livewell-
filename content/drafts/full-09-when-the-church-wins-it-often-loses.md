Constantine was the worst thing that happened to the Christian church in its first thousand years. He was also one of the best. The fact that both sentences are true is the lesson the church has spent seventeen centuries refusing to learn.

I want to write this essay as a counterweight to the persecution-narrative and the lost-Christendom-grief I have been pressing against in the previous essays. The American evangelical imagination is haunted by the loss of cultural power. The haunting is sincere and is making it impossible for us to see that almost every time the church has won political power, the church has paid for the winning more than the winning was ever worth. The pattern is not partisan. The pattern is not modern. The pattern has been the pattern for seventeen centuries.

I am going to walk it slowly. The walking is the argument.

## Constantine

The standard story of the conversion of Constantine — a vision before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge in 312, a confession of Christ as the source of his victory, the issuing of the Edict of Milan in 313 to grant Christians legal toleration, the slow movement of the empire toward Christianity over the next half-century — has been told as the great vindication of the early church. The three centuries of persecution ended. The Lord won. The empire bowed.

The standard story is partly true. The end of formal persecution was a relief. Christians could worship openly. Buildings could be built. Bishops could meet in councils — Nicaea in 325 was the first major one, called and largely paid for by Constantine himself — and define orthodox doctrine with imperial backing. The institutional infrastructure that allowed the church to consolidate its theology, build its hospitals and schools, and weather the eventual collapse of the western empire was, in a real sense, made possible by Constantine's patronage.

This is the part of the story most American evangelicals know, and the part most pastors have preached. The other part is harder.

The other part is what Constantine cost. The patronage that funded the bishops also tied the bishops to the imperial court. The legal toleration that protected Christians also extracted from Christians a willingness to align with imperial purposes. The bishops who would not align — like Athanasius, repeatedly exiled by Constantine's son Constantius II for refusing to soften his Christology in ways the court found politically useful — became a problem the empire had to manage. The empire managed them, on the whole, by replacing them with bishops who would align.

By the late fourth century, when Theodosius made Christianity the state religion in 380, the church had been thoroughly captured by the imperial structure. The bishops were imperial functionaries. The councils were managed by the emperors. The orthodoxy was, in significant respects, defined by what the empire could enforce. The church had won. The church had also been substantially conscripted. The early Christian conviction that the Christian could not say *kyrios kaisar* — that there was a hard line between the lordship of Christ and the lordship of the empire — was softened, then blurred, then mostly forgotten. The line did not disappear; the desert fathers fled to the desert in the fourth century precisely to maintain it. But the line stopped being the central conviction of the public church. The public church became, in a real sense, the chaplain of the empire.

The desert fathers' flight is the indispensable witness here. Anthony in Egypt, Pachomius founding monastic communities, the great spread of monasticism through the fourth and fifth centuries — these were people who saw what Constantine had done and concluded that the imperial church could no longer hold the gospel in its fullness, and that the gospel had to be preserved in communities that withdrew from imperial patronage. The monastic movement is the church's first and largest response to the Constantinian shift. It is the response of people who knew the church had won and had also, in the winning, lost something it could not afford to lose.

## Charlemagne

Four hundred years later, the pattern repeats. Charlemagne is crowned Holy Roman Emperor on Christmas Day, 800, by Pope Leo III in St. Peter's Basilica. The crowning is one of the most consequential events in European history. It marks the formal alliance of the papal church with the political project of a Christian European empire. It produces, over the next three centuries, the Holy Roman Empire as the political form of medieval Latin Christendom.

The good of the Carolingian arrangement was real. It funded the Carolingian Renaissance — Alcuin at the court at Aachen, the recovery of patristic manuscripts, the standardization of liturgy and ecclesiastical Latin, the slow rebuilding of literate culture after centuries of post-Roman decay. The church benefited. The culture benefited. The arrangement, in some real sense, saved European Christianity from disintegration in the post-Roman period.

The cost of the arrangement was also real. The investiture controversy — the eleventh-century struggle between popes and emperors over who had the right to appoint bishops — exposed how badly the church had been conscripted by political power. The papal reform movements under Gregory VII and his successors tried to recover some independence for the church, and partly succeeded; but the recovery was itself political, and required the papacy to develop its own imperial pretensions, and led to the medieval papal monarchy whose corruptions provoked the Reformation. The pattern is consistent. The church wins political power and then has to spend centuries trying to extract itself from the consequences, and the extraction is itself a political act, and the political acts compound, and at no point does the church return to the simplicity it had before it won.

The crusades were the natural endpoint of the Charlemagne arrangement. Urban II's call for the First Crusade in 1095 was a Christian-empire response to a perceived Christian-empire threat. The crusades killed enormous numbers of Muslims and Jews — and ended, in the Fourth Crusade in 1204, with Western Christian armies sacking the Eastern Christian capital of Constantinople. The split between Eastern and Western Christianity, formalized in 1054, was made permanent by the Fourth Crusade in ways no theological dialogue has yet been able to heal. The church that had won imperial power had used that power to attack itself.

## The Wars of Religion

The Reformation begins in 1517, and within a generation it is producing wars. The German Peasants' War (1524–25), the French Wars of Religion (1562–98), the Eighty Years' War in the Netherlands (1568–1648), and finally the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) — these are wars in which Christians killed Christians, on a scale and with a cruelty that had not been seen in European history before, over which version of Christianity the various political authorities would establish.

The Thirty Years' War killed perhaps eight million people. It depopulated parts of Germany so completely that the demographic recovery took over a century. It was fought, in significant part, by Christian armies under Christian banners, with Christian theologians on every side providing theological justifications. It ended with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, which essentially gave up on the question of which Christianity was the right one and agreed that the religious affiliation of a territory would be determined by its political ruler — *cuius regio, eius religio.* The settlement was political because the theological question had become unanswerable, and the political settlement marked the moment when European Christianity gave up on Christian political theology and accepted the rise of the nation-state as the basic political form.

The settlement was, in retrospect, the beginning of the end of Christendom. Westphalia established that Christianity would be a private and territorial matter, not a unifying political ideology. The next four centuries are the slow working-out of that settlement — the rise of religious toleration, the eventual establishment of religious liberty, the privatization of religious commitment, and the secularization of public life. Westphalia did not cause secularization, but Westphalia was the political precondition for it. The wars of religion had so discredited Christian political theology that no significant European intellectual movement after Westphalia attempted to recover it. The Enlightenment that followed was, in part, a response to the failure of Christian political theology to keep Christians from killing each other.

The church had won, again, in the sense that one or another version of Christianity had been established as the state religion of every major European polity. The church had lost, again, in the sense that the political establishment of Christianity had cost it the very theological coherence that made the establishment worth having.

## The American Settlement

The American experiment was different. The First Amendment refused the European pattern. It established no state religion, and it protected the free exercise of religion against state interference. The American settlement was the first significant Western attempt to do the church without political establishment, and it was, in some respects, enormously successful. The disestablishment of religion in the United States was followed by one of the most vigorous Christian centuries in the country's history. The Second Great Awakening, the missionary movements, the abolitionist movement (much of it Christian), the temperance movement, the social-gospel movement — these were the fruits of a Christianity that did not depend on state establishment for its energy.

But American evangelicalism, over the twentieth century, gradually drifted away from the disestablishment commitment. The civil-religion synthesis of the postwar period — the *under God* added to the Pledge of Allegiance in 1954, the *In God We Trust* added to currency in 1956, the rise of the Moral Majority and the Christian Right in the 1970s and 1980s — produced a generation of evangelicals who began to think of America as in some sense a Christian nation, and to think of political activism in defense of that Christian identity as a primary duty of Christian discipleship.

The cost of that drift is now becoming visible. Tim Alberta's *The Kingdom, the Power, and the Glory* (2023) is the most thorough recent documentation. The political organization of evangelicalism over the past forty years has produced, in many evangelical institutions, a thoroughgoing conflation of partisan identity with Christian identity, a willingness to abandon historic Christian moral commitments when those commitments interfere with partisan goals, and a generation of younger Americans who have left the church largely because they could not see the Christ of the gospels in the political project the institutional church had become.

The American evangelical experiment, which began as a refusal of European-style establishment, has gradually become a soft establishment of its own — a cultural Christianity backed by political organization, demanding political deference, willing to use political tools to defend cultural privilege. And in defending the cultural privilege, the evangelical movement has paid the same kind of price the Constantinian church paid, and the Carolingian church paid, and the post-Westphalian churches paid. It has lost theological coherence. It has lost moral credibility with the generations that watched the political project replace the gospel. It has lost the next generation, which has, in significant numbers, simply left.

## The verdict

I want to write this slowly. The pattern is not partisan. The pattern is not modern. The pattern is the seventeen-century pattern. Every time the church has gained political power, the gain has cost the church more than the gain has been worth. The Constantinian church paid; the desert fathers fled. The Carolingian church paid; the investiture controversy followed. The Reformation churches paid; the Thirty Years' War followed. The post-Vatican-II American Catholic and post-Civil-Rights evangelical Protestant churches are paying; the exit of the younger generations is following.

The cost is not always immediate. The cost is sometimes delayed by a generation or two. The cost has, in every case, eventually arrived. The church that has won political power has, in every case, eventually paid for the winning with the loss of something more important than what was won.

This is not a counsel of withdrawal. The church is not supposed to retreat from the public square. The church is supposed to be in the public square, witnessing to the gospel, doing the works of mercy, naming the truth at cost. The church is not supposed to be running the public square. The distinction is important. The Christian witness is one thing. Christian political power is another. The witness has been the church's mode for most of its history. The political power has been the church's exception, and the exception has, in every case, cost the church the witness.

I am writing this for the American evangelical reader who is grieving the loss of political power. I want to say, slowly and carefully: the loss is mercy. The loss is the restoration of the church to the condition that the church has historically held best. The political power that we are losing was, in many ways, costing us the witness we were supposed to be giving. The losing is hard. The losing is also the church being given back what the church should have refused in the first place, when Constantine made the offer.

The church that is willing to lose can keep what matters. The church that has insisted on winning has, in seventeen centuries, kept the winning and lost the rest. We are at a hinge point in the American case. We can lose well, and recover the witness. We can keep insisting on winning, and lose what we have left.

The strongman politics that has been offered to American evangelicals as a defense against the loss is not a defense at all. The strongman is not your savior. The strongman has, in seventeen centuries, never been the church's savior. The next essay says so plainly, and pays the price for saying so. Stay for it.
