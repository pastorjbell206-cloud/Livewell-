Around the year 397, a middle-aged African bishop sat down and did something no one in the ancient world had really done before. He wrote the story of his own soul. Not his accomplishments — he had plenty. Not his battles or his travels. He wrote the story of his interior life, the wantings and the failures and the long detour he had taken before he finally stopped running. He addressed the whole thing to God, as a prayer, which means we are reading over his shoulder while a man talks to his Maker about everything he is most ashamed of. The book is called the *Confessions*, and sixteen hundred years later it is still in print, still being read by people who have never set foot in a church, because they open it and find, to their alarm, that it is about them.

His name was Aurelius Augustinus — Augustine. He was born in 354 in a town in what is now Algeria, in Roman North Africa. He was not born into power or wealth, but he was born brilliant, and everyone around him knew it early. His mother, Monica, was a Christian who prayed for his soul for decades. His father was not. Augustine grew up smart, ambitious, hungry — for sex, for status, for the applause that comes to a man who can speak well — and he spent the first half of his life chasing every one of those hungers down to the bottom and finding nothing there. The most famous sentence he ever wrote sits on the first page of the *Confessions* and explains the whole rest of it: "You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you."

That is the thesis of his life. He had a restless heart, and he tried to quiet it with everything a person reaches for, and none of it worked, and the not-working is the story.

## The most honest book anyone had written

You have to grasp how strange the *Confessions* was. Ancient autobiography, where it existed, was a monument. A great man told you about his great deeds so you would admire him. Augustine did the opposite. He told God — and us — about the time he stole pears as a boy, not because he was hungry but for the pure thrill of doing wrong, and then he spent pages agonizing over what that small theft revealed about the rottenness in the human will. He wrote about his sexual life with a candor that still makes readers shift in their seats. He had a long-term partner, a woman he never names, with whom he lived for years and had a son he loved, and he wrote about the wrenching pain of leaving her for the sake of an ambitious marriage that never happened. He prayed one of the most famous and honest lines in religious literature: "Grant me chastity and self-control — but please, not yet." He wanted to be good. He did not want it badly enough to stop, and he knew it, and he said so.

This is why the great historian Peter Brown, whose 1967 biography *Augustine of Hippo* is still the book scholars hand you first, called him essentially the inventor of the inner self as we know it. Before Augustine, people did not generally write as though the most interesting and most contested territory in the universe was the inside of one's own divided heart. After Augustine, the West could not stop. Every memoir you have ever read that turns its gaze inward and tells the truth about the gap between who we want to be and what we actually do — every one of them is, whether the author knows it or not, walking in tracks Augustine cut sixteen centuries ago.

And this is also why a newcomer to the faith should read him before almost anyone else. Augustine does not start with answers. He starts with the wanting. He starts exactly where most of us actually live — restless, divided, reaching for the next thing in the hope that it will finally be enough. He is not a man who had it figured out lecturing you from above. He is a man who was a mess, writing up from inside the mess, telling you how he got out.

## The years of running

For most of his twenties and into his thirties, Augustine ran. Not from God, as he understood it at the time — he would have told you he was searching for truth, and he was. He tried a fashionable religious movement of the day, a sect called the Manichees, who offered a tidy explanation of evil that flattered his sense of being one of the enlightened few. He left it disillusioned. He flirted with the skeptics, who said you couldn't really know anything for certain. He chased a career as a teacher of rhetoric — the ancient equivalent of a high-end communications consultant — and it carried him from Africa to Rome to Milan, the imperial capital, where he landed a prestigious post and the kind of platform an ambitious provincial dreamed of.

And he was miserable. He tells the story of riding through Milan in his fine clothes on his way to deliver a grand speech praising the emperor, a speech full of lies he knew were lies, and passing a drunk beggar in the street, laughing, happy. And Augustine realized the beggar had, by sheer dumb luck, gotten to the joy that Augustine was killing himself to reach and never reaching. He had everything the world told him to want. It was ash in his mouth.

What broke the spell, slowly, was two things. First, he encountered Christian preaching he could not dismiss — in Milan he heard the great bishop Ambrose, and discovered that the faith of his mother, which he had written off as simple, was in fact intellectually serious in ways that embarrassed him. Second, he could not get free of the wanting. He could see the good. He could not will himself into it. He describes the agony of a divided will with a precision no one had matched: the part of him that wanted God and the part of him that wanted everything else, locked in a war he kept losing.

If you want the older biblical echo of this, it is the apostle Paul on the road to Damascus in Acts 9 — a man dead set on his own course, certain he is right, knocked flat and remade against everything he had planned for his life. Augustine knew that story well. He just did not yet know it was about to become his.

## The garden

It came to a head in a garden in Milan, in the late summer of 386. Augustine tells it as the hinge of his whole life, and it is worth slowing down for, because it is one of the most vivid conversion scenes ever written.

He was in torment. A friend had just told him the story of two Roman officials who had given up their careers to follow Christ, and the story landed on Augustine like an accusation: *the unlearned rise up and take heaven by storm, and here we are with all our learning, wallowing.* He fled into the garden of the house where he was staying, weeping, tearing at his hair, throwing himself down under a fig tree. His will was at war with itself and he could not break the deadlock. He cried out the question that anyone who has ever wanted to change and couldn't has cried out: *How long? How long this "tomorrow and tomorrow"? Why not now? Why not an end to my uncleanness this very hour?*

And then he heard, from a neighboring house, the voice of a child — he could not tell if it was a boy or a girl — singing a little phrase over and over as part of some game: *Tolle, lege. Tolle, lege.* Take up and read. Take up and read.

He took it as a command from God. He ran back to where he had left a copy of Paul's letters, snatched it up, and read the first passage his eyes fell on. It was Romans 13:13–14: "Let us behave decently, as in the daytime, not in carousing and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and debauchery, not in dissension and jealousy. Rather, clothe yourselves with the Lord Jesus Christ, and do not think about how to gratify the desires of the flesh." He says he did not need to read further. "Instantly, as I reached the end of the sentence, it was as though the light of certainty flooded my heart, and all the shadows of doubt were swept away." The war was over. The wanting that had ruled him for thirty-one years let go.

He went and told his mother, Monica, who had prayed for this day longer than he had been alive. The *Confessions* records her joy. She died not long after, content, and he buried her, and you can feel in the pages how much that ordinary grief — a son burying his praying mother — sat at the center of his understanding of grace.

## What he saw that we keep forgetting

Here is why Augustine still matters, and not just as a good story. Out of those running years he drew a conclusion about the human heart that the modern world has spent a great deal of effort trying to forget, and that keeps proving true anyway.

The conclusion is this: our problem is not mainly that we lack information. It is that we love the wrong things, or we love the right things in the wrong order. Augustine called it *disordered love*. We are not empty vessels who need to be told what is good. We can usually see what is good. We want the lesser thing more. The drunk beggar in Milan was happier than the celebrated rhetorician because Augustine's loves were pointed at things that could not hold the weight he was putting on them — applause, status, the next pleasure. Psalm 139, which Augustine prayed constantly, says God has searched us and known us, knows our sitting down and our rising up, has hemmed us in behind and before. "Where can I go from your Spirit? Where can I flee from your presence?" the psalm asks (Psalm 139:7). Augustine read that not as surveillance but as rescue. The God who knows the inside of you, all of it, the part you would never confess to your closest friend — that God is the only one who can finally reorder what you love, because he is the only one who already sees what you actually love.

This is the architecture of his whole thought, and it is why Tim Keller, who learned more from Augustine than from almost anyone, kept returning to it in our own day: you are not what you think. You are what you love. And you cannot simply decide to love differently by force of will — Augustine tried that for years in the garden of his own effort and failed every time. Love is reordered by a greater love breaking in. That is what happened under the fig tree. He did not finally muster the willpower to choose God. God, as he tells it, finally became more desirable than the things that had owned him, and the chains he could not break fell off because he no longer wanted them on.

## The bishop, and the long shadow

Augustine did not vanish into private piety after the garden. He went home to Africa, was more or less drafted into the priesthood by a congregation that recognized what it had, and spent the last thirty-four years of his life as the bishop of Hippo, a working pastor in a provincial town. This is the part that endears him to me, and that ought to encourage anyone who imagines the great figures of church history lived on some higher plane. The man who wrote the most influential spiritual autobiography in history spent most of his days doing what pastors have always done: settling petty disputes among his people, preaching week after week to an ordinary congregation, burying the dead, answering letters from the anxious and the argumentative, and trying to keep a small church together in a crumbling empire. He wrote his greatest works in the cracks of an exhausting ministry, not in a scholar's leisure.

And the shadow he cast is almost impossible to overstate. For more than a thousand years, Western Christianity essentially thought with Augustine's mind. The reformers — Martin Luther was an Augustinian monk before he was anything else — read him as the great witness to grace, to the truth that we are rescued by God's mercy rather than by our own moral achievement, which was the thing Augustine had learned the hard way in the garden when his own willpower failed him over and over. His insistence that human beings are not basically fine, that there is something genuinely disordered in us that we cannot fix by trying harder, ran against the optimism of his age and ours alike, and it keeps being confirmed by anyone honest enough to watch their own behavior for a week. He died in 430, an old man, as an army of invaders besieged his city and the Roman world he had known collapsed around him. He had spent his life pointing past every collapsing thing to the one rest that does not fall.

## The restlessness was the mercy

I want to end where he began, because it is the most pastoral thing he ever said and the easiest to miss.

We tend to treat our restlessness as a defect. The dissatisfaction that follows us through one accomplishment after another, the way the thing we were sure would finally settle us turns out, once we have it, to settle nothing — we treat that as a sign that something is wrong with us, or that we just haven't found the right thing yet. Augustine spent half his life on that assumption and it nearly destroyed him. Then he turned it around. The restlessness was not the disease. The restlessness was the symptom that pointed to the cure. *You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you.* The ache was not random. It was a homing signal. It was the part of him that had been made for God refusing to be satisfied by anything less than God, no matter how hard he tried to drug it quiet.

If you have felt that — the strange poverty in the middle of having what you wanted, the hunger that the next thing never fills — Augustine would tell you not to despair of it and not to try to kill it. He would tell you it is the truest thing about you. He chased it down every wrong road for thirty-one years and it would not let him go, and in the end he was grateful, because the only reason he was still restless was that the One who made him had refused to let him settle for less than himself.

He could not outrun God. He tells the whole story as the discovery, late and astonished, that he had never been the one doing the chasing.
