There is a letter, written around the year 155, from one church to another, describing the death of an old man. The old man's name was Polycarp, and he was the bishop of a city called Smyrna, on the coast of what is now Turkey. He was eighty-six. The Roman governor had him brought into the arena, where a crowd was waiting, and offered him the simplest way out anyone has ever been offered. All Polycarp had to do was say the words *Caesar is Lord*, drop a small pinch of incense onto a fire, and curse the name of Christ. Three small actions. A few seconds of his life. And then he could go home to his church and his old age and his bed.

The letter records what he said. "Eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King who saved me?" They burned him alive. The letter says the believers gathered his bones afterward, which they considered "more precious than gold," and buried them, and met there on the anniversary of his death to remember him with joy.

I want you to sit inside the strangeness of that for a moment, because it is the doorway into everything that follows. A pinch of incense. That is all it would have taken. Not a renunciation of his whole moral life, not a betrayal of his friends, not anything that would have made the morning paper. A gesture so small that a modern person can barely understand why he didn't just do it and cross his fingers. He had eighty-six years of reasons to live. He chose the fire instead. To understand the people who came before us — the people on whose graves the entire Christian story stands — you have to understand why that pinch of incense was the one thing he could not do.

## The world that made the question impossible to dodge

Start with the Roman world, because we have flattened it in our imaginations into something it never was. We picture Rome as a secular state with a hobby of feeding Christians to lions. It was nothing of the kind. Rome was drenched in religion. The gods were everywhere — at the door of every house, over every meal, in the founding myths of the city, in the calendar, in the army, in the marketplace. Religion was not a private opinion you held on the weekend. It was the glue of public life, the thing that held the whole vast empire together. To be a good Roman was to honor the gods who had made Rome great, and at the center of it all, by the first century, stood the emperor himself, increasingly treated as divine.

This is the part that matters. When a Roman official asked a Christian to burn incense to the emperor, he did not, in his own mind, think he was demanding much. He was asking for a loyalty oath. It was closer to standing for a national anthem than to a profession of theology. The gesture said: *I am a good citizen, I wish the empire well, I am one of us.* Refusing it was not refusing a creed. It looked, to Rome, like sedition. It looked like a person announcing they did not belong to the human community at all.

And the Christians knew exactly how it looked. That is what makes their refusal so striking. They were not confused. They understood they could keep their heads down, honor Caesar in this one symbolic way, and go right on worshiping Jesus in private. Plenty of people in the ancient world held many gods at once; adding one more cost nothing. The Christians would not add one more. They had read the first commandment and taken it with a literalness that baffled their neighbors. There was one Lord, and his name was not Caesar.

The sociologist Rodney Stark, in his 1996 study *The Rise of Christianity*, asks the hard-nosed question that a historian has to ask: how did a tiny, persecuted, illegal movement of perhaps a few thousand people grow, in less than three centuries, into the dominant faith of the Roman Empire? His answer is not what you would expect from a man writing as a social scientist. Part of it, he argues, is precisely this willingness to die. A faith that asks nothing of you is worth nothing to you. A faith that people will pay for with their lives is a faith that announces, with total clarity, that it believes its own claims. The martyrs were not a public-relations disaster the church had to survive. They were, in a strange and terrible way, the church's most persuasive argument.

## Who they actually were

We say "the martyrs" and the word goes abstract, like "the casualties" or "the fallen." So let me give you two of them by name, because they were not a category. They were people.

Polycarp you have already met. He mattered enormously to the early church for a reason beyond his courage: he was a living wire back to the beginning. The tradition, well attested in the early sources, is that Polycarp had been a disciple of the apostle John — that as a young man he had sat at the feet of someone who had walked with Jesus. When he died in 155, he was one of the last living links to the eyewitness generation. The early church held onto men like him the way a family holds onto the last grandparent who remembers the old country. When he refused the incense, it was not theology refusing a gesture. It was memory refusing to lie.

The second is harder to read about, and you should read about her anyway. Her name was Perpetua, and she died in Carthage, in North Africa, around the year 203. She was about twenty-two. She was a young mother, well-born, educated, and still nursing her infant son. We know her not from a stranger's letter but, remarkably, from what scholars believe to be her own prison diary — one of the earliest surviving pieces of writing by a Christian woman, set down in her own voice in the days before her death. In it she describes her father coming to the prison again and again, begging her to recant. He is not a villain. He is a frightened old man who loves his daughter and cannot understand why she will throw her life away over a word. At one point he takes the baby. At one point he tears at his own beard and throws himself on the ground. She writes that she grieved for him — and that she would not deny the name. She was killed in the arena for the entertainment of a crowd, alongside her pregnant slave, Felicity, who had given birth in prison days before.

Read Perpetua's diary and the abstraction shatters. This is not a stained-glass saint. This is a young woman with a baby at her breast and a weeping father at the bars, making the one decision none of us can imagine making, and writing it down so we would know it was real. We tend to picture the early Christians as old men with beards. A great many of them were young, and a great many of them were women, and a number of them were enslaved people like Felicity who owned nothing in this world and gave up the one thing they had left.

## Why the incense, of all things

Here is where a newcomer's honest question deserves an honest answer. Why was a pinch of incense the line? Why not just do it?

Because the Christians had grasped something about worship that we have largely lost, and it is worth recovering even if you are not sure you believe any of it. They understood that worship is not a feeling. It is an act of allegiance, and allegiance cannot be divided. To offer incense to Caesar was to say, with your body, *Caesar holds the place that belongs to God.* It did not matter that you didn't mean it. The act meant it for you. They had inherited this conviction from the Jewish people, who had spent centuries dying rather than bow to the gods of whatever empire happened to be occupying them at the time, and who had handed the early church a story like the one in Acts 7 — the death of Stephen, the first Christian killed for his faith, stoned while he looked up and said he saw the heavens opened. Stephen set the pattern. He did not curse his killers. He prayed for them, and then he died.

The Christians read that story and others like it and drew a conclusion that the book of Revelation states with brutal economy. Speaking of the believers who overcame the great accuser, it says: "They triumphed over him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony; they did not love their lives so much as to shrink from death" (Revelation 12:11). *They did not love their lives so much as to shrink from death.* That is not a description of fanatics. It is a description of people who had relocated the center of gravity of their existence. Their lives were no longer the thing they were protecting. Something else had become the thing, and their lives had become negotiable in its service.

Jesus had told them this would be the shape of it. "Do not be afraid of those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul. Rather, be afraid of the One who can destroy both soul and body in hell" (Matthew 10:28). To a modern ear that sounds harsh. To Polycarp it sounded like freedom. If the worst the empire could do was kill his body, then the empire had already lost its grip on him. There was nothing left to threaten. A man who does not fear death cannot be ruled by anyone who trades in death — and that, more than any army, is what Rome could not finally defeat. The apostle Paul had said the same thing from a prison cell of his own: "For to me, to live is Christ and to die is gain" (Philippians 1:21). Polycarp simply lived out the arithmetic.

## What they were not

It is important to say what the martyrs were not, because the word "martyr" has been dragged through a great deal of mud in the centuries since, and the early Christians would not recognize most of what now travels under their name.

They were not people who went looking for death. The early church was, in fact, suspicious of believers who courted martyrdom, who turned themselves in or provoked the authorities. That was considered a kind of spiritual pride, a grasping after a crown you were supposed to receive, not seize. Polycarp himself had withdrawn to a farmhouse outside the city when the persecution began; he did not march into the arena. He went when he was found. The model was not a death wish. It was a refusal to lie when the lie was finally demanded.

And they were not haters. This is the detail that the Roman world found most disorienting, and the one Stark argues did the most to grow the church. The Christians prayed for the emperors who killed them. During the plagues that swept the empire — when the pagans, terrified of contagion, fled the cities and abandoned their own dying relatives in the streets — the Christians stayed. They nursed the sick, including the sick who were not their own, and many of them caught the diseases and died doing it. A faith that would die rather than worship Caesar would also die nursing a stranger's fever. It was all one thing. The historian Robert Louis Wilken, in *The Spirit of Early Christian Thought* (2003), spent his career insisting that we will never understand these people if we treat their faith as a set of opinions they happened to hold strongly. It was a whole way of seeing — of seeing God, the world, the body, the neighbor, and death itself — and the dying made no sense apart from the loving, and the loving made no sense apart from the God they refused to deny.

## What this asks of us

I have to be careful here, because the easy move is to turn the martyrs into a rebuke. To point at Polycarp in the fire and say to a comfortable modern church: *look how soft you have become.* And there is a truth in that, but it is not my truth to wield, because I have never been asked to do anything that cost me what it cost them. I have never been offered the incense. None of us reading this likely have. To stand at a safe distance of eighteen centuries and use their courage as a stick is its own kind of cowardice, and I will not do it.

What I will say is gentler and, I think, harder. The martyrs are not mainly a measuring stick. They are a family. When you become a Christian, you are not joining a movement that started with your favorite preacher or your grandmother's church. You are being grafted into something that runs back through Perpetua's prison cell and Polycarp's farmhouse and Stephen's bloodied face, all the way to an empty tomb. These people are not historical curiosities. They are the relatives you never met whose decisions made your faith possible. The reason there was a church for you to walk into at all is that an eighty-six-year-old man in Smyrna decided that eighty-six years of mercy were worth more than one more morning of his life.

So I do not ask you to die. I ask you to recognize whose house you have walked into. There are bones under the floor, "more precious than gold," and the people who gathered them did so with joy, not grief, because they were sure — surer than we tend to be about anything — that the King they served had already walked out of his own grave and would not leave them in theirs. They built the room you are standing in. They are still in it. And the question they leave on the table is not whether you could die like them. It is whether you believe, as plainly as they did, that there is anyone worth it.
