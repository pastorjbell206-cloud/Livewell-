Reach over and pick up a Bible. Any Bible — the one on the shelf, the app on your phone, the hotel-drawer copy. Notice how ordinary it feels. You can read it. You did not have to ask anyone's permission. You can underline it, argue with it, leave it in the bathroom, lose it and buy another for the price of lunch. It is the most printed book in human history and one of the cheapest objects you own. The thing in your hands is so easy to get that it is easy to forget it was ever hard.

It was hard. For most of Christian history, ordinary people could not read the Bible for themselves, and a great many powerful people intended to keep it that way. The book you can pick up without a thought was, for centuries, locked behind a wall of Latin and law and fear, and the men who set out to break that wall down were hunted for it. One of them — a brilliant, stubborn Englishman named William Tyndale — was strangled and his body burned in 1536 for the crime of putting the New Testament into plain English. He died for the sentences you can now read on a screen while waiting for coffee. This is the story of how the book got to you, and it is one of the most consequential stories in the history of the West.

## The wall, and why it stood

To feel the story you have to understand the wall. By the late Middle Ages, the Bible existed in Western Europe almost entirely in Latin — a translation called the Vulgate, made by the scholar Jerome around the year 400. In Jerome's time, Latin was the common tongue; the whole point had been to put the Bible into the language people spoke. But a thousand years later, Latin was a dead language that only the educated clergy could read. The Bible was, in effect, locked in a box only the professionals had the key to. The ordinary farmer or weaver or mother heard the Scriptures recited in a language they did not understand, and received their content secondhand, filtered entirely through what the church chose to tell them it said.

Now, plenty of churchmen defended this honestly. The Bible is a difficult book; left to untrained readers, they argued, it would be twisted into a thousand private heresies. There is a real concern buried in that, and it would be unfair to pretend the whole motive was control. But control was certainly part of it. A people who cannot read the source for themselves cannot check the institution against it. If you alone hold the text, you alone decide what it means, and your authority is unchallengeable because no one else can open the book. The wall was not only about protecting truth. It was also about protecting power, and the two had become difficult to tell apart.

Into that situation, across two centuries, came a series of men who decided that the people had a right to read the words of God in the language of their own kitchens.

## Wycliffe: the morning star

The first great Englishman to push hard against the wall was John Wycliffe, a sharp and controversial Oxford scholar in the 1300s. Wycliffe had a radical conviction for his time: that the Bible, not the institution, was the final authority, and that ordinary Christians ought to be able to read it. Under his influence, around the 1380s, the first complete English Bibles were produced — translated by hand from the Latin, copied by hand, since the printing press did not yet exist in Europe.

Think about what that meant. Every single copy had to be written out by hand, word by word, by someone who risked punishment for owning it. They were precious, rare, and dangerous to possess. Wycliffe's followers, mockingly called Lollards, carried them in secret and read them aloud in homes. The authorities responded with fury. Owning an English Bible could get you tried for heresy. The hostility to Wycliffe was so deep that decades after he died of natural causes, the church had his body dug up, burned, and the ashes thrown into a river — a posthumous execution of a man they could no longer reach. He has been called the morning star of the Reformation, the first light before the dawn. He saw what was coming a hundred and fifty years early.

It is hard, from here, to feel how shocking his conviction was. We take it for granted that a Christian reads the Bible. For most of the people in Wycliffe's England, the Bible was not something you read; it was something that was done to you, in a language you did not know, by men who alone could open it. The idea that a weaver in her own kitchen might hold the words of God in her own tongue and weigh for herself what they meant — that was not a small reform. It was a reordering of who had access to God, and everyone understood that reordering authority over the book meant reordering authority over the people who lived by it. That is why a scholarly question about translation could get a man's bones dug up and burned. It was never only about translation.

## Tyndale: the man who would not stop

The dawn came with William Tyndale, and his is the story that should be told in every church that owns an English Bible, because we mostly do not know it.

Tyndale was born around 1494, a Gloucestershire man, fiercely gifted in languages — he eventually worked in Hebrew, Greek, Latin, and several living European tongues. He came of age just as two things converged that Wycliffe never had: the printing press, which could now produce thousands of identical copies cheaply, and the recovery of the original Greek and Hebrew, which meant a translator no longer had to work from the Latin but could go back to the source. Tyndale wanted to translate the Bible directly from Greek and Hebrew into an English so plain that, in his famous boast to a learned clergyman who scorned the idea, "the boy that driveth the plough" would know more of Scripture than the scholar did.

The English authorities refused him permission and made it clear they would stop him. So Tyndale did something extraordinary: he left England, never to return, and spent the rest of his life on the run on the European continent, translating and printing the Bible in exile and smuggling the copies back into England hidden in bales of cloth and sacks of grain. His English New Testament appeared in 1526. The bishops bought up copies to burn them, sometimes unwittingly funding the printing of the next batch. He worked under constant threat, moving from city to city, betrayed more than once.

He was betrayed for the last time in 1535, near Brussels, by a man who had pretended to be his friend. He was imprisoned for over a year in a damp castle cell, where he wrote a letter — it survives — asking his captors for a warmer cap, a coat, and above all his Hebrew Bible, grammar, and dictionary, so he could keep working in prison. In October 1536 he was condemned, strangled at the stake, and his body burned. The tradition records his final words as a prayer: "Lord, open the King of England's eyes."

Here is the staggering part. Within three years of his death, the King of England did exactly that. Henry VIII authorized an official English Bible to be placed in every parish church in the land — and that authorized Bible, and nearly every major English Bible after it, including the celebrated King James Version of 1611, drew enormously on Tyndale's own translation. The very words the church burned him for printing became the words England read in church on Sundays. Phrases we think of as simply "biblical English" — "let there be light," "the powers that be," "the salt of the earth," "fight the good fight" — are Tyndale's. He lost his life and won the language. His prayer was answered within his own decade.

## Why anyone thought this was worth dying for

A newcomer is entitled to ask the obvious question. It is a book. Why would a sane, brilliant man throw away his life, on the run for a decade, to put it into English? Why not let the scholars keep their Latin and find some less fatal cause?

Because Wycliffe and Tyndale and the reformers who came after them believed something specific about this particular book, and it is the conviction that drove the whole upheaval we call the Reformation. They believed that the Scriptures are, as 2 Timothy 3:16 puts it, "God-breathed" — that through these words the living God actually speaks, not merely about ancient matters but to the reader, now. If that is true, then keeping the book locked in a dead language is not a neutral administrative arrangement. It is keeping people from the voice of God. It is standing between a person and the One trying to speak to them. They had taken God at his word in Isaiah 55:11 — that the word which goes out from his mouth "will not return to me empty, but will accomplish what I desire" — and they reasoned that a word with that kind of power had no business being locked away from the people it was sent to. Tyndale was not risking his life for literature. He was risking it for access — for the conviction that a plowboy had as much right to hear God speak as a bishop did, and that no institution had the right to stand in the middle.

The historian Alister McGrath, who has written the standard popular histories of this whole drama in *In the Beginning* (2001, on the making of the King James Bible) and *Christianity's Dangerous Idea* (2007, on the Reformation), names the dangerous idea precisely. The dangerous idea was that ordinary Christians could read and interpret the Bible for themselves. It was dangerous because it was destabilizing — it did, in fact, fracture Western Christianity into the many traditions we have today, for better and for worse, and McGrath does not pretend the costs were small. But it was also, he argues, one of the most powerful and liberating ideas in the history of the West, because it put the source back into the hands of the people, and a people who can read the source can never again be told, unchallenged, what it says.

It is worth pausing to be fair to the other side of this, because a newcomer should not be handed a simple story of heroes and villains. The men who wanted to keep the Bible in Latin were not all cynics guarding their power. Some of them had watched untrained readers take a verse out of context and build a destructive movement on it, and they were genuinely afraid of what would happen if everyone became their own interpreter. They were not entirely wrong to be afraid. The centuries after Tyndale did produce a great deal of confident misreading, a great deal of division, a great many people sure that their private reading of a difficult passage settled a question the wisest minds had argued for a thousand years. McGrath is candid about this. The dangerous idea was dangerous in both directions. It liberated, and it fractured, and it did both at once, and the same conviction that freed the plowboy also armed every crank with a proof text.

But the reformers made a wager about which danger was worse, and it is the wager worth understanding. They decided that a people kept from the source, dependent entirely on what they were told it said, was in greater peril than a people given the source and trusted, at real cost, to wrestle with it. They bet that a difficult freedom was better than a comfortable dependence. They bet that the God who breathed out these words was capable of reaching ordinary people through them without an official standing in the middle to license the meaning. It was a bet on the competence of ordinary believers and on the clarity of God's own voice, and they staked their lives on it. You can argue about whether they were entirely right. You cannot argue that they were cowards, and you cannot argue that the bet did not change the world.

There is an old picture of what this is supposed to feel like, and it is in Nehemiah 8. The people of Israel have returned from exile, and the scribe Ezra stands on a wooden platform and reads the Book of the Law aloud to the whole assembled people, from morning until midday, "and all the people listened attentively." Others translated and explained it so the people could understand the meaning. And the people wept when they heard it, because they grasped the words for the first time. That scene — ordinary people, hearing the words of God in a language they understand and weeping at what they finally grasp — is exactly what Wycliffe and Tyndale wanted to give their own people. The wall had kept it from them for a thousand years. These men tore it down with their lives.

## What you are holding

So pick the book up again, and feel it differently this time.

The reason it is in a language you can read is that men were burned for translating it. The reason it is cheap enough to own three copies is that a printing press once smuggled it past soldiers in sacks of flour. The reason you do not need a priest to tell you what it says is that a plowboy's right to read it for himself was bought at the cost of William Tyndale's life, strangled at a stake in 1536 with a prayer on his lips that his enemies' eyes would be opened. John 1:1 calls Christ himself the Word — the Word who was with God and was God and became flesh and lived among us. The reformers risked everything on the belief that this Word still wanted to be heard, by everyone, in their own tongue, without a wall in the way.

We treat the book as common because it is everywhere. They knew it was the most uncommon thing in the world, and they spent their lives to get it into ordinary hands. The least we can do, having been handed it at no cost, is to open it — and remember, when we do, the price someone paid for the simple, astonishing fact that we can understand the words.
