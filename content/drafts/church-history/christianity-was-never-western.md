Most of us, if we are honest, carry a mental picture of Christianity that goes something like this. It is a Western religion. It started, more or less, in Europe — or it became itself in Europe — and then European and American missionaries carried it out across the world to Africa and Asia and Latin America, where it took root among people who had nothing like it before. The faith flows outward, in this picture, from the West to everyone else. Christianity is the home team's religion, exported.

That picture is not just incomplete. It has the map upside down. Jesus of Nazareth was a brown-skinned Middle Eastern man who never set foot in Europe and would not have recognized a European. The very first person recorded as converting after the day of Pentecost was an African official going home to the African continent. The church was flourishing in Egypt, Syria, North Africa, Ethiopia, and as far east as India and China for centuries before most of Europe had heard the name of Christ, and some of those churches are still there, having outlasted empires. The story of Christianity as a Western faith that went out to the world is a story Europe told about itself during a few centuries when it happened to be powerful. It is not the actual shape of the thing. To understand the people who came before us, we have to recover the truth that the family is far older, far larger, and far more global than the picture lets us see — and always was.

## It began in the wrong place for the story we tell

Start with the obvious thing we manage to forget: the geography of the New Testament is not European. It is the eastern Mediterranean, the Middle East, North Africa. Jesus lived and died in Roman-occupied Judea, in the Middle East. The faith first spread through cities like Jerusalem, Damascus, and Antioch — Antioch being in what is now Turkey, near the Syrian border, and a place that matters enormously to our story.

Acts 11:19–26 records that after persecution scattered the first believers, some of them came to Antioch and began preaching to non-Jews — to Greeks, to Gentiles — and a great number believed. The church there became the launching point for the spread of the faith across the empire, and the text records, almost as an aside, one of the most important sentences in the whole history of the church: "The disciples were first called Christians at Antioch." The name we all carry was first given not in Rome or anywhere in Europe but in a multicultural Middle Eastern city, to a community of Jews and Greeks and others who had become, in the eyes of their neighbors, something new enough to need a new word. The faith was multiethnic from almost the first breath. It did not start as one nation's religion and later open up. It started open.

## The African at the side of the road

Now to the convert. Long before any missionary set out for Europe, before Paul had written most of his letters, the book of Acts records a conversion that should reorder the whole picture in our heads. It is in Acts 8:26–39.

An Ethiopian — a court official of the Kandake, the queen of the Ethiopians, a man in charge of her entire treasury — is traveling home on a desert road, riding in his chariot, reading aloud from the scroll of the prophet Isaiah. He has been to Jerusalem to worship, which tells us he was already drawn to the God of Israel. Philip, one of the early Christians, is sent by the Spirit to run alongside the chariot, and he hears the man reading the passage about a lamb led to the slaughter — and Philip asks him, "Do you understand what you are reading?" The official says the human thing: "How can I, unless someone explains it to me?" And he invites this stranger up into his chariot, and Philip, starting from that very passage of Isaiah, tells him the good news about Jesus. They come to some water. The official says, "Look, here is water. What can stand in the way of my being baptized?" And he is baptized, right there on the road, and goes on his way home to Africa rejoicing.

Sit with what that scene actually is. The first individual conversion narrated in detail after Pentecost is an African — a Black African official from the kingdom that sat south of Egypt — and he takes the faith home with him, to a continent, before the gospel had reached most of the places we now think of as its heartland. Ancient tradition connects this conversion to the deep early roots of Christianity in the Horn of Africa, and to this day Ethiopia has one of the oldest continuous Christian communities on earth, one that predates the conversion of most of Europe by centuries. The faith was in Africa, carried by Africans, from the very beginning of the story. It was never waiting for the West to bring it.

And this fits a pattern, not an exception. North Africa produced some of the greatest minds of the early church — Augustine of Hippo, whom we met as the restless man, was a North African. Egypt gave us the desert monks and the great theological school of Alexandria. The church in Syria, in Persia, and along the trade routes carried the faith east; there were Christian communities in India that trace their founding to the apostle Thomas, and by the 600s Christianity had reached China. While much of Europe was still pagan, the church was already a sprawling, multilingual, multicontinental thing.

## The churches we never learned about

Part of why this comes as a surprise is that the version of church history most of us absorbed, if we absorbed any at all, runs along a single track: Jerusalem to Rome to Europe to America, as though the faith traveled one road westward and the rest was a footnote. But there were other roads, older than the one to Rome, and the churches at the end of them are some of the most remarkable communities the faith ever produced. We were simply never taught they existed.

There was, for centuries, a thriving Christian world to the east of the Roman Empire entirely, in the lands of Persia and beyond — the Church of the East, often called Nestorian, which sent its missionaries not west but east, along the Silk Road. A stone monument unearthed in China, dating to 781, records in Chinese and Syriac that Christian monks had arrived in the Tang capital more than a century earlier and that the emperor had received them. There were bishops in Central Asia. There were Christian communities in India — the Thomas Christians of the southwest coast — who hold to this day that the apostle Thomas himself brought the faith to them, and who were worshiping in their own ancient liturgy long before any European arrived to "discover" them. There were the churches of Egypt, the Copts, who can trace their tradition to the evangelist Mark and who endure as a living community after two thousand years. There were the Armenians, whose kingdom became, around the year 301, the first state in the world to adopt Christianity as its official faith — years before the Roman emperor Constantine had legalized it.

Hold those facts next to the picture we started with. Armenia was officially Christian before Rome tolerated it. Christian monks were preaching in China while most of northern Europe still worshiped the old gods. The faith was being read in Syriac and Coptic and the languages of India and the Persian court while the conversion of the English and the Germans and the Scandinavians lay centuries in the future. The map in our heads has Christianity radiating outward from a Western center to a waiting world. The actual map has it spreading in every direction at once from a Middle Eastern origin, and reaching much of Asia and Africa long before it had finished reaching Europe at all. Many of those eastern and African churches later suffered terribly, shrank under pressure, and dropped out of the story the West chose to tell. They did not drop out of existence. A good number of them are still there, still praying, having outlasted every empire that ever tried to end them.

## Why we forgot

So how did we end up with the map upside down? How did a Middle Eastern faith with deep African and Asian roots come to feel, to so many of us, like a Western possession?

Mostly by an accident of power and timing. For a few centuries — roughly from the rise of European colonial empires through the twentieth century — the West was the dominant force on earth, and during that stretch a great deal of Christian missionary activity did flow outward from Europe and America. That era was real, and it left the impression, especially in the West itself, that Christianity was a Western thing being shared with the world. But that era was a chapter, not the book. It came late, it is already ending, and it sat on top of a far older and more global story that it temporarily obscured.

The scholar Andrew Walls spent his career making this argument and is one of the people most responsible for the recovery of it: that there has never been a single cultural home for Christianity, that the faith has been "translated" into culture after culture from the start, and that its center of gravity has always been on the move. It began in the Middle East, shifted toward the Mediterranean and then Europe, and is now shifting decisively south and east again — back, in a sense, toward where it started.

## The center has already moved

This is where it stops being merely history and becomes the present, and where the historian Philip Jenkins delivered the news the Western church is still absorbing. In his 2002 book *The Next Christendom*, Jenkins documented what the numbers had already made undeniable: the typical Christian in the world today is not a white Westerner. The typical Christian is far more likely to be a Nigerian woman, a Brazilian, a Filipino, a Chinese house-church member, a believer in Kenya or Korea. Christianity is growing fastest, by far, in the global South — in sub-Saharan Africa, in parts of Asia, in Latin America — even as it declines in the Europe that once thought it owned the franchise. The future of the faith, Jenkins argued two decades ago and the data has only confirmed, is southern and global, not Western. In many ways it already is.

There is a piece of this that should especially move anyone who comes to the faith as an outsider, and it comes from the scholar Lamin Sanneh, a Gambian convert from Islam, in his book *Translating the Message*. Sanneh noticed something distinctive about Christianity that the colonial picture obscures. Unlike some religions that bind their sacred text to a single holy language, Christianity has translated itself, from the very beginning, into every tongue it touched. There is no one holy language of Christianity. The early church did not even preserve Jesus' own words in his native Aramaic; it wrote them down in Greek, the common language of ordinary people, so the most people possible could understand. And this translatability, Sanneh argued, is not a weakness but the heart of the thing. A faith that insists on being spoken in your own mother tongue is a faith that takes your culture seriously enough to enter it rather than erase it. Christianity does not have a home culture it asks you to adopt. It comes home to wherever you are. That is why it could be African and Asian and Middle Eastern and European all at once, with no contradiction, from the start.

## The picture at the end of the book

There is an image at the very end of the Bible that I think is the truest portrait of what the church actually is, and it has been there the whole time, waiting for us to notice. In Revelation 7:9, John sees a vision of the final gathering of God's people, and here is what he sees: "a great multitude that no one could count, from every nation, tribe, people and language, standing before the throne and before the Lamb."

Every nation. Every tribe. Every language. That is not a hope that someday Christianity might become diverse if the missionaries do their job. It is a description of what it has been aiming at, and quietly always been, from the first African on the desert road and the first mixed crowd in Antioch who needed a new name. It is worth saying plainly what this means for how we hold the faith now. If the church was global before it was European, and is global again now that the European chapter is closing, then no culture gets to own it and no culture is merely a guest in it. The African believer is not receiving a Western inheritance. The Western believer is not the keeper of a family heirloom that others are lucky to share. We are all latecomers to something older than any of us, all equally at home and equally dependent on a story that began on the wrong continent for our pride. The crowd before the throne is not a Western crowd with guests. It is the whole human family in every color and tongue, and no one language is the holy one, and no one nation is the home team.

So if you come to this faith feeling like an outsider to something that belongs to other people, to a culture or a country or a kind of person you are not — let the actual history correct you. You are not joining someone else's religion. You are coming home to the most global, most translated, most multinational movement in the history of the world, one that was African and Asian and Middle Eastern before it was ever anything else, and that has been waiting, since a man read Isaiah aloud in a chariot on a desert road, to be spoken in your own language too. The map was never the one we drew. The family was always larger than the room we were shown.
