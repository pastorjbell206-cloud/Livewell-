# Essays — Money & Calling

Five essays. The track: deep theology applied to the actual Tuesday-afternoon
of money. Brueggemann on possessions, Wendell Berry on vocation, Wesley on
giving, Berry again on debt. The scholarly tone made practical.

---

## 41. Why the Church Talks About Sex More Than Money

**Slug:** `why-the-church-talks-about-sex-more-than-money`
**Track:** `finances` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** Jesus talked about money more than he talked about sex. Almost twice as much. The American church has reversed this. The reversal is theological corruption, and it is doing visible damage to a generation that watches the church be obsessed with the second most important thing and silent on the first.

**Argument beats:**
1. The data. Count the parables. Count the direct teachings. Money is everywhere in the Gospels; sex is present but much less central. Brueggemann's *Money and Possessions* is the load-bearing text here.
2. The reversal in American evangelicalism. The sermons on sex outnumber the sermons on money by a factor most pastors would not want to count.
3. Why: money is harder. The pastor speaks to a congregation that is mostly upper-middle-class. The pastor depends on those people for the church's budget. Telling them what scripture actually says about money is professionally dangerous in a way that sex is not.
4. What scripture actually says about money: that accumulation is dangerous; that the wealthy are warned more than the poor are; that the early church practiced an economics most American Christians would call socialism if they saw it described in another context; that the love of money is a root of evil; that the rich young ruler walked away.
5. The cost of the reversal: a generation that watches the church talk about sex and stay silent on housing, debt, healthcare, and the structural conditions of poverty. The generation has noticed. The generation has left.
6. The constructive: preach on money. Preach on it pastorally — naming the specific temptations of the specific congregation. Lose some donors. Find some disciples.
7. The verdict: Jesus talked about money more. We should too. The reversal is theological cowardice. Repent of it.

**Opening sketch:** "Count the verses. Jesus on money: about 2,300 verses in the Gospels touch it. Jesus on sex: under five hundred. The American church preaches the inverse. The inversion is not accidental. The inversion is what cowardice looks like when it has had forty years to become liturgy."

**Closing weight:** Money is the thing Jesus talked about most. Money is what the American church talks about least. The mismatch is not theology. The mismatch is professional self-interest dressed up as restraint. The pastors who recover the courage to preach on money will lose congregants, will lose donors, will probably lose their jobs in some cases. They will also recover the New Testament. The trade is worth it. We do not have anything else worth preaching if we cannot preach what Jesus actually preached.

**Sources:** Brueggemann (*Money and Possessions*); Ron Sider (*Rich Christians in an Age of Hunger*); Craig Blomberg (*Neither Poverty Nor Riches*); Andy Crouch (*Playing God*); Wesley's sermon "The Use of Money."

## 42. Debt Is Not Morally Neutral

**Slug:** `debt-is-not-morally-neutral`
**Track:** `finances` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** Debt is one of the most consistently warned-against conditions in scripture. The American Christian assumption that debt is just a tool for financial optimization is a discipleship problem disguised as a financial one.

**Argument beats:**
1. The biblical witness. Proverbs on borrowing and being slave. The Jubilee tradition. The Lord's Prayer's "forgive us our debts" carrying both spiritual and economic meaning.
2. The pastoral reality. The average American Christian household carries enough debt to make most of the New Testament's economic teaching impossible to live out. You cannot tithe meaningfully if you are servicing 20% credit-card interest. You cannot be generous to the poor if your own house is in the red.
3. The structural problem. Not all debt is the borrower's fault. Medical debt, predatory student loans, the housing market — many people are in debt because of conditions they did not choose. Honest pastoral conversation has to acknowledge both individual choices and structural forces.
4. The categories. Mortgage debt for a home you can actually afford — different from credit-card consumer debt; different from student loans for a degree that did not pay; different from medical debt incurred to stay alive.
5. The way out. Slow, costly, often involving family and church community. Most contemporary financial advice is good. Most of it does not have a theology of debt that goes deeper than "be more disciplined." The deeper work is about what we were trying to buy when we took the debt on.
6. The verdict: debt deserves a theology. The theology has consequences. The first consequence is the willingness to live below what we could borrow our way into.

**Closing weight:** The household that owes nothing has freedom the borrower does not. Scripture knew this. The historic church taught it. The American evangelical tradition has, for forty years, treated debt as morally neutral and bowed to the marketing of borrowing as opportunity. The bow was a discipleship failure. The recovery starts at the household level — your household, this year, with the debt you are inside of. The way out is real. The way out is slow. The way out is worth it.

**Sources:** Wendell Berry (essays on debt and the household economy); Dave Ramsey (cite for the practice, with theological reservations); Walter Brueggemann (*Sabbath as Resistance*); Wesley's sermon on money; the Jubilee scholarship of John Howard Yoder.

## 43. The Generosity That Costs You Something

**Slug:** `the-generosity-that-costs-you-something`
**Track:** `finances` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~8 min

**Positioning sentence:** Most contemporary Christian giving does not cost the giver anything meaningful. The widow's mite was meaningful because it was *all she had*. The tithe from disposable income is a different category of act. Recovering the distinction is the work of Christian generosity in our generation.

**Argument beats:**
1. The widow's mite. The two stories side by side: the rich putting in much, and the widow putting in everything. Jesus praised the widow. The framing is uncomfortable.
2. The American Christian giving pattern. Average evangelical giving is about 2.5% of income. Among those giving above 10%, most have incomes that make the 10% painless.
3. The constructive distinction: giving as a percentage of income, vs. giving as a percentage of what you would have otherwise spent on yourself. The second is the harder metric. The second is closer to what scripture is measuring.
4. The lifestyle question. Generosity that costs the giver something requires the giver to *not buy something they could have bought*. The historic Christian tradition called this asceticism — not as world-rejection, but as the practice of letting your wealth be downward-moving instead of upward-moving.
5. The structural piece: where you give matters as much as how much. Local church, yes. Also: organizations that serve the people Jesus said to serve. Also: direct, personal, unaccounted generosity to the people in your life.
6. The verdict: give more. Give costlier. Give in ways your accountant cannot fully track. The accountant tracks the tax-deductible. The cross was not tax-deductible.

**Closing weight:** The widow had two coins. She put both in. The act was foolish by any financial metric and praised by the only metric that finally matters. I am not asking you to put in both coins this week. I am asking you to find the act of giving this year that costs you something you could otherwise have bought. The cost is the offering. The cost is what makes it worship and not philanthropy.

**Sources:** Mark 12:41–44 (the widow's mite); Wesley's "The Use of Money"; Craig Blomberg; Christian Smith (*Passing the Plate*); Andy Crouch (*Playing God*); Tim Keller (*Counterfeit Gods*).

## 44. Vocation Is Not Career

**Slug:** `vocation-is-not-career`
**Track:** `finances` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** Career is what you get paid for. Vocation is what you were made for. The American church has conflated them, and the conflation has produced two generations of Christians who have either deified their careers or felt guilty about their day jobs.

**Argument beats:**
1. The Latin *vocatio* — calling. The historic Christian use covered marriage, parenthood, citizenship, friendship, *and* work. The recent American Protestant tradition has shrunk vocation to mean professional work.
2. The cost: a generation of Christians who think their "real calling" is something other than what they actually do. The dad who feels his job is not his calling because it is not "ministry." The mother who feels her parenting is not her calling because it is not "career."
3. Luther's recovery of vocation. Every honest work is participation in God's care for the world. The plowman is doing God's work. The diaper-changer is doing God's work. The accountant honestly accounting is doing God's work. The pastor preaching is doing God's work *no more* than the others.
4. The two ditches. The careerist ditch: career is identity; work is meaning. The despising-the-work ditch: my real life starts after hours; my day job is the unfortunate condition of being human. Both miss vocation.
5. The constructive: ask the harder questions. What are you actually being formed by? Who is your work serving? What does it cost? What would faithful would look like in the specific work you have, this week?
6. The pastoral piece: most people are not changing jobs. The vocation question for most people is not *should I quit?* It is *how do I do this work as worship?*
7. The verdict: vocation is not career. Career is one site of vocation. The other sites are equally vocational. The Christian who treats career as the only vocation has half a life. The Christian who treats career as a distraction from vocation has half a life. The full life is the integration.

**Closing weight:** You were made for something. What you were made for is bigger than what you get paid for, and is not separable from what you do every weekday. The vocation question is not whether to quit. The vocation question is whether your work is being done in a way that participates in God's care for the world. Most work can be. Most workers do not yet know it can be. The recovery starts in the noticing.

**Sources:** Luther (*The Freedom of a Christian*); Wendell Berry (*The Unsettling of America*; *What Are People For?*); Os Guinness (*The Call*); Tim Keller (*Every Good Endeavor*); Lester DeKoster (*Work: The Meaning of Your Life*).

## 45. The Christian Who Owns a Business

**Slug:** `the-christian-who-owns-a-business`
**Track:** `finances` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** The Christian business owner has a vocational responsibility most evangelical resources do not name. The responsibility is not primarily to evangelize employees. It is to run the business in a way that loves them — including in how you pay them, treat them, and structure their work.

**Argument beats:**
1. The standard Christian business-owner pitch: be ethical, do not lie, give to the church. This is necessary and insufficient.
2. The deeper question: the business itself is a structure of human relationships, and the way you structure it is itself a theological act. Wages, benefits, hours, the dignity of work, the security of the worker — these are the substance of your discipleship as an owner.
3. Catholic Social Teaching has been better at this than evangelicalism has. Rerum Novarum (1891); Centesimus Annus (1991); Laudato Si' (2015). Read them even if you are not Catholic.
4. The specific questions: Do you pay a wage your workers can actually live on, or are you relying on government assistance to subsidize the wages you do not pay? Do your workers have stability, or are you exploiting the gig economy in a way that lets you avoid commitment? Do your prices honor your customers and your suppliers? Is your business one God could be present in without embarrassment?
5. The hard pastoral case: businesses that cannot afford to be this kind of employer. Many small businesses are barely surviving. The question becomes whether the business *can* be one that loves its workers — and if it cannot, whether it should exist at all.
6. The verdict: the Christian business owner has a discipleship task most Christian businesses have not begun. The task is structural, not merely behavioral. Start.

**Closing weight:** Your business is your discipleship. The way you pay the cashier is your worship. The hours you ask of your servers are part of your sanctification. The Christian who owns a business and runs it the same way every other business is run has not yet brought the business inside the gospel. The bringing is the work. The work is yours. Do it.

**Sources:** Pope Leo XIII (*Rerum Novarum*); Pope John Paul II (*Centesimus Annus*); Wendell Berry; Eugene Peterson; Andy Crouch (*Playing God*); Jeff Van Duzer (*Why Business Matters to God*); the work of John Bolt on the Reformed tradition on business.
