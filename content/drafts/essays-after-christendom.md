# Essays — After Christendom Arc

Ten essays in argument order. Together they constitute the lede arc that
defines the brand. Essay 2 ("The God Who Doesn't Bless America") is
already drafted in full (`full-01-...`). The others are outlined here.

The arc moves from diagnosis (essays 1–7) to indictment (8–9) to the only
constructive piece (10). The constructive piece is the hardest one to
write. Don't skip it.

---

## 1. What We Lost When Cultural Christianity Died

**Slug:** `what-we-lost-when-cultural-christianity-died`
**Track:** `after-christendom` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Format:** `article`
**Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** Cultural Christianity is dying, and we are right to grieve — but we are wrong about what we are losing. We are not losing the faith. We are losing the costume.

**Argument outline (beats):**
1. The data. Pew's "religious nones," generational graphs. Open with numbers, get them out of the way fast.
2. The two stories we tell ourselves about the decline: (a) the world hates us; (b) we did this to ourselves. Both are wrong in different ways.
3. The honest third story: what is dying is not Christianity. What is dying is Christendom.
4. What Christendom actually delivered: the assumption that Christian moral vocabulary was the public moral vocabulary; church as default civic institution; Sunday as default sabbath; the wedding as a default Christian ceremony; the funeral as a Christian funeral.
5. What Christendom hid: that most of the people inside it were not, by any serious measurement, disciples. James Davison Hunter is good here.
6. The grief is real. Acknowledge it pastorally without baptizing it.
7. The verdict: we are not losing the faith. We are losing the camouflage the faith hid behind. The faith is going to have to live in the open now. Most of it has never had to do that.

**Opening sketch:** "I have spent my pastoral career watching the slow extraction of a thing that was never actually mine. The thing was Christendom. It was the costume of Christianity worn by a culture that did not, in any operational way, believe what the costume said. It is coming off now. The grief in our institutions is real, but the grief is misdescribed. We think we are losing the faith. What we are losing is the camouflage."

**Key moves:**
- *Not X. Y.:* "We are not losing the faith. We are losing the costume."
- Self-implication: I have grieved this myself, and the grieving was wrong.
- Verdict: The faith will be smaller, quieter, costlier, and probably better.

**Closing weight:** The church that survives the loss of Christendom will be a church that has had to decide whether it actually believes any of this. The deciding will be painful. The deciding is the recovery. Most of us will not make it through. The ones who do will be the church.

**Sources:** James Davison Hunter (*To Change the World*); Charles Taylor (*A Secular Age*, chs. 1, 11); Tom Holland (*Dominion*); Mark Noll (*The Scandal of the Evangelical Mind*); Lesslie Newbigin (*Foolishness to the Greeks*).

## 3. Christian Nationalism Is a Theological Failure, Not a Political One

**Slug:** `christian-nationalism-is-a-theological-failure-not-a-political-one`
**Track:** `after-christendom` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Format:** `article`
**Reading time:** ~12 min

**Positioning sentence:** Christian nationalism is bad theology before it is bad politics. Treating it as politics first lets it survive theological scrutiny — which is the only scrutiny that should disqualify it.

**Argument outline:**
1. Define Christian nationalism honestly — using definitions Christian nationalists would recognize, not strawmen. Cite Stephen Wolfe alongside the critics.
2. The theological failure: the conflation of national identity with redemptive identity. This is not a new heresy. It is an old one — *Reichskirche* under Wilhelm II, *Deutsche Christen* under Hitler, the segregated white evangelicalism of the American South. The pattern is consistent and the verdict on the pattern has always been the same.
3. Why politics-only framing is inadequate: it makes Christian nationalism's defense theological while its critique is merely political. That asymmetry is fatal to the critique.
4. The doctrinal architecture that Christian nationalism violates: the catholicity of the church (every nation, every tongue); the eschatological frame (the Kingdom is not yet); the cross (power-through-suffering, not suffering-around-power).
5. The Confessing Church example. Bonhoeffer at the Bethel Confession. What it cost. What it still costs.
6. Self-implication: I have been silent on this for the wrong reasons. The silence was complicity, even when it felt like prudence.
7. The verdict: Christian nationalism is not Christian. The "Christian" in the name is the part that has failed.

**Opening sketch:** "I have watched, over twenty years, Christian nationalism move from the fringe to the center of American evangelical life. The watching has changed me. It has also taught me something about why the church did not notice — because the church was treating it as a political problem, and Christian nationalism is not, primarily, a political problem. It is a theological failure that has happened to take political form."

**Key moves:**
- *Not X. Y.:* "Christian nationalism is not Christian. The Christian in the name is the part that has failed."
- The historical analogy carries the load: name the Reichskirche; name the segregationist church; do not let the reader pretend this is unprecedented.

**Closing weight:** The Confessing Church was small. It was always going to be small. The faithful church in this season will be smaller, and quieter, and the cost of joining it will not be politics. The cost will be friendships, congregations, livelihoods. The question is whether what we believe is worth that cost. The Confessing Church, in 1934, decided it was.

**Sources:** Bonhoeffer (*Ethics*; *Discipleship*); Karl Barth (*The Barmen Declaration*); Tim Alberta (*The Kingdom, the Power, and the Glory*); Mark Noll (*God and Race in American Politics*); Stephen Wolfe (cite for definitional honesty, not endorsement).

## 4. What the Early Church Knew About Living in Babylon

**Slug:** `what-the-early-church-knew-about-living-in-babylon`
**Track:** `after-christendom` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Format:** `article`
**Reading time:** ~11 min

**Positioning sentence:** The early church — the church of the first three centuries — knew how to be Christian in an empire that did not pretend to be Christian. We have forgotten what they knew. Recovering it is the most urgent reading we can do.

**Argument outline:**
1. Set the scene: AD 60–313. Christians as a small, suspect, often-persecuted minority inside the Roman world. No Constantine. No cathedrals. No public power.
2. What they did *not* do: they did not try to take over the empire. They did not call for Christian senators. They did not organize voting blocs.
3. What they did do: they cared for plague victims (the great Antonine plagues; cite Rodney Stark); they took in abandoned infants; they freed slaves disproportionately; they educated women; they died well in the arenas, and the dying was itself evangelism — Tertullian's *the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the church.*
4. The shape of their public witness: parallel institutions, not insurgent ones. They built things the empire could not build. The empire eventually noticed.
5. The contrast with American Christendom: we have inherited an instinct to seize political power and have lost the instinct to build parallel institutions.
6. What it looks like now: hospitality networks, schools that actually catechize, mutual aid funds, prison visitation, the slow infiltration of caring for the people the empire cannot care for.
7. The verdict: the early church did not save Rome. It outlived Rome. The outliving is the practice.

**Opening sketch:** "For roughly three hundred years, Christianity was illegal somewhere in the Roman Empire most of the time. The Christians did not lobby for legality. They did not vote out the emperor — they could not. They did not, in any sense we would recognize, try to take political power. They did three things instead. They worshipped. They cared for the people the empire did not care for. They were willing to be killed for it. By the third century, the empire had noticed."

**Key moves:**
- The Antonine plague mortality data; Stark's case for Christian disproportionate survival because of their care.
- Tertullian quote in Latin and translation.
- The verdict: parallel institutions, not insurgent ones.

**Closing weight:** The early church had no Caesar. They had Christ. They had each other. They had the city's poor, whom they fed. They outlasted three hundred years of intermittent persecution because they were not, in any meaningful sense, trying to win. They were trying to be faithful. The winning happened anyway, three centuries later, and it was, on balance, probably a worse thing for the church than the persecution had been.

**Sources:** Rodney Stark (*The Rise of Christianity*); Robert Louis Wilken (*The Spirit of Early Christian Thought*); Tertullian (*Apologeticus*); Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History*); Stanley Hauerwas (*Resident Aliens*).

## 5. The Faithful Have Always Been a Minority

**Slug:** `the-faithful-have-always-been-a-minority`
**Track:** `after-christendom` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Format:** `article`
**Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** Christendom was the exception in church history. Minority status is the rule. Reading the Bible with this in mind cures most of the nostalgia.

**Argument outline:**
1. The remnant motif in scripture. Isaiah 10:20–22. Romans 11. The seven thousand who had not bowed to Baal.
2. The historical pattern: the church has been a minority almost everywhere except Christendom. Even within Christendom, the *living* church (the actual disciples) has usually been a minority within the named church.
3. The cure for the lost-majority panic: we never had what we think we had. The mid-twentieth-century American church appeared majoritarian; most of its members were not, by any operational measurement, disciples.
4. What minority church looks like: deeper formation, less assumed knowledge, more explicit commitment, less political leverage, more pastoral cost.
5. The North African church example. The Coptic church. The Chinese house church. The Korean church under Japanese occupation. These are the models, not 1955 American suburban congregationalism.
6. Self-implication: I have spent half my pastoral life mourning a majority that was never real, and I should have spent it pastoring the people in front of me.
7. The verdict: minority is fine. It is more than fine. It is most of what the church has ever been.

**Opening sketch:** "The remnant is the oldest fact in Christian history. The remnant is the church when the church is not pretending. The remnant is what is left when the costume comes off. And the remnant is almost always the size we tell ourselves it never was."

**Closing weight:** The remnant has always been small. The remnant has always been faithful. The remnant has always been hidden inside whatever larger thing called itself the church. Christendom hid the remnant in plain sight, surrounded by what looked like millions of others. The post-Christendom moment is the moment the remnant becomes visible again — not because the remnant has grown, but because the camouflage has fallen away.

**Sources:** Isaiah 10–11; Romans 9–11; Lesslie Newbigin (*The Gospel in a Pluralist Society*); William Cavanaugh (*Migrations of the Holy*); Andrew Walls (*The Missionary Movement in Christian History*).

## 6. What Persecution Actually Looks Like (And This Isn't It)

**Slug:** `what-persecution-actually-looks-like-and-this-isnt-it`
**Track:** `after-christendom` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Format:** `article`
**Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** Calling cultural-defeat persecution is theft from people who are actually being persecuted. Stop.

**Argument outline:**
1. Name actual contemporary persecution. Nigerian Plateau State villages. Pakistani blasphemy laws. Pastor Wang Yi in China. Coptic communities under attack in Egypt. North Korea. Iran.
2. Tell one specific story in detail. Name names. Put it on the page.
3. The American comparison: list the actual hardships American Christians face in 2026. Some are real. None are persecution.
4. Why the inflation matters: it cheapens the word for the people it should still describe; it warps our political theology toward grievance; it makes us bad allies to actually-persecuted Christians elsewhere.
5. Where the line is: persecution is when the state or culture imposes material harm because of the faith. Discomfort, criticism, loss of cultural privilege — those are different things. They are not always pleasant. They are also not persecution.
6. The historical pattern: every time the church has cried persecution when it was merely losing cultural power, it has been wrong, and it has been embarrassed by the next generation.
7. The verdict: stop. Pray for the persecuted. Be in concrete solidarity with them. Stop using their word for your inconvenience.

**Opening sketch:** "Linda was sixty-three when the men came to her village in Plateau State. They killed her husband first. They killed the pastor next. They burned the church with her grandchildren in it. This is what persecution is. We should not use this word for ourselves. We have not earned it. The people who have earned it deserve the word back."

**Key moves:**
- One vivid sustained named example. Not a list. A person.
- *Not X. Y.:* "Persecution is when the state imposes material harm because of the faith. The rest is something else."
- Self-implication: I have used the word wrongly from the pulpit. Repenting publicly here.

**Closing weight:** Pray for the persecuted. Send money to the persecuted. Visit the persecuted. Be friends with the persecuted. And give them back the word we have been using for our discomfort. They paid for that word. They are paying for it still.

**Sources:** Open Doors World Watch List; Voice of the Martyrs; Wang Yi's letters from prison; Tom Doyle (*Killing Christians*).

## 7. When the Church Wins, It Often Loses

**Slug:** `when-the-church-wins-it-often-loses`
**Track:** `after-christendom` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Format:** `article`
**Reading time:** ~11 min

**Positioning sentence:** Political victory has been catastrophic for the church's witness in every age. The pattern is consistent and the lesson has been refused.

**Argument outline:**
1. The Constantinian shift. The instant the church got political legitimacy, the gospel became socially convenient and spiritually expensive.
2. The Holy Roman Empire. The marriage of cross and crown. The Crusades as the inevitable outcome.
3. The Wars of Religion. Westphalia as the cease-fire after a century of Christians killing each other over which Christianity was correct.
4. Anglican establishment and the dissenting tradition's recovery of voluntary church.
5. The American case: the Moral Majority. The religious right. The cost in evangelical credibility with two generations. (Cite Tim Alberta and David Kinnaman here.)
6. The pattern: every time the church gets the political win it asked for, the spiritual cost is greater than the political gain.
7. The verdict: be careful what you ask for. The losses we mourn are usually the wins we should never have wanted.

**Opening sketch:** "Constantine was the worst thing that happened to the Christian church in its first thousand years. He was also one of the best things that happened to it. The fact that both sentences are true is the lesson the church has spent seventeen centuries refusing to learn."

**Closing weight:** The church that learns to lose well — to lose elections, to lose cultural battles, to lose institutional position — is the church that will discover something it has not had since Constantine. The discovery will not be pleasant. It will be the gospel. The gospel does not require political victory. The gospel was first preached, and best preached, by people who had none.

**Sources:** Augustine (*City of God*, esp. Books 18–22); Yoder (*The Politics of Jesus*); Hauerwas (*Resident Aliens*); Roger Olson (*The Story of Christian Theology*); Tim Alberta (*The Kingdom, the Power, and the Glory*); David Kinnaman (*UnChristian*).

## 8. The Strongman Is Not Your Savior

**Slug:** `the-strongman-is-not-your-savior`
**Track:** `after-christendom` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Format:** `article`
**Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** Direct address. Costly. Will lose readers. Should be written anyway.

**Argument outline:**
1. The longing for a savior-figure who will protect the church politically is ancient. Israel asked for a king and got one — and the prophets spent the next four centuries telling Israel what the king cost.
2. Samuel's warning in 1 Samuel 8. Walk through it slowly. Show how every cost Samuel names has now been paid by American evangelicalism.
3. The historical pattern: strongman-as-protector is a deal the church has made many times. Constantine, Wilhelm II, Mussolini, Putin's relationship with the Russian Orthodox Church. The deal always costs the same thing — the gospel itself, traded for protection.
4. The current American iteration. Name it without partisanship: any politician who promises to protect Christianity in exchange for theological compliance is selling the same deal. The deal is bad theology.
5. The cross is not a sword. The cross is what the sword did to Jesus. The Christianity that picks up the sword is no longer carrying the cross.
6. Self-implication: I have wanted to be protected. The wanting was understandable. The wanting was also idolatry.
7. The verdict: refuse the deal. Pay the price. The church that refuses the strongman is the church that has remembered who its actual King is.

**Opening sketch:** "Israel asked for a king because they wanted to be like other nations. Samuel told them what the king would cost. The cost included their sons, their daughters, their fields, their freedom. The king they got was an answered prayer that became a punishment. The American church has prayed the same prayer."

**Closing weight:** The strongman cannot save the church. The strongman cannot even save himself. The King who can save the church already has, and he did it without a sword, without a throne, and without anyone's permission. The deal the strongman offers is the deal Satan offered in the wilderness — all the kingdoms of the world in exchange for one bent knee. The bending is the loss. Jesus refused. We can too.

**Sources:** 1 Samuel 8; Matthew 4:1–11; Mark Noll (*Religion and American Politics*); Andrew Whitehead and Samuel Perry (*Taking America Back for God*); Bonhoeffer's *Letters and Papers from Prison*.

## 9. Voting Is Not Discipleship

**Slug:** `voting-is-not-discipleship`
**Track:** `politics` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Format:** `article`
**Reading time:** ~8 min

**Positioning sentence:** Vote however you want. Just stop calling it discipleship. The confusion is killing us.

**Argument outline:**
1. The category error: a vote and a confession are not the same act, and treating them as the same act has hollowed both.
2. The recent history: how American evangelicalism conflated political and theological identity over the past forty years.
3. The cost to discipleship: when voting is discipleship, sermons become campaign speeches; when sermons become campaign speeches, the pastor's authority becomes political authority, and the political authority is small and contested.
4. The cost to politics: when voting is discipleship, your political opponent is your theological opponent, and conversation across difference becomes impossible.
5. The constructive frame: vote your convictions, including theological ones, *and* refuse to let voting be the whole of your discipleship. Discipleship is bigger than political theology. Discipleship is sabbath, prayer, care for the poor, formation of children, faithfulness in marriage, the long work of becoming the kind of person whose voting matters less than the rest of their life.
6. The verdict: vote. Then close your laptop and go pastor someone.

**Opening sketch:** "An election will not save the church and an election will not destroy it. The church that thinks otherwise has stopped being a church and started being a constituency. The starting was gradual. The stopping was almost complete by the time anyone noticed."

**Closing weight:** Vote. Vote your convictions. Vote with prayer and study and conscience. Vote, and then go pastor someone. Go love your spouse. Go feed the hungry. Go form your children. Go bury the dead. Go open your home to the stranger. Discipleship is what you do on the four years and the three hundred sixty-four other days of every election year. Most of discipleship has nothing to do with voting. The church that remembers this is the church that has chance.

**Sources:** James K.A. Smith (*Awaiting the King*); Stanley Hauerwas; Oliver O'Donovan (*The Desire of the Nations*); Richard Mouw (*Uncommon Decency*).

## 10. After Christendom: A Practical Theology

**Slug:** `after-christendom-a-practical-theology`
**Track:** `after-christendom` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Format:** `article`
**Reading time:** ~14 min (long-read)

**Positioning sentence:** The constructive close of the arc. The only essay that tries to answer the question of what to do. Hardest one to write. Worth writing well.

**Argument outline:**
1. Reframe the moment. We are not at the end. We are at the end of one settlement. The thing that ends after Christendom is the assumption that Christian moral vocabulary will be the public moral vocabulary. The faith itself is fine.
2. The four practices the post-Christendom church recovers. Sabbath. Hospitality. Catechesis. Liturgical formation. Each of these is older than Christendom and has survived Christendom's collapse before.
3. Sabbath: refusing to be ruled by the empire's time. The Christian who rests for twenty-four hours a week is making a political statement before the statement is anything else.
4. Hospitality: care for the people the empire does not care for. The early church did this. The post-Christendom church will do this again. The doing of it is most of the witness.
5. Catechesis: actually teaching the faith. The mid-twentieth-century church could assume cultural Christianity would teach the faith. The post-Christendom church cannot. We have to teach our children — and our adults — the thing the culture used to teach us by default.
6. Liturgical formation: the slow shaping of people by what they do every week. The thin worship of the cultural-Christianity era will not survive. The historic liturgies — recovered, simplified, made indigenous — are the formation the church needs now.
7. Institutions: build parallel ones. Schools that catechize. Hospitals that pray. Mutual aid networks. Funeral practices that look like the resurrection.
8. Sources of hope: name them — the African church, the Asian church, the Hispanic church, the Black church in America. The faith is not dying. The white American settlement of the faith is dying. The faith is fine. The faith is, in fact, growing faster now than in any century since the first.
9. The verdict: post-Christendom is not the end. It is the beginning of the second great expansion of Christianity, and the white American church will be a participant in it only if we stop trying to recover what we lost and start joining what God is already doing.

**Opening sketch:** "I want to end this series the way I have not been allowed to end most of the essays in it: with a list. The list is not a program. It is a description of what is already happening. The post-Christendom church does not need to be invented. It is here. It is small. It is faithful. It is multiethnic, transnational, plural in its expression, ancient in its practices, and it has been here, in the corners, for the entire duration of Christendom — waiting for the costume to come off. The costume is off. The church is in the room. Let me describe it."

**Closing weight:** The end of Christendom is the beginning of the church. This sentence is too clean to bear all the weight I want it to. So let me put it another way. The end of Christendom is the beginning of the only thing the church has ever actually been — a people of God, scattered through every nation, gathered around a table, eating bread that does not belong to Caesar, drinking wine that does not belong to Caesar, learning to live as though Caesar does not get the last word. He does not. He never did. We forgot. We are remembering.

**Sources:** Newbigin (*The Open Secret*; *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society*); Hauerwas + Willimon (*Resident Aliens*); Andy Crouch (*Strong and Weak*); James K.A. Smith (*You Are What You Love*; *Imagining the Kingdom*); Tish Harrison Warren (*Liturgy of the Ordinary*); Andrew Walls; Lamin Sanneh.
