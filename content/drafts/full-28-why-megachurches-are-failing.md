I have watched, over twenty years, three megachurches near me collapse in scandal and seven more shrink in slow decline. The collapses look surprising in the news cycle. They are not surprising. They were structural. The model produces what we are seeing. The model was always going to produce what we are seeing. The only surprise is how long the music kept playing.

I want to write this essay because the collapse of the American megachurch is one of the most consequential institutional shifts of our generation, and the conversation about it has been dominated by hot takes and obituaries that miss the structural argument. The structural argument is the one that matters, and it is the one I want to lay out plainly.

## What the megachurch model actually is

Let me define the model before I critique it. The American megachurch model that emerged in the 1980s and matured through the 2000s has several consistent features.

It centralizes teaching around a charismatic pastor. The pastor is the brand. The pastor's voice is the pulpit. The pastor's writing is the bookstore. The pastor's personality is, in significant respects, the product the church markets to its surrounding community. The teaching ministry of the church is the teaching of one man, broadcast through the auditorium on Sunday morning and through expanding media on every other day of the week.

It relies on volunteer corps for ministry execution. The megachurch's economic model depends on extracting most of its operational labor from volunteer congregants, with a small professional staff providing direction. The model has produced spectacular volunteer-recruitment efficiencies in some cases and spectacular burnout in others, often the same congregation at different points in its cycle.

It markets itself for cultural relevance. The megachurch's growth has historically been driven by sophisticated marketing — branded services, contemporary worship designed to remove the cultural friction the historic church created for unchurched visitors, sermon series designed for accessibility and shareability, facility design that signals contemporary professionalism rather than ecclesial tradition.

It replaces programmatic depth with experiential intensity. The discipleship of the megachurch happens, in significant part, through high-energy weekly worship experiences and brief small-group encounters. The deeper formation work that the historic church has relied on — catechesis, liturgical formation, the integration of spiritual practice into the rhythm of the household — is less central, and is often left to the small groups that meet outside the formal church program.

It positions itself as the local hub of a regional brand. The megachurch is usually one of several campuses, branded under a single name, with the central teaching pastor's content distributed across all campuses through video. The brand is the megachurch. The local campus is a delivery node.

These features are not neutral choices about how to organize a church. They are theological choices that have theological consequences. The consequences have, in our generation, become visible.

## What the model produces

The model produces certain predictable outcomes. I want to name them, because the naming is what the conversation about megachurch decline has often refused to do.

The model produces pastors who are accountable to no one. The charismatic pastor at the center of a megachurch is, in practice, accountable to an elder board that he in significant respects appointed, that depends on his ministry for the church's economic viability, and that has limited interest in challenging the pastor whose performance their salaries depend on. The accountability structures of the historic church — denominational oversight, elder boards with actual independence, congregational polity that limits charismatic-leader autonomy — have been weakened or abandoned in most megachurch contexts. The result is consistent. Charismatic pastors with no real accountability eventually do what charismatic pastors with no real accountability tend to do. The scandals that have shaped the past decade — the Mars Hill collapse, the Willow Creek crisis, the Hillsong abuses, the Independent Fundamental Baptist scandals — are not aberrations. They are the predictable outcomes of accountability structures that were not adequate to the personalities they were supposed to oversee.

The model produces leadership succession crises. The megachurch built around one charismatic personality is, by structural necessity, fragile when that personality is removed. The pastor's departure — by retirement, by scandal, by death — produces an immediate crisis because the church's identity has been bound up with the personality in ways that make succession functionally impossible. The succession crises at major megachurches over the past decade have been almost uniformly painful, and many have resulted in the substantial decline or fragmentation of the congregation in the years following the transition. The historic church has its own succession challenges, but they are nowhere near as severe as the megachurch's, because the historic church's identity is bound up with the tradition and the polity rather than the personality.

The model produces volunteer burnout at scale. The megachurch's economic model requires the extraction of large amounts of volunteer labor. The extraction works for several years in many congregations and then fails — the volunteers burn out, the recruitment pipeline slows, the church has to either raise its giving levels to hire more staff or accept declining quality in ministry execution. Most megachurches have, over time, gradually hired more staff and reduced their reliance on volunteers. The hiring has costs that the original model had been designed to avoid.

The model produces marketing-driven discipleship that does not actually disciple. The discipleship pipeline of the megachurch is, in many cases, broader than it is deep. The number of attendees who can be tracked through entry programs, small groups, and serving opportunities is high. The number of attendees who, after ten years, can articulate Christian theology, hold to historic Christian moral commitments under cultural pressure, and integrate Christian practice into the rhythm of their households is much lower than the original conversion numbers would have predicted. The megachurch has been producing nominal disciples at scale. The next generation is exposing the limits of the production.

The model produces fragmentation when the cultural moment shifts. The megachurch's marketing-driven approach to cultural relevance was effective during a period when American evangelicalism was broadly aligned and the surrounding culture was broadly tolerant. Both conditions have changed. The cultural moment has shifted in ways the marketing approach cannot easily adapt to, and the internal disagreements within evangelicalism — over politics, over race, over sexuality, over the appropriate posture toward the surrounding culture — have produced fragmentation that the megachurch model is poorly equipped to absorb. The fragmentation has produced campus closures, denominational splits, and the gradual decline of multi-site megachurches into more limited regional churches.

## What the smaller faithful churches got right

I want to be careful not to romanticize small churches. Small churches have their own pathologies. Some are unhealthy in the same ways megachurches are unhealthy, just on smaller scale. Some are deeply unhealthy in different ways — territorial pastoral leadership, anti-intellectual fundamentalism, denominational stagnation.

But the smaller faithful churches that have been quietly maintaining their work through the megachurch era have gotten several things right that the megachurch model has gotten wrong. They have maintained accountability structures that actually accountable their leadership. They have built identities not bound up with charismatic personalities, so succession is structurally manageable. They have relied on more sustainable volunteer rhythms. They have maintained deeper catechetical and liturgical work. They have been more honest about the costs of discipleship, and have produced disciples who can hold the faith under cultural pressure.

These churches are what the post-Christendom faithful church will largely look like. Smaller, older, more accountable, more theologically formed, less marketing-driven, less dependent on cultural alignment. The future is not, on the evidence of the past two decades, the megachurch model. The future is the smaller faithful church that the megachurch era largely overlooked.

## What this means for pastors and members

If you are a pastor in a megachurch context, the work is hard. The structures you are inside are not, on the analysis I have offered, sustainable in their current form. The work of reforming them — restoring genuine accountability, reducing personality-centeredness, recovering deeper discipleship — is countercultural inside your own institution, and is professionally costly. Some pastors will do it and will be removed from their churches for doing it. Others will leave to start smaller, more sustainable congregations. Others will stay and watch the slow decline of institutions that cannot reform themselves.

If you are a member of a megachurch, the question to ask is whether the church you are part of is doing the deeper formation work, or whether it is doing entertainment-driven attraction work that will eventually leave you without the spiritual resources to hold your faith through the next decade. The honest assessment is harder than the church will help you make, because the church has structural interests in not having members ask this question. The asking is yours to do.

If you are a young pastor considering whether to plant or join a megachurch-style ministry, the model is not a wise long-term bet. The cultural moment that produced the megachurch boom is gone. The structural problems with the model are now visible. The work that needs doing in the next thirty years is the work of building smaller, more accountable, more theologically formed churches. The work is less glamorous. The work is more sustainable. The work is what the post-Christendom church will be built from.

## The verdict

The American megachurch model was never sustainable. The model contained the seeds of its own collapse from the beginning. The collapse has been gradual and is now becoming visible, and the visibility has produced the conversation we are now having about why so many high-profile megachurches have, in the past decade, collapsed in scandal or shrunk in slow decline.

The collapse is not a tragedy in the simple sense. The collapse is the structural consequence of choices the model made about accountability, personality, marketing, and depth. The choices were made with good intentions in many cases, and produced real ministry impact in many cases. The choices were also producing predictable consequences from the beginning, and the consequences are now arriving.

What replaces the megachurch will be different. It will be smaller. It will be older in its forms. It will be more accountable in its structures. It will be less dependent on cultural alignment and more deeply formed by Christian tradition. It will not produce the megachurch-era growth metrics. It will produce disciples who can carry the faith through whatever comes next. The trade is worth it.

The next essay in this writing project takes up the part of the American church that has, on the whole, been doing the deeper work all along — the Black church, which has held the prophetic and pastoral tradition under conditions far harder than anything most white evangelical institutions have faced. The Black church is, by every honest historical measure, the faithful remnant of American Christianity. The white evangelical institutions that are now collapsing might learn something from the tradition that has been quietly faithful for four hundred years. The learning is overdue.
