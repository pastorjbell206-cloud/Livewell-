The dinner table is where it shows.

You are physically present. Hands around a glass, feet under the table, voice producing words at the appropriate intervals. But you are also somewhere else — with the family whose marriage is in crisis, with the Sunday sermon that is not resolved, with the elder whose email arrived at four o'clock and has not been answered. Your children ask you something and you answer a question they did not ask. Your wife begins a sentence and you finish it with something that was not where she was going. You are there. You are not there.

Ministry is not like other work. A lawyer can close the file and be done with the case, at least for the evening. A surgeon completes the procedure and goes home. But the pastoral relationship does not have a closing ritual. The people you care for carry their grief around the clock, and by a logic of care that is entirely legitimate, their grief is in some sense always your responsibility. The phone could ring at any moment. The crisis does not observe the dinner hour. And the pastor who is genuinely formed by love for the congregation carries that love home with him, and it sits at the table.

This is not a character flaw. It is, in one sense, evidence of the right instincts. The pastor who is indifferent to his congregation when he crosses the threshold of his home has a different problem. But the indiscriminate availability of the pastoral mind — the inability to be fully present in the ordinary and private — is its own failure, and it costs the family a father and a husband in ways that are real even when they are not visible.

I have been told by people who love me that I was not present at important moments. I was there. I was thinking about the church. The testimony of the people who know me best carries more weight than my internal experience of my own availability.

The rhythms that create genuine presence are specific and require protection. The first is a defined ending to the pastoral workday — not a time when all urgent matters are resolved, because that moment never arrives, but a time when the phone goes to voicemail and the laptop closes and the mind is given permission to be in the house instead of in the office. This requires a theological conviction underneath it: that God is capable of caring for the congregation during the hours when the pastor is not available, and that the pastor's constant availability is not what keeps the church alive. That conviction, held seriously, is liberating.

The second rhythm is what I call an arrival practice — something small and specific that marks the transition from the pastoral role to the domestic one. A walk. A changed set of clothes. A deliberate conversation with a child before dinner. The psychological research on cognitive load suggests [cite — confirm] that transitions between roles require some kind of physical signal to be effective. The pastor who walks from his study directly to the dinner table is asking his mind to shift without giving it any reason to. The signal matters.

The third is listening that does not serve the ministry. Most pastors are good listeners in a professional sense — trained, attentive, asking the right questions. But professional listening is always aimed somewhere. Pastoral listening has a purpose. What the family needs is the other kind: unhurried, without agenda, listening that is interested in them for their own sake and not because the information will eventually be useful. My sons have noticed the difference. They know when I am listening because I love them and when I am listening because I am a pastor. The second kind, offered to family, is a form of absence.

None of this is simply willpower. The mind that has been shaped by years of pastoral attentiveness does not redirect itself through effort alone. The rhythms must be built — and then protected, repeatedly, against the genuine claims of the congregation that will always seem more urgent than the ordinary life of the household.

The ordinary life is the life. The family watching it is the congregation that cannot be replaced.

Be there.
