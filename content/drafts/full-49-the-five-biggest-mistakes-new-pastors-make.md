A new pastor arrives with two things in abundance and one thing not at all. He has faith. He has calling. He does not have experience, and there is no way to acquire it except the slow way, in real time, in front of real people, while the stakes are real. That is the hard arithmetic of the first years.

I have watched the same mistakes recur across enough young pastors to believe they are not personal failings. They are predictable. And a predictable mistake is a survivable one, if someone names it before it names you.

**The first is trying to win the room before you have loved it.** The new pastor wants respect, and he wants it now, and he reaches for the fastest lever — a bold change, a sharper sermon, a line drawn. The change may even be right. But a congregation does not grant authority to competence. It grants authority to love, demonstrated over time, in funerals and hospital rooms and the unglamorous attendance of ordinary weeks. Lead the change before you have earned the trust and you will spend the trust you do not yet have. Most new pastors go into debt this way in the first year and spend the next three repaying it.

**The second is preaching to the church you wish you had.** It is easy to preach at the congregation in your head — the one that should be more committed, more theological, more like you. It is harder to preach to the people actually in the chairs, with the marriages they actually have and the doubts they actually carry. The young pastor mistakes the first for prophetic courage. It is usually just impatience wearing prophetic clothes. The pulpit is not where you correct the congregation for failing to be you. It is where you feed the people in front of you what they can actually eat this week.

**The third is confusing being needed with being loved.** A new pastor is needed constantly, and the need is intoxicating. Every call answered, every crisis attended, every gap filled — it feels like love, and it is not. A congregation that needs you will still replace you. A man who builds his identity on being indispensable is building it on sand, and he will know it the first season the calls slow down. Let yourself be needed. Do not let yourself be fed by it.

**The fourth is leading alone.** This is the one that does the most damage, because it hides as virtue. The young pastor believes that carrying it by himself is what strength looks like — that asking for help is the admission of a man not cut out for the work. The opposite is true. The pastors who last are not the ones who needed the least help. They are the ones who built, early and on purpose, the handful of relationships that could hold them: a mentor, a peer who is not in his system, someone who can ask how he actually is. Isolation is not the cost of leadership. It is the thing most likely to end it. [cite — a representative line on counsel, e.g. Proverbs 11:14.]

**The fifth is mistaking the church for the kingdom.** This one comes slowly, and it is the most dangerous because it looks the most like devotion. The new pastor pours everything into his congregation — and then the congregation becomes the whole horizon. His marriage thins. His own walk with God becomes sermon prep. The work of the kingdom quietly shrinks to the size of one building's attendance. A pastor who loses himself in his church has not become more spiritual. He has made a smaller god out of a good thing, and the good thing cannot bear the weight.

None of these are fatal. Every veteran pastor I respect made most of them, survived them, and learned to name them for the men coming up behind. That is most of what mentoring is — not strategy, but warning, handed from someone who paid for the lesson to someone who does not have to.

If you are new, hear the one thing under all five: you cannot shortcut the experience you do not yet have. You can only refuse to walk through it alone. Find the people who have been where you are going. Let them tell you the truth. And give yourself the grace you will need to give your people — the grace of a man who is allowed to be learning in public, because there was never any other way to learn this at all.
