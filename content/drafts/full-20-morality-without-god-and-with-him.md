Some of the most moral people I know are atheists. The argument that you cannot be moral without God is empirically false, theologically lazy, and pastorally damaging. I will not make it here.

What I want to make is a different argument, the argument that has actually mattered to me, about what objective morality — if it exists — has to be grounded in. That argument is harder for both sides of this debate than the easy version, and it is the argument that finally moved me as a young atheist in a way the dismissals of secular morality could not.

I want to lay this out carefully because it is the place where the skeptic and I are closest, and where the gap, when it appears, appears at the level of the underlying metaphysics rather than at the level of behavior. We agree, in many cases, on what is right. We disagree about why what is right is right.

## What the moral experience actually is

I want to start with the phenomenology of moral experience, because the phenomenology is where the argument has to begin.

When I say *the Holocaust was wrong,* I am not making a statement about my preferences. I am not making a statement about the cultural disapproval of my time and place. I am not, even when I say it strongly, making a statement that I take to be merely subjective. I am making a statement that, if pressed, I would defend as objectively true — true regardless of what I think, regardless of what my culture thinks, regardless of what the perpetrators thought. The Holocaust was wrong. The wrongness is not a matter of taste. The wrongness is a feature of the act itself, mind-independent, the way the structural integrity of a bridge is mind-independent. The bridge would fail under load whether or not anyone thought it would. The Holocaust was wrong whether or not anyone judged it to be.

This is the phenomenology. It is not the position of the most rigorous secular philosophers, but it is, in my experience, the position of almost every person who has not yet done philosophy on the question. The strong moral realism is the default. The careful philosophical work either grounds it on something — the question of *what* — or argues that the default is mistaken and that the strong moral realism is a kind of illusion. The second move is hard to live by. The first is, in my reading, hard to ground without God.

I want to be honest about how hard the secular grounding is, because the easy Christian apologetic of *secular morality has no foundation* is the embarrassment I named at the start of this essay, and I do not want to repeat it. The careful secular grounding is harder than the easy apologetic admits, but it is also harder than the careful skeptic sometimes lets on, and the difficulty is the substance of the conversation we should be having.

## What secular grounding tries to do

The strongest contemporary secular accounts of moral realism take several forms. I will summarize the main families.

Evolutionary ethics holds that our moral intuitions are products of evolutionary processes that selected for traits that supported group survival. Empathy, fairness, cooperation, and altruism are explained as adaptations that helped our ancestors thrive in cooperative groups. The account has descriptive power. The account explains why we have the moral intuitions we have. The account does not, however, ground moral *truth*. Evolutionary processes selected for behaviors that survived. The fact that a behavior survived does not make it true. The fact that we feel empathy does not make empathy morally correct in any mind-independent sense. The evolutionary account explains *why* we have moral intuitions; it does not establish that the intuitions track moral facts.

Contractarian accounts hold that morality is the result of a hypothetical or actual social contract — that we are morally obligated to those rules we would all rationally agree to, under appropriate conditions. The account of Rawls in *A Theory of Justice* (1971) is the most famous contemporary version. The account has substantial power for political philosophy. The account does not, however, fully ground the strong moral realism of the phenomenology. It establishes a procedure for arriving at moral rules; it does not establish that the rules track moral truths that exist independently of the procedure.

Intuitionist accounts, often associated with G.E. Moore and the more recent work of Russ Shafer-Landau, hold that we directly perceive moral facts in the way we perceive other features of the world. The account is closer to the phenomenology — it grants the strong moral realism — but it raises difficult metaphysical questions about what kind of facts moral facts are, and how we have epistemic access to them. The intuitionist account works best when it is paired with a metaphysics that makes moral facts possible, and the question of what that metaphysics is becomes the live question.

There are other options — error theories that grant the phenomenology but treat it as a systematic illusion, naturalistic moral realisms that try to identify moral facts with natural facts, constructivist views that root morality in idealized human practical reasoning. Each has serious defenders. None, in my reading, fully accounts for the strong moral realism that almost every person experiences as the default phenomenology of moral life.

The honest secular moral philosopher, like Derek Parfit in *On What Matters*, often grants the difficulty. The grounding problem is real. The strong moral realism is difficult to anchor secularly. Various ingenious attempts have been made; none has settled the question.

## What theistic grounding does

The Christian framework, in its most careful articulations, holds that moral truth is grounded in the character of God. This is not the *divine command theory* in its crudest form — the view that something is right because God commands it. Crude divine command theory has been refuted by Plato's Euthyphro dilemma since the fourth century BC. The careful Christian position holds that the standard of moral goodness is the character of God himself, and that God's commands flow out of that character. The good is not arbitrary. The good is what God is. The commands of God describe the good because God himself is the standard.

This account does several things the secular accounts struggle to do.

It grounds the strong moral realism of the phenomenology. Moral facts are facts about the character of God, and the human capacity to recognize moral truth is, in the Christian framework, a participation in the divine — what the tradition has called the *imago Dei* and the *natural light* of reason. The strong moral realism is what we would expect, if humans are made in the image of a God whose nature is the standard of goodness.

It accounts for the universality of certain moral intuitions across cultures. The basic moral commitments shared by cultures otherwise very different — prohibitions on murder and theft, recognitions of obligations to children and the vulnerable, a sense of the dignity of human persons — are what we would expect, if all humans are made in the image of the same God. They are harder to account for, on the secular view, except as products of common evolutionary heritage; but the common evolutionary heritage does not, on its own, establish moral truth.

It accounts for the seriousness with which we experience conscience. The voice in your head telling you that you have done wrong, even when no one else knows, even when you could escape any consequence — the persistent moral conscience is, on the Christian view, the voice of the moral reality you have been made to participate in. The conscience is data, not noise. The conscience is part of how God draws us toward truth, including the truth about ourselves. The secular account has difficulty explaining why conscience operates so persistently, against self-interest, in conditions where no observer would catch us.

I want to be careful here. The theistic account does not prove that God exists. The theistic account provides a metaphysical framework in which the moral experience we actually have makes sense. The secular accounts can also provide frameworks; the question is which framework is finally more adequate to the data we are trying to explain. My judgment, after twenty years of working on this, is that the theistic framework is more adequate. The skeptic who reads the same considerations and reaches the other conclusion is not making an obvious mistake. We are weighing the same evidence and reading it differently.

## What it means practically

If moral realism is grounded in something beyond us, then the moral life is not just a matter of preference. It is a matter of alignment with a real moral order. The decisions we make have consequences not just in terms of social fallout but in terms of the kind of person we are becoming. The character formation that the historic Christian tradition has cared about — the slow shaping of the affections, the disciplines of the body, the catechesis of the conscience — is not just a religious oddity. It is the appropriate response to the recognition that moral truth is real, that we are made to participate in it, and that we are formed by what we choose to love and what we choose to refuse.

The voice in your head telling you that your life is supposed to be different is data. The moral seriousness you experience when you have wronged someone you love is data. The persistent sense that some acts are not just disliked but actually wrong is data. The Christian framework reads this data as pointing somewhere — pointing, finally, to God himself, who is the source and standard of the good we are reaching for and falling short of. The reading is not coercive. The reading is, in my experience, the one that makes the moral life intelligible.

## The verdict

Morality without God is possible. People without theistic commitments live moral lives — sometimes more moral than the theists who claim moral high ground. The crude apologetic that says otherwise is empirically false and pastorally damaging, and I will not repeat it.

Morality without God is also harder to ground than the casual atheist admits. The strong moral realism that almost every person experiences as the default phenomenology of moral life is difficult to anchor on purely secular foundations, and the honest secular philosophers who have worked on the question generally grant the difficulty. The Christian framework, by contrast, makes the strong moral realism intelligible — provides a metaphysical setting in which moral truth is grounded in the character of God, and human moral experience is a participation in the divine.

This is not a proof. It is a piece of the cumulative case I have been making throughout this series. The cumulative case is the sum of considerations that, in my reading, makes the Christian framework more adequate than the secular alternatives to the actual data of human experience. The case is comparative. The case admits of honest disagreement. The case is, in my judgment, the case that finally tips the question.
