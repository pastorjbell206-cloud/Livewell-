Family worship — the daily practice of prayer and scripture inside the household — is the practice that survives the rest. Most evangelical families have lost it. Recovering it is the highest-leverage parenting work available in our generation, and almost no one is naming it as the highest-leverage work.

I want to write this essay for the Christian parent who has been doing the youth-group circuit and the Christian-school enrollment and is watching her teenagers drift in ways the strategy was supposed to prevent. The strategy was incomplete. The daily household practice of worship is the missing piece, and the missing piece is what the data suggests is doing most of the actual formation work in the families whose children hold the faith into adulthood.

## What family worship actually is

I want to be precise about what I am describing, because the term has been used in different ways and the imprecision has been part of why the practice has been so hard to recover.

Family worship, in the historic Protestant tradition, was the daily practice of the household gathering — usually in the evening, sometimes also in the morning — for scripture reading, prayer, and often the singing of psalms or hymns. The practice was led by the father in most cases, with the mother participating fully and the children present and engaged at age-appropriate levels. The practice took ten to thirty minutes depending on the household and the season. The practice was the spine of household spiritual formation for three centuries of Protestant Christianity.

The practice was widespread. Calvin's Geneva, Cromwell's England, Edwards's New England, and the broad swath of evangelical and reformed households across the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — all assumed family worship as a normal feature of Christian household life. The practice was assumed by pastors in their pastoral counsel, was discussed in catechetical and devotional literature, and was the standard means by which Christian doctrine and Christian piety were transmitted from one generation to the next inside the household.

The practice has nearly disappeared in two generations. The dominant American evangelical household of 2026 does not practice family worship in the historic sense. The household may pray over meals. The household may attend church on Sundays. The household may send children to Christian school or to youth group. The household does not, in most cases, gather daily for the sustained practice of scripture reading and prayer that the historic practice involved.

The loss has been gradual and largely unmourned. The mid-twentieth-century evangelical assumption that catechesis could be outsourced to the church and the school made family worship feel optional. The post-war busyness of American family life made it feel impossible to schedule. The decline of confident pastoral and lay leadership in many households made it feel inappropriate to lead. The cumulative effect has been the near-disappearance of one of the most consistently formative practices in the history of Protestant Christianity.

## Why the loss matters

Christian Smith's research, which I referenced in the essay on raising kids with doubts, found that the most consistent predictor of young-adult religious commitment was the practiced religion of the parents. The practiced religion includes, in the families whose children held the faith into adulthood, regular household religious practice. The regular household practice is not Sunday attendance alone. The regular household practice is what happens in the daily rhythm of the home.

Vern Bengtson's *Families and Faith* (2013) confirms the finding with different methodology. Bengtson tracked families across multiple generations and found that the transmission of religious commitment from parents to children depended substantially on the practiced religious life of the household. The households that had daily religious practices transmitted religious commitment at substantially higher rates than the households that did not. The finding holds across denominations and theological traditions.

The data converges on a simple claim. The household is the basic unit of religious formation in Christianity. The household that has religious practice forms the next generation in the faith. The household that does not have religious practice does not, on average, form the next generation in the faith — regardless of how active the parents are in church, how much money they spend on Christian education, or how much they verbally affirm Christian commitments. The verbal affirmation without the household practice is not sufficient. The data is consistent.

The contemporary American evangelical household has been operating, on the whole, on the assumption that the verbal affirmation plus the institutional involvement would be sufficient. The assumption has been wrong. The next generation has been leaving in numbers the assumption did not predict, and the leaving is, in significant respects, the predictable consequence of the missing household practice.

The recovery of family worship is, on the available evidence, the single most leveraged thing Christian parents can do to support the formation of their children's faith. The leverage is not theoretical. The leverage is documented across multiple research traditions. The leverage is being ignored in most contemporary Christian parenting literature, which has emphasized the institutional pieces over the household practice. The emphasis has been mistaken, and the mistake has had consequences.

## How to start, concretely

I want to give specific practical counsel for the parent who has read this far and is willing to consider beginning a household worship practice. The counsel is concrete because the abstract version is hard to apply.

Start small. The historic family worship practice was typically ten to thirty minutes. The contemporary parent who is starting from nothing should begin with five minutes. The five minutes is a single brief practice — a short scripture reading, a single prayer, the singing of one verse of a hymn or the recitation of the Apostles' Creed or the Lord's Prayer. The five minutes is small enough that the practice can be sustained even on busy or tired evenings. The five minutes is the foundation. The five minutes can grow over time.

Pick a consistent time. The practice should be at the same time most days. Some households do it after dinner. Some do it at bedtime. Some do it at breakfast. The specific time matters less than the consistency. The consistency is what allows the practice to become a habit rather than a sporadic event. The habit is what allows the formation to do its work over years.

Use a simple structure. The historic family worship structure is reproducible. Read a brief passage of scripture, often working through a book over time. Pray briefly — often including thanksgiving, confession, intercession for specific people in the family's life. Sing or recite something familiar. End. The structure does not need to be elaborate. The structure can be the same each evening, and the sameness is part of how the formation accumulates.

Use resources designed for households. The historic resources are available in modern editions. *The Valley of Vision* is a collection of historic Puritan prayers usable in households. *The Lord's Prayer* in the catechetical context is a model. The various edited family-worship guides — Don Whitney's *Family Worship* (2016) is a brief and practical contemporary one — provide structure for parents who do not feel confident leading from nothing. The use of structured resources reduces the barrier to entry and allows the practice to develop confidence over time.

Adapt to the children's ages. The five-minute practice with young children looks different from the fifteen-minute practice with teenagers. The young children's version emphasizes participation through familiar prayers, the singing of simple hymns, and brief scripture passages connected to the children's actual life. The teenagers' version moves into more substantive scripture reading and discussion. The same household rhythm sustains both, with appropriate adaptation across the years.

Refuse to skip. The temptation to skip the practice on busy evenings is real and is what has dissolved most family worship attempts in the contemporary household. The household has to treat the practice as non-negotiable in the same way meals are non-negotiable. The treatment as non-negotiable is what makes the practice last. The treatment as optional is what makes the practice dissolve, regardless of how good the original intentions were.

Survive adolescence. The hardest season for family worship is the early adolescent years, when children are likely to be sullen, busy, and embarrassed by anything that feels different from the surrounding culture. The household that established the practice during the children's younger years has a structural advantage; the practice is already part of the household's identity, and the adolescent resistance is to a piece of household life that the children themselves have known their whole lives. The household that tries to start family worship for the first time when the children are thirteen is doing harder work, but the work is still possible, and the doing is still worth it.

## What this looks like in my own household

I want to be honest. Family worship in our household has not been consistent for the entire twenty-two years of our marriage. We had it strongly in the first five years, lost it for several years during a particularly difficult season of my ministry, recovered it imperfectly during a period of household reflection, lost it again, and have been doing it consistently for the past seven years. The interrupted history is more typical of contemporary American Christian households than the unbroken history that the historic literature describes.

The recovery has been one of the more important things I have done as a father. The children who have grown up under the consistent practice — the youngest three, who were small enough to remember the practice as the household's normal — are noticeably more comfortable with prayer, more conversant with scripture, and more able to integrate Christian categories into their thinking than they would have been without the practice. The two older boys, who lived through the inconsistent years, are doing well in their own faith but have had to do more of the recovery work themselves, partly because the household practice was not as consistent during their formative years.

I am not writing this as someone who has done it perfectly. I am writing as someone who has done it imperfectly across two decades and has seen the difference the practice makes when it is sustained. The seeing is part of why I want to commend the practice to other parents. The commending is part of the pastoral work I owe my readers.

## The verdict

The household is the smallest unit of the church and the most important. The household that prays is the household that forms. The household that does not pray is the household that is being formed by whatever it does instead — and what it does instead is usually screens.

Recover the practice. Do not be embarrassed by simplicity. Five minutes a day, for eighteen years, is two hundred and seventy hours of liturgy. That is more formation than the church can offer in the same period. Use it.

I am writing this for the parent who has read this far and is wondering whether the recovery is possible in her particular household, with her particular schedule, with her particular children. The recovery is possible. The recovery is hard. The recovery is among the most important things you can do for your children's faith. Begin tonight. Five minutes. The recovery starts in particular households on particular evenings. Begin in yours.
