One of the most formative relationships of my ministry has been with a pastor who is wrong about several things I consider important. I mean that warmly, and I suspect he would say the same about me. We disagree about baptism, about how the gifts of the Spirit function, about a few of the harder texts. We have not resolved it. We are not going to. And he has shaped me more than most of the men who agree with me ever have.

This will sound strange in a season that treats every disagreement as a threat and every difference as a line to be defended. We have built a Christian culture that sorts people quickly — our tribe and the other tribe, the sound and the suspect — and then keeps each tribe safely among its own. It feels like faithfulness. It is mostly fear, and it produces pastors who are confident, unchallenged, and slowly narrowing, surrounded only by the echo of their own conclusions.

Here is what the brother who disagrees with me did that the brothers who agree could not. He made me defend what I believe to someone who was not already persuaded — which is the only way you find out whether you hold a conviction or merely inherited a slogan. The doctrines I had only repeated, I now had to actually understand. Some of them got stronger under his pressure. A few of them, honestly, did not survive, and I am better for having lost them. You cannot get that from a room of people nodding. Agreement is comfortable and it is not formative; it leaves your weak places exactly as weak as it found them.

He also showed me the difference between the center and the edge. When you spend all your time among people who share your whole list, every item on the list starts to feel equally central — the cross and the calendar, the resurrection and the worship style, all defended with the same heat. A friendship across real difference forces the question the tribe never asks: *what here is actually essential, and what is just mine?* I came to hold the gospel more firmly and a dozen secondary things more loosely, and that is not a softening. It is the maturity Paul kept pleading for — unity in the essentials, freedom in the rest, charity in all of it.

None of this means truth does not matter or that every disagreement is harmless. Some differences are not secondary, and a man who can no longer tell the center from the edge has not grown generous; he has grown vague. The point is not that the brother and I decided our disagreements did not matter. The point is that we stayed in the room with them — refused to let the things we differ on erase the Lord we share — and discovered that the staying was its own kind of school.

Jesus prayed that we would be one so that the world would believe (John 17:21). He did not pray that we would be uniform. The unity he asked for is harder than uniformity and more credible than it, because uniformity only proves that similar people can get along, while unity across real difference proves that something larger is holding people who, by every natural measure, should have split. The watching world has seen plenty of Christians who agree with each other. It has rarely seen Christians who disagree and will not let go.

So find the brother who is wrong, by your lights, about things that matter to you, and stay close enough to be changed. Let him press your convictions until you know which ones you actually hold. Let him show you where your certainty was tribe and not gospel. You will lose a few things you should lose, hold the central things more firmly, and gain something the agreeable rooms could never give you.

The man who only learns from people who already agree with him has not protected the truth. He has simply stopped finding out whether he has it.
