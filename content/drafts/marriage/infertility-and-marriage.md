Picture a woman sitting in her car in a church parking lot on the second Sunday in May, engine off, trying to decide whether she can go in. Inside, the pastor will ask the mothers to stand. There will be carnations. Her husband is waiting for her to decide, and he's also grieving, though he'd have trouble saying so, and he doesn't know whether to take her hand or give her space. They've been trying for four years. There have been tests, a procedure, a loss early in a pregnancy that almost no one knew about, and a long list of well-meaning comments. Just relax and it will happen. God's timing is perfect. Have you thought about adoption? Everyone they know seems to be pregnant.

Infertility touches far more couples than most churches seem to realize. The World Health Organization reported in April 2023 that roughly one in six adults worldwide, about 17.5 percent, experiences infertility at some point in their lives. Many of those people are sitting in pews on Mother's Day, and many of them are married, and their marriages are carrying a weight that has no name in most congregations.

Couples facing infertility usually hear two kinds of answers. The first comes from a culture that trusts technology: there are treatments, there are clinics, and with enough persistence and money a child can almost always be had. The second comes from well-meaning Christians who either promise that God will give a child if the couple prays with enough faith, or who say nothing at all. The first treats infertility as a technical problem. The second treats it as a test of faith, or as something too awkward to mention. Neither recognizes it for what it is: a real grief, one that strains a marriage in particular ways, and one that the Christian faith, rightly understood, has more to say about than either promise or silence.

This is not medical advice. Couples facing infertility should see a physician, and often a reproductive specialist, and they deserve care from people trained to give it. This essay is about the grief, the marriage and the faith that has to hold both.

## A grief without a funeral

When someone dies, there are rituals. There's a funeral, casseroles, cards, time off work, a recognized period of mourning. Infertility has none of these. There is no single day of loss, no body to bury, often no one who knows. The grief comes in monthly waves, with hope rising and falling on a cycle that never lets it settle. And the thing being grieved, a child who hasn't existed, is hard even for the couple to name.

The grief scholar Kenneth Doka gave this kind of mourning a name in *Disenfranchised Grief* (1989): grief that isn't openly acknowledged, socially supported or publicly mourned. People experiencing disenfranchised grief are grieving losses that others don't recognize as losses, and so they grieve alone. Infertility is a clear example. Friends who would rush to comfort a couple after a death may not realize that a failed treatment or another negative test is a death of a kind. Miscarriage is often the same. The couple loses a child whom few others knew existed, and the world expects them to carry on.

Pauline Boss described a related experience in *Ambiguous Loss* (1999): losses that lack clarity or resolution. Unlike a death, infertility may never have a clear ending. There is always one more treatment, one more month, one more possibility. Couples can spend years suspended between hope and grief, unable to mourn fully because the door hasn't quite closed, and unable to hope freely because it has closed so many times before. That suspension is exhausting in its own way.

Scripture names this particular pain with remarkable precision: "Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but a desire fulfilled is a tree of life" (Proverbs 13:12). Couples facing infertility know the sickness of deferred hope intimately. They live in it month after month. What they need first isn't an answer. It is for someone to recognize that what they are experiencing is grief, and that it deserves to be mourned.

## What Scripture says, and what it doesn't

The Bible is full of women who couldn't have children. Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Hannah, the mother of Samson, Elizabeth. Their stories are often read to infertile couples as encouragement, and they can be. But they can also wound, if read carelessly, and they deserve a closer reading.

Consider Rachel. Her sister Leah had children easily, and Rachel had none. "When Rachel saw that she bore Jacob no children, she envied her sister. She said to Jacob, 'Give me children, or I shall die!' Jacob's anger was kindled against Rachel, and he said, 'Am I in the place of God, who has withheld from you the fruit of the womb?'" (Genesis 30:1–2). That exchange is one of the most honest pictures of an infertile marriage ever written. Rachel's grief has become desperation, and it comes out as demand. Jacob hears the demand as accusation, and his helplessness comes out as anger. He is technically right that he isn't God. He's wrong in everything else. Two people who love each other are wounding each other because neither can fix what is broken.

Then there is Hannah. Her husband Elkanah's other wife, Peninnah, had children and "used to provoke her grievously to irritate her, because the LORD had closed her womb" (1 Samuel 1:6). Hannah wept and would not eat. Elkanah, who loved her, asked, "Hannah, why do you weep? And why do you not eat? And why is your heart sad? Am I not more to you than ten sons?" (1 Samuel 1:8). He meant it kindly. But his question, like so many kind questions asked of infertile women, missed her grief entirely. Then Hannah went to the house of the LORD, where "she was deeply distressed and prayed to the LORD and wept bitterly" (1 Samuel 1:10), her lips moving without sound, and the priest Eli watched her and concluded she was drunk. She had to explain, "I have been pouring out my soul before the LORD" (1 Samuel 1:15). A rival who mocked her, a husband who didn't understand, a religious leader who misread her. Hannah's story contains almost every way the people around an infertile woman can fail her.

Elizabeth, much later, spoke of her childlessness as "my reproach among people" (Luke 1:25). In the ancient world, childlessness was commonly treated as a woman's shame, sometimes as a sign of divine displeasure, and the weight fell almost entirely on her.

These stories are not promises that every infertile couple will conceive. They show something else: that God sees the barren, that their grief is recorded in Scripture without embarrassment, and that God's purposes often move through impossibility. Each of these women did eventually bear a child, and those children mattered to the story of redemption. But reading their stories as a formula, where Hannah prayed hard enough and so you must pray harder, turns grace into a transaction and adds guilt to grief. Hannah's prayer wasn't a technique. It was an outpouring of bitterness before God, and God received it.

The canon doesn't end with those stories. The prophets speak a word to the barren that isn't about conception at all. "Sing, O barren one, who did not bear; break forth into singing and cry aloud, you who have not been in labor! For the children of the desolate one will be more than the children of her who is married, says the LORD" (Isaiah 54:1). Paul later applied that verse to the church, the family God gathers from every nation. And in Isaiah 56, God speaks to those who, by law or by body, could never have children: "let not the eunuch say, 'Behold, I am a dry tree.' For thus says the LORD: 'To the eunuchs who keep my Sabbaths, who choose the things that please me and hold fast my covenant, I will give in my house and within my walls a monument and a name better than sons and daughters; I will give them an everlasting name that shall not be cut off'" (Isaiah 56:3–5). In a world where a person's name and future lived on through children, God promises the childless something better: a place in his house that no genealogy could give.

## How the marriage bears it

Infertility strains marriages in particular ways, and couples are often surprised by how much. They expected to face this together, and they do, but they discover that facing something together doesn't mean experiencing it the same way.

Spouses often grieve on different schedules and in different styles. One may want to talk constantly, while the other retreats into silence or work. One may be ready to stop treatment while the other wants to try again. One may feel the loss sharply each month, while the other feels it as a dull, steady ache that surfaces unexpectedly. It is easy for each to misread the other. The one who talks can seem to the other to be obsessed. The one who's quiet can seem not to care. Like Rachel and Jacob, both can end up wounding each other out of pain.

There is often the question of blame, spoken or unspoken. When the medical cause lies with one spouse, that spouse may carry heavy guilt, feeling that they have deprived their partner of something precious. They may even wonder whether their spouse would be better off with someone else. The other spouse may feel the loss and also feel they're not allowed to grieve it, for fear of making their partner feel worse. When the cause is unknown, both may silently wonder whether it's their fault. None of this is usually said aloud, which only makes it heavier.

Treatment brings its own strain. Intimacy, meant to be a gift between spouses, can become a scheduled medical procedure. The body of one spouse, usually the wife, becomes the site of injections, tests and procedures, while the other stands by feeling useless. Money becomes a constant question, as treatments can be very expensive and often aren't fully covered. This is not financial advice, but couples should talk openly and early about what they can afford and what they're willing to spend, before the pressure of another cycle forces a rushed decision.

And beneath all of this lies a question that many couples are afraid to ask: are we enough? If a child never comes, is this marriage, just the two of us, sufficient? Can we have a full life together without the family we imagined? Elkanah's question, "Am I not more to you than ten sons?", was clumsy, but it touched something real. Many infertile couples have to face, eventually, whether they can love the marriage they have rather than the one they imagined.

Some couples find, in the end, that infertility drew them closer. Having faced something so painful together, and having learned to carry each other through it, they discover a depth in their marriage that they might not otherwise have found. That isn't a guarantee, and it doesn't make the grief worthwhile. But it is a real possibility, and it usually depends on a few things. The couple names the grief to each other, rather than each carrying it alone. They decide together, not separately, how far to pursue treatment and when to stop. They protect some part of their life together from the treatment, so that the marriage doesn't become only a fertility project. They let at least a few trusted people know, so they're not entirely alone. And they seek help, from a counselor who understands infertility, from support groups such as those organized by RESOLVE: The National Infertility Association, and from a church that knows how to grieve with them.

If the grief becomes despair, if a spouse begins to feel that life isn't worth living, that needs to be named immediately. The 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline answers by call or text at 988. Despair isn't a spiritual failure, and it's not a reason for shame. It is a sign that someone needs more help than a spouse alone can give.

## The hard questions of treatment

Modern infertility treatment raises moral questions that earlier generations never faced. On July 25, 1978, Louise Brown was born in England, the first person conceived through in vitro fertilization. In the decades since, IVF and related treatments have become common, and they have brought joy to many couples. They have also raised questions that Christians haven't fully resolved.

The Catholic Church has given the most developed answer. In *Donum Vitae* (1987), the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith taught that IVF is morally wrong even when it uses a married couple's own gametes, because it separates the conception of a child from the act of marital love, and because the process commonly involves the creation and loss of embryos, whom the Church regards as human lives deserving the respect due to persons from conception. The strongest form of this position isn't a rejection of the desire for children. It is a conviction that children should be received as gifts arising from the love of their parents, not produced by technique, and that human life at its earliest stages deserves protection.

The Anglican theologian Oliver O'Donovan articulated a related concern in *Begotten or Made?* (1984). He distinguished between begetting, in which we bring forth someone like ourselves, equal to us, and making, in which we produce something according to our own design and will. He worried that reproductive technology tends to move procreation from the first category into the second, treating children more like products of our choices than like persons we receive. His concern wasn't that infertile couples are wrong to want children, but that the technology carries a logic that can quietly reshape how we think about children themselves.

Many Protestants have come to a different conclusion, and their case deserves the same care. They see no principled objection to medical help that assists a married couple in conceiving their own child, much as medicine assists in other areas of bodily life. The child is still the fruit of the couple's own bodies and their marriage, still received as a gift, and God has always been willing to work through human skill. But many of them share the concern about embryos, and so they accept IVF with limits: creating only as many embryos as the couple is prepared to transfer, refusing to discard or destroy embryos, and considering what will happen to any that remain. Some Christians have taken up embryo adoption, in which remaining embryos are given to other couples to carry and raise. Others, Protestants among them, hold a developmental view: that the early embryo is owed real respect, growing as it develops, but not yet the full protection owed a person, and so they accept practices the more protective views reject. And some Protestants share the Catholic conclusion that IVF should not be used at all. The 2024 ruling of the Alabama Supreme Court that frozen embryos counted as children under the state's wrongful-death law brought many Christians face to face with questions they had not previously considered.

These are serious questions, and Christians of good faith have come to different answers. Couples considering treatment should think them through before they're in a clinic being asked to make decisions under pressure. They should know what they believe about embryos, what they're willing to do and not do, and what they'll do if treatment works in ways they didn't expect. A pastor, a Christian ethicist or a physician who shares their convictions can help, and this site's entry on [IVF and reproductive technology](/ethics/ivf-and-reproductive-technology) sets out each position at greater length. The point isn't to add guilt to people already grieving. It is to make sure that grief doesn't make decisions that faith would later regret.

Adoption deserves its own word. Many couples who face infertility do adopt, and adoption can be a profound good. Russell Moore, in *Adopted for Life* (2009), argues that adoption reflects the gospel itself, since Christians are children of God by adoption. But adoption isn't a cure for infertility, and it should never be offered as one. The grief of infertility doesn't disappear when a child is adopted, and a child should not be asked to heal a loss that came before them. Adoption is its own calling, entered for the child's sake, and it also begins in loss: the child's loss of a first family. Couples should grieve their infertility on its own terms, and then, if they're called to adoption, enter it with open eyes.

## How the church made it worse

The church, sadly, has often added to the grief of infertile couples, and those of us who preach need to own that.

The ancient world tied a person's worth closely to their children. Rome under Augustus passed laws, in 18 BC and again in AD 9, that penalized citizens who remained unmarried or childless, because the empire needed heirs and soldiers. Peter Brown showed in *The Body and Society* (1988) that early Christianity broke with this logic in a striking way. By honoring virginity and widowhood, the early church declared that a person's worth didn't depend on their capacity to bear children for the city or the family. A woman could be a full member of God's people, honored and fruitful, without ever giving birth. The kingdom of God grows by baptism, not by birth.

Much of the modern church has forgotten this. In the late twentieth century, many American churches organized themselves around the family, and the family meant married parents with children. Programs, sermons and ministries were built around that picture. Mother's Day, proclaimed a national observance in 1914, became in many churches an occasion to honor mothers from the pulpit. None of this was meant to wound. But it often made infertile couples, and the unmarried, feel like second-class members of a community whose center of gravity was the nursery. The questions asked in church lobbies, when are you two going to have kids?, were rarely cruel, but they fell on wounds no one could see.

The church also lost the practice of lament. Walter Brueggemann, in *The Message of the Psalms* (1984), described the psalms as moving through orientation, disorientation and new orientation. Many churches sing only psalms of orientation, songs of confidence and praise, and have little language for disorientation, the experience of a world that has come apart. Yet the Psalter is full of it. Psalm 88 ends with no resolution at all: "my companions have become darkness" (Psalm 88:18). Infertile couples need a church that can pray with them in the dark without hurrying them into the light.

What would it look like to do better? It would mean acknowledging infertility and pregnancy loss openly, including in prayer and preaching. It would mean handling Mother's Day with care, honoring mothers while remembering those who long to be mothers, those who have lost children, and those whose mothers hurt them. It would mean training members not to ask intrusive questions about family plans. It would mean honoring the many forms of spiritual parenthood, the mentors, teachers and friends who raise up faith in others. And it would mean making room for lament, so that grieving couples don't have to pretend in order to belong.

## A fruitfulness that doesn't depend on a child

The first command given to human beings was "Be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:28), and for couples facing infertility, that verse can feel like an accusation. But the Bible's idea of fruitfulness is wider than biology. Jesus himself was unmarried and childless, and his life was the most fruitful ever lived. When his mother and brothers came looking for him, he looked around at those sitting with him and said, "Here are my mother and my brothers! For whoever does the will of God, he is my brother and sister and mother" (Mark 3:34–35). The family of God isn't built by birth alone. A marriage without children can still be fruitful in hospitality, in service, in the raising up of faith in others, and in a love that shows the world something of God's faithfulness.

That doesn't make the grief go away, and no one should use it to hurry a couple past their mourning. The Christian hope isn't that grief will be explained, but that it will be redeemed. Nicholas Wolterstorff, writing after the death of his son in *Lament for a Son* (1987), came to see God not as a distant being untouched by loss, but as one who suffers with those who suffer. He didn't find an answer to why. He found that God was with him in the question. Infertile couples may never know why. But they can know that the God of Hannah, who received her bitterness without rebuke, receives theirs too.

Hagar, fleeing into the wilderness, gave God a name: "You are a God of seeing" (Genesis 16:13). For couples whose grief goes unseen by nearly everyone around them, that name matters. God sees what the church misses, what friends don't know, what spouses can't always say to each other.

Paul, writing about the whole creation's longing for redemption, reached for an image that couples facing infertility may find almost unbearable, and also true: "For we know that the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now. And not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:22–23). The redemption of our bodies. That is the Christian hope for bodies that have not worked as they should: not that every longing will be fulfilled in this life, but that the bodies we have now, with all their brokenness, will be raised and made whole.

The woman in the parking lot may or may not go in this Sunday. If she doesn't, she shouldn't be ashamed. If she does, she deserves a church that knows she's there. Either way, she and her husband go home to a marriage that may have to carry this grief for years, or for the rest of their lives. The question they face isn't whether God will give them a child. It's whether they'll let this grief drive them apart, or let it be the thing they carry together, before a God who sees, into a future where bodies and longings alike will be redeemed.
