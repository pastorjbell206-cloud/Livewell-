It's often the small things that undo you. Setting out two cups in the morning out of forty years of habit, and putting one back. Reaching across the bed in the dark. Hearing a story on the radio and turning to tell someone who isn't there. Filling out a form at the doctor's office and stopping at the box marked "marital status," because the word you now have to check is one you've only ever associated with other people, older people, people in the back pews of churches. Widow. Widower.

The funeral was weeks or months ago. The casseroles have stopped coming. Most people have gone back to their lives, and you're discovering that your life has no obvious shape to go back to. The house is the same house, but everything in it has become evidence of someone's absence. And beneath the grief, which is enormous, there are questions you haven't said out loud. Who am I now? Is it all right that I'm still angry, or that I'm not? How long is this supposed to last? And, for some, sooner or later, a question that feels almost shameful to ask: would it be wrong to love someone again?

This essay tries to speak to those questions honestly. It's not medical, legal, or financial advice, and if your grief has brought you to thoughts of ending your life, please call or text 988 now. The claim it makes is this: the death of a husband or wife takes away a person and a self at the same time, and grief is the long work of living with both losses. Scripture gives the widowed a real freedom, to remarry or to remain as they are, and it gives the church a duty toward them that it once kept far better than it does today.

## What the loss of a spouse does

The writer Joan Didion lost her husband, John Gregory Dunne, to a sudden heart attack at their dinner table on December 30, 2003. The book she wrote about the year that followed, *The Year of Magical Thinking* (2005), begins with lines she wrote days after his death: "Life changes fast. Life changes in the instant. You sit down to dinner and life as you know it ends." Didion wrote with the precision of someone who had spent a career refusing sentimentality. What she discovered in grief was that her mind would not accept what she knew to be true. She could not give away her husband's shoes, she wrote, because some part of her believed he would need them when he came back. She called this magical thinking, and she recognized it as irrational even as she kept doing it.

C. S. Lewis, who was a believer, discovered something similar after his wife Joy Davidman died of cancer in July 1960, only a few years after their marriage. The notebook he kept, published the next year under a pseudonym as *A Grief Observed*, records the same disorientation and a faith shaken badly enough that he refused to hide it; [When Grief Doesn't Go Away](/writing/the-weight-that-stays) walks through what he found there.

These two books, one that offers no religious consolation and one written by a Christian, tell the same truth. The death of a spouse is not just the loss of someone you love. It's the loss of the world you shared with them. Grief isn't only sadness. It is fear, confusion, exhaustion, anger, numbness, and sometimes a strange and guilty relief if the dying was long. It affects the body as well as the mind. In a study published in the *British Medical Journal* in 1969, the psychiatrist Colin Murray Parkes and his colleagues found that widowers had a raised death rate in the first months after losing their wives, a finding that gave some medical weight to the old phrase "a broken heart." If you've lost your spouse, take care of your body. See your doctor. Sleep and eat as well as you can, even when you don't want to. These are not unspiritual things. They're part of how you'll survive.

Grief after the loss of a spouse often lasts longer than people expect, and far longer than the culture allows for. If yours has gone on for years and seems to be getting heavier rather than lighter, you're not failing, and you're not alone; the lasting-grief essay linked above speaks to that. But if you find you can't function, can't care for yourself, or can't imagine any future at all, please talk to a doctor or a counselor. Help for that kind of grief exists, and seeking it is not a lack of faith.

## Who you are when half of you is gone

The English word *bereave* comes from an Old English word meaning to rob or deprive. The bereaved have been robbed. What was taken from them is not only a person but, in a real sense, a self.

Genesis says that in marriage "they shall become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). Christians have read that phrase for centuries as a description of something that actually happens between two people over a shared life. After thirty or forty or fifty years, a husband and wife aren't just two people living side by side. They've become a single unit in countless ways: a shared history, a shared set of friends, a division of labor, a way of seeing the world that was formed in conversation with each other. When one of them dies, the survivor loses not only the other person but the self that existed in relation to them. Who am I, if I'm no longer a husband? Who am I, when the person who knew me best is gone?

The sociologist Helena Znaniecka Lopata, in *Widowhood in an American City* (1973), one of the first serious studies of widowhood, described how the death of a husband stripped many women of much of their social role. They had been "wife" in countless settings, and the social world they moved in had been built for couples. After their husbands died, many found that their couple friends drifted away, that they were no longer invited to the same gatherings, and that they had to build a new identity from the ground up. Widowers often face their own version of this, and in some ways a harder one: many men of older generations relied on their wives for their social connections and emotional lives, and find themselves profoundly isolated after their wives die.

That loss of self is part of what makes widowhood so disorienting. Grief is not only mourning the one who died. It's also slowly discovering who you are without them, and that discovery can take years. There's no shortcut, and anyone who tells you to "move on" doesn't understand what has been lost.

## How we lost the forms of mourning

Earlier generations had something we've largely lost: shared forms for mourning, the black clothes and the recognized year that told everyone around a widow that she was grieving and gave her a script to follow. Those forms could be oppressive, especially for women, who bore the heaviest restrictions, and their disappearance was partly a gain in freedom. But by the middle of the twentieth century, as the anthropologist Geoffrey Gorer found in *Death, Grief, and Mourning in Contemporary Britain* (1965), mourning had become a private matter, even a slightly embarrassing one, to be finished quickly and out of sight. What replaced the old forms was not a better set of forms but almost none at all.

Today, most workplaces give a few days of bereavement leave. Friends are attentive for a few weeks. After that, the widowed are often expected to be more or less themselves again. Churches have sometimes made things worse. Funerals have become "celebrations of life," and while there's something right in giving thanks for a life well lived, the celebration can leave no room for the grief. Well-meaning Christians tell the widowed that their spouse is "in a better place," and the implication, never quite spoken, is that the widowed shouldn't be so sad.

Paul didn't say that. He wrote to the church in Thessalonica, "But we do not want you to be uninformed, brothers, about those who are asleep, that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13). He didn't tell them not to grieve. He told them to grieve differently, as people who have hope. Christian grief is still grief. Jesus himself stood at the tomb of his friend Lazarus, knowing he was about to raise him, and wept (John 11:35).

## The widow in Scripture and in the early church

The Bible speaks about widows constantly, and almost always in the same breath as the orphan and the stranger, the people most exposed to harm in the ancient world. God describes himself as their protector: "Father of the fatherless and protector of widows is God in his holy habitation" (Psalm 68:5). "He executes justice for the fatherless and the widow, and loves the sojourner, giving him food and clothing" (Deuteronomy 10:18). Some of Scripture's most memorable figures are widows: Naomi, who returned home empty and bitter; Ruth, who refused to leave her; the widow of Zarephath, who fed Elijah with her last flour and oil; Anna, the prophetess who, after seven years of marriage, lived as a widow until she was eighty-four and spent her days in the temple praying (Luke 2:36–37). When Jesus saw a widow at Nain following the coffin of her only son, "he had compassion on her and said to her, 'Do not weep'" (Luke 7:13), and he gave her son back. From the cross, he made provision for his mother, entrusting her to the beloved disciple (John 19:26–27).

The early church took these texts seriously. One of the first crises recorded in Acts was a complaint that Greek-speaking widows were being neglected in the daily distribution of food (Acts 6:1), and the apostles responded by appointing seven men to make sure it didn't happen again. Paul gave Timothy detailed instructions about the care of widows, beginning with "Honor widows who are truly widows" (1 Timothy 5:3). James made it a test of real religion: "Religion that is pure and undefiled before God the Father is this: to visit orphans and widows in their affliction, and to keep oneself unstained from the world" (James 1:27).

The sociologist Rodney Stark, in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996), argued that the church's care for widows was one of the reasons it grew in the Roman world. Under the marriage laws of Augustus, widows faced penalties if they did not remarry within a set period, because the state wanted them bearing children. Christian widows, Stark argued, were supported by their churches and free to remain unmarried if they chose, and that gave them a security and a dignity that pagan widows often lacked. Whatever one makes of Stark's broader thesis, the picture of the early church is clear: it was a community that noticed its widows, fed them, honored them, and gave them a place.

We don't always do as well. The widowed often drift to the edges of church life, particularly when so much of it is organized around couples and families. The church that once appointed deacons for its widows now sometimes forgets their names after the funeral. I include myself and my fellow pastors in that failure. The early church's example is a standing rebuke.

## Remarriage: what Romans 7:2 and 1 Corinthians 7:39 say

Sooner or later, many widowed people face the question of whether they may, or should, marry again. For some, the question never arises; they know from the start that they won't remarry. For others it comes unexpectedly, sometimes years later, sometimes sooner than they think is decent, and often with a heavy load of guilt.

Scripture's answer is clearer than many people realize. Paul uses marriage as an illustration in Romans 7, in the middle of an argument about the law: "For a married woman is bound by law to her husband while he lives, but if her husband dies she is released from the law of marriage. Accordingly, she will be called an adulteress if she lives with another man while her husband is alive. But if her husband dies, she is free from that law, and if she marries another man she is not an adulteress" (Romans 7:2–3). Paul's real subject here is the believer's relationship to the law of Moses, and he isn't writing a guide to remarriage. But his illustration works only because it states something his readers already took for granted: death ends the marriage bond, and a widowed person who marries again does nothing wrong.

He says the same thing directly in 1 Corinthians 7:39: "A wife is bound to her husband as long as he lives. But if her husband dies, she is free to be married to whom she wishes, only in the Lord." The freedom is real and it's broad: "to whom she wishes." The one condition, "only in the Lord," means that a Christian should marry another believer. And Paul adds his own opinion in the next verse: "Yet in my judgment she is happier if she remains as she is" (1 Corinthians 7:40). Elsewhere, writing to Timothy about younger widows, he goes the other direction: "So I would have younger widows marry, bear children, manage their households, and give the adversary no occasion for slander" (1 Timothy 5:14).

The church has not always been at peace with this freedom. Tertullian, writing to his own wife around the year 200, urged her not to remarry if he died first, and in later years, after joining the rigorist Montanist movement, he condemned second marriages outright. Others in the early centuries viewed remarriage with suspicion. But the wider church rejected the idea that a second marriage after a spouse's death was sinful. The Council of Nicaea in 325, in its eighth canon, required members of the rigorist Novatianist sect who wished to return to the church to accept communion with those who had married twice. Augustine, in his treatise *On the Good of Widowhood* (around 414), argued that remaining a widow was a higher calling for those able to receive it, but insisted that a second marriage was not a sin.

So there are two honorable paths, and both have strong defenders. The case for remaining single rests on Paul's own judgment that the widow may be "happier" as she is, on his teaching that the unmarried can give themselves to the Lord with undivided attention (1 Corinthians 7:32–35), on the example of Anna, and on the sense many widowed people have that their first marriage was complete and that they have no desire for another. The case for remarrying rests on Paul's explicit freedom, on his counsel to younger widows, and on the words God spoke at the beginning: "It is not good that the man should be alone" (Genesis 2:18). Both are faithful. Neither is second best. This is one of the questions that Scripture itself leaves open, and the widowed person who chooses either path should be able to do so without judgment from the church.

There's another fear that sometimes hides underneath the question. Many widowed people wonder what remarriage would mean in the resurrection. Would they be betraying their first spouse? Whose husband or wife would they be? The Sadducees once put a version of this question to Jesus, describing a woman who had been married to seven brothers in turn and asking whose wife she would be in the resurrection. Jesus answered, "For in the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven" (Matthew 22:30). He was not saying that the love between spouses will be erased. He was saying that the resurrection life is larger than marriage, not smaller, and that the question assumes a scarcity that won't exist there.

Practically, remarriage raises questions that deserve care. There's no rule about timing, but many people find it wise not to make major decisions in the first year of grief, when the loneliness is sharpest and judgment is least steady. Adult children may have strong feelings, and those feelings deserve a hearing even if they don't get a veto. And remarriage can affect pensions, survivor benefits, and inheritance in ways worth understanding before the wedding; talk to an attorney and a financial adviser you trust. This is not legal or financial advice.

And there's the guilt. Many widowed people who fall in love again feel that they're betraying the one who died. Grief researchers have moved away from the older idea that healthy grieving means severing ties with the dead; the continuing-bonds work of Dennis Klass and his colleagues (1996) argued that the bereaved commonly and healthily carry the dead with them into the rest of life. A second marriage doesn't erase the first. It includes it, as part of who the widowed person has become. Love is not a fixed quantity that must be taken from one person to give to another. A parent who has a second child doesn't love the first one less.

## Grieving toward the resurrection

Christian hope for the widowed is not the hope that the grief will go away quickly, or that it won't be so bad, or that the dead are simply watching from somewhere nearby. It's the hope of the resurrection. Paul continued his word to the grieving Thessalonians: "For since we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so, through Jesus, God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep" (1 Thessalonians 4:14). The one you lost is not lost to God. And the last book of the Bible ends with a promise that speaks directly to every widow and widower: "He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away" (Revelation 21:4).

That promise doesn't make the second cup in the cupboard any easier to put away tomorrow morning. It doesn't fill the empty side of the bed or answer the question of who you are now. Those things will take time, and some of them will never be fully answered in this life. But the promise does change what the grief is. It means the grief is not the last word. It means the one you loved is held by the God who raised Jesus from the dead. And it means that whatever you choose, to remain as you are or to love again, you're choosing it in the light of a morning that hasn't come yet, when the former things will have passed away and every tear, including the ones you're crying now, will be wiped away by the hand of God himself.

Until then, the work is ordinary and slow. Get up. Set out one cup. Let someone from church sit with you. Say the name of the one you lost, out loud, to people who will hear it. And let the God who calls himself the protector of widows be exactly that, for as long as it takes.
