The question comes on an ordinary afternoon, and it comes from the person who has shared your bed for fifty years. "Who are you?" Or it comes more gently and more painfully: "When is my husband coming home?" asked of the husband who is standing in the kitchen. Or it doesn't come as a question at all but as a slow fading, the stories that repeat, the names that slip, the checkbook that stops balancing, the drive home from church that suddenly becomes a drive to nowhere. You know what's happening before the doctor says it. And when the doctor does say it, a word like Alzheimer's or dementia settles over the rest of your life together.

If you're reading this as a spouse who has become a caregiver, you probably don't have much time to read. So here are the most useful things first. The Alzheimer's Association runs a 24-hour helpline at 1-800-272-3900, staffed by people who can answer questions about the disease, about care, and about the decisions ahead. The Eldercare Locator, at 1-800-677-1116, can connect you with services in your area. If you are exhausted to the point of despair, or having thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988. And nothing on this page is medical advice. Your spouse's doctors, and the professionals who specialize in dementia care, are the ones to guide the medical decisions.

What this essay offers is something else: an attempt to answer the question underneath all the practical ones. What does marriage mean when one partner can no longer remember it? What did the vows promise, and what do they still ask? The claim here is that our culture quietly believes a person is their memory, so dementia looks like the end of the person and the end of the marriage. The Christian faith says something different. We are held in God's memory, not only our own. And the vow asks for faithfulness, which is not the same as doing everything alone.

## Marriage in the long afternoon

Before the disease, there was simply old age, and old age has always been part of what the vows anticipated. The marriage service in the *Book of Common Prayer* of 1662, whose words still echo at many weddings, has the couple promise "to have and to hold from this day forward, for better for worse, for richer for poorer, in sickness and in health, to love and to cherish, till death us do part." Most couples say those words when they're young and healthy, and the phrase "in sickness and in health" sounds like a formality. By the time the sickness comes, the young couple has become an old one, and they discover what they meant.

Old marriages have a particular dignity. Two people who have grown old together carry decades of shared memory, a private language, habits of care built over a lifetime. They also carry failing bodies, and more and more of the marriage becomes a matter of one caring for the other: the arm offered on the stairs, the pills sorted into the weekly case, the appointment remembered. Jesus spoke to Peter about the shape of old age in words that apply to every aging body: "Truly, truly, I say to you, when you were young, you used to dress yourself and walk wherever you wanted, but when you are old, you will stretch out your hands, and another will dress you and carry you where you do not want to go" (John 21:18). John tells us Jesus was speaking of the kind of death Peter would die. But the image is true of age itself. Everyone who lives long enough will eventually be dressed by another.

Scripture's prayers for old age are prayers for exactly this season. "Do not cast me off in the time of old age; forsake me not when my strength is spent" (Psalm 71:9). And God's promise to his people answers the prayer: "even to your old age I am he, and to gray hairs I will carry you. I have made, and I will bear; I will carry and will save" (Isaiah 46:4). The one who is being cared for by a spouse is also being carried by God, and so is the spouse who is doing the caring.

## The fear underneath: that a person is their memory

Dementia is uniquely frightening because it seems to attack the person, not just the body. A spouse with cancer is still themselves. A spouse with advanced dementia may not know their own name, and it's natural to wonder whether the person you married is still there at all.

That fear has deep roots in modern thought. The philosopher John Locke, in the second edition of his *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1694), argued that personal identity consists in consciousness, and that a person's identity extends only as far back as their consciousness can reach, which in practice means as far as their memory reaches. On Locke's view, if memory is lost, the person, in the sense that matters, is lost with it. Locke's theory has been debated ever since, but its instinct has become part of how many people think. We are what we remember.

The ethicist Stephen Post, in *The Moral Challenge of Alzheimer Disease* (1995), argued that modern Western culture is "hypercognitive": it values people according to their rationality, their memory, and their mental capacities, and it tends to see those who lose these capacities as diminished, as less than full persons. In such a culture, dementia is not just an illness. It's a kind of erasure.

The strongest philosophical version of this view came from Ronald Dworkin in *Life's Dominion* (1993). Dworkin distinguished between a person's experiential interests, the pleasures and pains of the present moment, and their critical interests, the convictions and commitments that give a life its shape. He argued that when dementia has destroyed a person's capacity for critical interests, the earlier, competent self's wishes should govern decisions about the later self's care, a principle he called precedent autonomy. He discussed the case of a woman called Margo, described by a medical student who visited her, who seemed content in her dementia, painting the same picture day after day. Dworkin's argument implied that if the earlier Margo had wished not to be kept alive in such a state, her earlier wishes should prevail over the apparent contentment of the later Margo. The argument deserves to be taken seriously; it's an attempt to respect who a person was over a lifetime, rather than reducing them to their present condition. But the law professor and ethicist Rebecca Dresser, responding to Dworkin in 1995, raised the objection that has troubled many since: the person with dementia is still a person with present interests, and a theory that lets a past self overrule the present one can end up treating a living, experiencing human being as though she were already gone.

The same premise can surface far from a philosopher's study. In September 2011 a caller to *The 700 Club* asked Pat Robertson about a friend whose wife had Alzheimer's and no longer recognized him, and who had begun seeing another woman. Robertson answered that the man, if he was going to do that, should divorce his wife and start over, while making sure she had custodial care, and he described the disease as a kind of death. He added that he would not lay a guilt trip on someone who made that choice. Within days he said his remarks had been misinterpreted. They were widely criticized, including by many Christians, and rightly. Yet the broadcaster and the philosopher, for all their differences, were leaning on the same assumption: that as memory goes, the person, and with the person the claims of love, go too.

Against this, the psychologist Tom Kitwood argued in *Dementia Reconsidered* (1997) that personhood is not located only inside the individual's mind but is sustained in relationships. People with dementia, he argued, often declined faster when those around them treated them as objects, talking over them, ignoring them, rushing them. He called this a "malignant social psychology." When they were treated as persons, addressed, touched, included, and respected, they often did better. The person is not simply what they can remember. The person is also who they are in relation to others.

The theologian John Swinton, in *Dementia: Living in the Memories of God* (2012), went further. Christian faith, he argued, has never located human identity primarily in our own memory. We are who we are because God remembers us. When we forget, even when we forget ourselves, we are not forgotten. The thief on the cross made one request of Jesus: "Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom" (Luke 23:42). He did not ask to be able to remember. He asked to be remembered. And God's promise through Isaiah is exactly this: "Can a woman forget her nursing child, that she should have no compassion on the son of her womb? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you. Behold, I have engraved you on the palms of my hands" (Isaiah 49:15–16).

That is the distinction this whole question turns on. If a person is their memory, then dementia ends the person and, with the person, the marriage. If a person is held in God's memory, then the person remains, however much is lost, and the marriage remains too, even when only one of the two can remember it.

## What the vow asks, and what it doesn't

In 1990, Robertson McQuilkin resigned as president of Columbia Bible College and Seminary in South Carolina to care full time for his wife Muriel, who had Alzheimer's disease. He explained his decision publicly, first in an article in *Christianity Today* that October and later in his book *A Promise Kept* (1998). He had promised, he said, to care for her in sickness and in health, till death. And beyond the promise, he spoke of fairness: she had cared for him for decades, and if he cared for her for as long, he would still be in her debt. He described caring for her not as a burden he was forced to bear but as something he was glad to do. His story has moved many people, and it should. It's a picture of the vow kept at great cost, with love.

But McQuilkin's story can also be misused, and caregivers need to hear that too. Not every spouse can do what he did. Not every spouse has the health, the resources, or the kind of illness to contend with that makes full-time home care possible. Some forms of dementia bring aggression, wandering, sleeplessness, and medical needs that no single aging person can safely manage. And the caregiver's own health is at stake. In 1999, the researchers Richard Schulz and Scott Beach published a study in the *Journal of the American Medical Association* finding that elderly spouses who were caring for a disabled partner and reported strain from it had a significantly higher risk of death over the following four years than spouses who were not caregivers. The vow doesn't require you to die before your spouse does.

So what does the vow ask? It asks for faithfulness: that you won't abandon your spouse, that you'll keep loving and honoring them, that you'll make sure they're cared for, protected, and treated with dignity, and that you'll stay married to them until death. It does not ask you to do all the caring with your own hands, to refuse help, to sacrifice your health, or to keep your spouse at home when their needs have outgrown what home can safely provide.

Paul offers a distinction that helps here. In Galatians he writes, "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2), and then, three verses later, "For each will have to bear his own load" (Galatians 6:5). The two Greek words are different. The first is a heavy weight, too much for one person to carry. The second is closer to a pack, the ordinary load each person carries for themselves. Caring for a spouse with advanced dementia is the first kind. It is not meant to be carried alone. The body of Christ exists, in part, so that burdens like this one can be shared.

Families often agonize over the decision to move a spouse into memory care. Some have made promises never to "put them in a home," and some come from cultures and families where such a move would feel like betrayal. Those instincts come from love, and they deserve respect. There are good reasons to keep a spouse at home as long as it's safe and sustainable, and families who do so at great cost are doing something honorable. But there are also good reasons to move a spouse into skilled care, and a move made for the spouse's safety and the caregiver's survival is not abandonment. Faithfulness can look like driving to the care facility every day, holding a hand, singing an old hymn, and advocating for good care. The promise was to love and to cherish. It can be kept in a memory-care unit. These are decisions to make with your spouse's doctors and care team, not from a page like this one. ## The hard things no one says out loud

Caregivers carry feelings they're often ashamed of, and it helps to name them.

There is grief, and it is a strange kind of grief because the person you're grieving is still alive. The family therapist Pauline Boss called this "ambiguous loss" in her book of that name (1999): a loss that has no clear boundary, where someone is physically present but psychologically absent. Ambiguous loss is hard to mourn, because there's no funeral, no clear moment of ending, and no social permission to grieve. You can find yourself grieving your spouse while bathing them. That's not a failure of love. It is love, in one of its hardest forms.

There is anger, sometimes at the disease, sometimes at God, sometimes at your spouse, even though you know they can't help it. There is exhaustion so deep that it frightens you. There may be moments when you're afraid of what you might say or do. If you ever feel close to hurting your spouse, or yourself, that is a sign that you need help immediately, not a sign that you're a bad person. Call the Alzheimer's Association helpline, call your spouse's doctor, call 988, or, if anyone is in danger, call 911. And if your spouse's illness has made them physically aggressive toward you, which can happen, your safety matters too. Their disease doesn't make them morally responsible in the way a healthy abuser is, but it doesn't make you obligated to be harmed.

And there is loneliness. Many caregivers are desperately lonely, having lost the companionship of their spouse without losing the marriage. Some find themselves drawn to a friendship with someone else, sometimes another caregiver in a support group or an old friend, and some begin to wonder whether a new romantic relationship would be wrong when the spouse no longer knows them. This is one of the hardest questions caregivers face, and it deserves an honest answer rather than a quick one. The strongest case for such a relationship is that the marriage has, in every functional sense, already ended, and that God does not require a person to live out their remaining years in total loneliness. That argument comes from real pain, and it shouldn't be met with contempt. But Scripture is clear that the marriage bond lasts as long as both spouses live (Romans 7:2; 1 Corinthians 7:39), and the vow was made precisely for the case in which one spouse can no longer return what the other gives. This essay lands where the vow does: the covenant holds until death. That's a heavy thing to say to someone who is lonely, and it has to be said with grief, not judgment. It also places a real obligation on the church. If the vow asks a caregiver to remain faithful, the church must make sure that faithfulness is not the same as isolation. Friendship, practical help, and ordinary companionship are what the lonely caregiver needs, and the church can offer them.

## The church's part

The church has not always done well here, and it's worth saying so plainly. People with dementia often simply disappear from church life. They stop coming because the service confuses them or because getting them there is too hard. Their caregivers stop coming too. The church roll keeps their names, but no one visits. We tell ourselves we don't know what to say, or that they won't remember our visit anyway, and we stay away. That's a failure of love, and pastors are not exempt from it.

Swinton and others who work with people with dementia have noticed something remarkable. People who can no longer remember their children's names can often still sing the hymns they learned as children, pray the Lord's Prayer, or respond to the words of a familiar liturgy. What was laid down deep by long practice remains when newer memories are gone. The church's worship, repeated week after week and year after year, becomes a kind of memory held in the body. That's a reason for the church to keep bringing worship to people with dementia, in care facilities and in homes, and to keep including them in its life.

It's also a reason for the church to share the caregiver's burden in practical ways: sitting with the spouse so the caregiver can rest, bringing meals, helping with the paperwork, driving to appointments, and simply visiting. The law of Christ, Paul said, is fulfilled by bearing one another's burdens. Few burdens in a congregation are heavier than this one.

## Fully known

There is a verse in Paul's great chapter on love that speaks to this whole season with unusual precision: "For now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known" (1 Corinthians 13:12). The person with dementia knows only in part, and the part grows smaller. But they are fully known. God has not forgotten a single day of their life, a single prayer they prayed, a single act of love they gave. What they can no longer hold, he holds.

And the Christian hope is not that the mind will be preserved but that the whole person will be raised. "What is sown is perishable; what is raised is imperishable. It is sown in dishonor; it is raised in glory. It is sown in weakness; it is raised in power" (1 Corinthians 15:42–43). The body that is being dressed and fed and bathed by another, the mind that no longer knows its own story, is being sown in weakness. It will be raised. The resurrection is not the escape of a soul from a failing brain. It's the restoration of the whole person, memory and all, by the God who never forgot them.

Until then, there is the ordinary faithfulness of a spouse who keeps coming back. The one you married may not know your name today. They may ask who you are, or call you by someone else's name, or simply stare past you. You are still keeping a promise, and it is still being kept for you, by the one who is remembering both of you all the way home. When you drive back from the care facility tonight, or turn off the light after the last bewildered question of the day, the marriage is not over. It is simply being carried, for now, by the one who can still remember it, and by the God who has never forgotten either of you.
