The first night is often the strangest. The car is empty, the dorm room or the first apartment has been set up, the goodbye has been said in a parking lot, and two people drive home to a house that is suddenly too quiet. The bedroom down the hall still has the posters on the wall. There are too many chairs at the table. Dinner, which used to be a negotiation among schedules and appetites, is now a meal for two, and somewhere between the salad and the dishes one of you realizes that you've been talking about the kids for twenty minutes because you aren't sure what else to talk about.

Then the question arrives, sometimes spoken and more often not. Who are we now?

For some couples it's a pleasant question, the beginning of a season they've looked forward to. For others it's frightening, because the honest answer seems to be that they don't know. The children were the project, the calendar, the shared subject, the reason for the budget and the vacations and the arguments. Now the project has moved out, and what's left is two people who haven't been alone together, really alone, in two decades.

The claim this essay makes is that the empty nest doesn't create that question. It reveals the answer that was already there. It shows whether a marriage has been a union of two people or a partnership in a project, and it gives both kinds of marriage a chance to become something new. And it's worth knowing at the start that Scripture planned for this leaving long before we learned to dread it.

## A newer stage of life than it looks

The empty nest feels like a timeless part of family life, but in its modern form it's surprisingly recent. In 1900, life expectancy at birth in the United States was about 47 years; by 2019 it was about 79. The early figure is pulled down by the many children who died young, so it doesn't mean that most adults died in their forties. But it does mean that couples in earlier generations, who typically had more children over a longer span of years and lived shorter lives, often had little time together after the last child left home. The long post-parental marriage, twenty or thirty years of two people alone together after the children are grown, is largely a development of the last century.

Other changes shaped it too. The historian Stephanie Coontz, in *Marriage, a History* (2005), traced how marriage in the West shifted over the last few centuries from an economic and social arrangement, embedded in extended families and household economies, toward a relationship centered on love and personal satisfaction. That shift made marriage potentially richer and certainly more fragile, because a marriage built on love has to keep finding reasons to love.

And children changed. The sociologist Viviana Zelizer, in *Pricing the Priceless Child* (1985), described how, between roughly 1870 and 1930, American children went from being economically useful members of the household, whose labor contributed to the family, to being economically worthless but emotionally priceless. Children stopped working and started being invested in. Annette Lareau, in *Unequal Childhoods* (2003), described the style of middle-class parenting she called concerted cultivation: the intensive, scheduled development of a child's talents through activities, lessons, and constant adult involvement. Many parents of the last generation lived exactly this way, and their marriages were organized around it. The calendar was the children's calendar. The conversation was about the children's needs. The couple became, in effect, the management team for a demanding and beloved project.

When the project ends, the management team has to find out whether it was ever anything more. For some, the discovery is hard. The sociologists Susan Brown and I-Fen Lin, in a 2012 paper they called "The Gray Divorce Revolution," found that the divorce rate among Americans over fifty roughly doubled between 1990 and 2010, even as the overall divorce rate held roughly steady. Some of those marriages had been held together by the children, and when the children left, there was nothing left to hold.

But that's not the whole story, and it's important not to tell it as though it were. In a 2008 study following a group of women over many years, the psychologists Sara Gorchoff, Oliver John, and Ravenna Helson found that marital satisfaction tended to rise, not fall, after the children left home, and that the increase came not from spending more time together but from enjoying the time they had more. For many couples, the empty nest is a relief and a renewal. The quiet house gives back something the busy years took away.

Both truths belong together. The empty nest is a hinge. It can swing a marriage toward renewal or toward dissolution, and what decides the direction is usually what the marriage was built on.

## The marriage that was built around the children

It would be easy to blame the culture for all of this, and the culture deserves some of the blame. But the church has to take its share, and so do those of us who lead it.

For a generation, many churches made the family the center of discipleship. Ministries were organized by life stage, sermons were full of parenting advice, and the implicit message was that the good Christian life was a well-run household with children who stayed in church. Much of that came from good motives, a desire to strengthen families in a culture that seemed to be pulling them apart. But it had a cost. It trained parents to find their identity, and even their faithfulness, in their children. It made the children the measure of the marriage's success. And it left couples whose children had grown up, and especially couples whose children had wandered from the faith, with a quiet sense that they no longer had a place, or that they had failed at the one thing that counted.

Jesus did not share that view of the family. When his mother and brothers came looking for him, he asked, "Who are my mother and my brothers?" and answered by pointing to those seated around him: "For whoever does the will of God, he is my brother and sister and mother" (Mark 3:33, 35). And he spoke even more sharply: "If anyone comes to me and does not hate his own father and mother and wife and children and brothers and sisters, yes, and even his own life, he cannot be my disciple" (Luke 14:26). That word *hate* is a Semitic way of expressing priority, not a command to despise one's family. But the priority is unmistakable. The family is a gift, and like every gift it becomes an idol when it's asked to be the center.

The church's own liturgy once knew better. The marriage service in Thomas Cranmer's *Book of Common Prayer* (1549) named three causes for which matrimony was ordained. The first was the procreation of children, to be brought up in the fear and nurture of the Lord. The second was a remedy against sin. The third, in modern spelling, was "the mutual society, help, and comfort, that the one ought to have of the other, both in prosperity and adversity." Augustine, more than a thousand years earlier, had likewise named offspring as one good of marriage among three, alongside faithfulness and the sacramental bond. Neither Augustine nor Cranmer imagined a marriage whose only purpose was its children. The first cause reaches its fulfillment when the children are raised. The third cause, the mutual society and help and comfort of two people through good times and bad, has no expiration date. It was there before the first child was born, and it is meant to be there after the last one leaves. A couple who have lived as though the first cause were the only one will find the empty nest disorienting, because they have lost the purpose they built their life around. A couple who have tended the third all along will find that it's still there, waiting for more of their attention.

That is the distinction the whole season turns on: the difference between a partnership in a project and a union of persons. A partnership in a project is held together by the work, and when the work ends, the partnership has no reason to continue. A union of persons is held together by the people themselves, and the work was only ever one of the things they did together. Most marriages are some of both. The empty nest shows the proportions. Couples on the project end of that scale often describe themselves, by the time the last child leaves, as roommates, and [Why Married Couples Drift Apart](/writing/the-slow-drift-that-ends-marriages) looks at how that happens and what turns it.

A marriage built around the children is a marriage with an idol in the middle of it, even when the idol is beloved and the worship is sincere. When the children leave, the idol leaves with them, and the couple discovers the space it occupied.

It's worth saying that the discovery often falls unevenly. One spouse, frequently but not always the mother, may have poured most of their daily life and sense of purpose into the work of raising the children, and may feel the empty nest as a loss of vocation, not just of company. The other may have poured themselves into work outside the home, and may discover that the relationship they were counting on to be there after the children left has quietly thinned from years of neglect. Both have lost something. Both may have been living on the children's fuel.

## What Scripture meant by leaving

The first thing the Bible says about marriage is about leaving: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). The verse is usually read at weddings, and it's usually read from the point of view of the couple getting married. But it has another side. Every leaving implies someone who is left. The verse assumes that parents will raise children who leave them, and that this leaving is not a tragedy but the design.

That means the marriage comes first, in more than one sense. It came before the children, and it's meant to outlast their presence in the home. The children were always meant to go. The marriage was always meant to stay. A couple that understands this can grieve the leaving without being destroyed by it, because the leaving is what they were working toward all along.

The Psalms use an image that makes the same point: "Like arrows in the hand of a warrior are the children of one's youth" (Psalm 127:4). An arrow held forever in the hand is useless. Its whole purpose is to be released, to go where the archer cannot go. Parents who raise their children well are preparing to let them fly.

Two biblical parents show what this letting go can look like. Hannah prayed for years for a son, and when Samuel was born, she gave him back: "For this child I prayed, and the LORD has granted me my petition that I made to him. Therefore I have lent him to the LORD. As long as he lives, he is lent to the LORD" (1 Samuel 1:27–28). She understood that the child had never belonged to her in the way she might have wished. And Mary, when Jesus was twelve and stayed behind in the temple, searched for him for three days before finding him. His answer must have stung: "Why were you looking for me? Did you not know that I must be in my Father's house?" (Luke 2:49). Luke adds that his parents didn't understand what he meant, and that "his mother treasured up all these things in her heart" (Luke 2:51). A child raised to belong to God will, sooner or later, belong to God in ways that leave the parents standing outside, wondering.

## Grief is the right word

None of this means the empty nest should be painless. It's a real loss, and grief is the right name for what many parents feel. The daily presence of a person you love, the sounds in the house, the shared meals, the small rituals, the sense of being needed, all of it ends, and the ending hurts even when it's healthy.

Scripture doesn't ask us to pretend otherwise. Naomi, returning to Bethlehem after losing her husband and both her sons, said to the women who greeted her, "I went away full, and the LORD has brought me back empty" (Ruth 1:21). Her losses were far greater than a child leaving for college, and it would be wrong to compare them too closely. But her honesty is instructive. She didn't disguise the emptiness or hurry past it. And the book that bears her daughter-in-law's name ends with Naomi holding a grandson in her lap, her emptiness filled in a way she couldn't have imagined when she first spoke those words.

Many parents also carry regrets into the empty nest: things they wish they had done differently, time they wish they had spent, words they wish they hadn't said. Some carry the grief of a child who has left the faith, or who has left them, and whose absence is not the ordinary absence of an adult child but the painful distance of estrangement. Those griefs are real, and they deserve more than a paragraph ([When Your Teenager Says They Don't Believe Anymore](/writing/teenager-losing-faith) speaks to part of that ache). For now, it's enough to say that the empty nest often surfaces what the busy years kept below the waterline, and that bringing it into the light, before God and with each other, is part of the work of this season.

## Who are we now

So how does a couple become two again?

C. S. Lewis, in *The Four Loves* (1960), drew a distinction that is useful here. Lovers, he said, typically stand face to face, absorbed in each other. Friends stand side by side, absorbed in something they share. A good marriage needs both, but the child-rearing years often erode both. The face-to-face intimacy gets crowded out by exhaustion and logistics, and the side-by-side friendship gets replaced by a shared job. The empty nest is a chance to rebuild both, and friendship is often the place to start. What do you share besides the children? What would you like to share? What could you face together, side by side?

Tim and Kathy Keller, in *The Meaning of Marriage* (2011), describe marriage as a kind of spiritual friendship in which each spouse helps the other become the person God is making them to be. They draw on an idea from Lewis's sermon *The Weight of Glory* (1941), which says: "There are no ordinary people. You have never talked to a mere mortal." Every person you meet, Lewis argued, is on the way to becoming either a creature of astonishing glory or something unspeakably dark. The Kellers suggest that a husband and wife are meant to see in each other that future glory, and to help each other toward it. That's a purpose that doesn't end when the children leave. If anything, it can finally become the center of the marriage.

In practice, the rebuilding tends to be quite ordinary. It starts with conversation about something other than the children, which can feel awkward at first, like learning a language you once spoke fluently and have mostly forgotten. It involves curiosity, the recognition that your spouse has changed in the last twenty years and that you may not know them as well as you think. It often involves repentance, the honest admission that some of the distance between you was your doing, that you let the marriage run on autopilot while you poured yourself into the kids or the career. And it usually involves finding new shared purpose.

The Bible gives older couples a great deal of purpose. Paul tells Titus that older men and older women are to teach and model faithfulness for the younger (Titus 2:2–5). Empty bedrooms can become places of hospitality for students, for single people far from family, for missionaries on leave, for neighbors in need. Grandchildren, if they come, are a gift: "Grandchildren are the crown of the aged, and the glory of children is their fathers" (Proverbs 17:6). Many couples in this season also find themselves caring for aging parents, which can strain a marriage but can also draw it together around a shared act of love. And the church needs older couples, not as an audience at the back of the sanctuary but as mentors, elders, and friends to people who are where they used to be.

There's also a new relationship to build with the grown children themselves. The task shifts from parenting to friendship, from managing to trusting. That shift is hard, especially for parents who are used to being needed, and it takes time. But a couple that has found its own center is far better able to release its children into adulthood than a couple that still needs the children to hold the marriage together.

It has to be said that some marriages reach the empty nest carrying more than drift. Some couples stayed together for the children through years of real unhappiness, and some through years of harm. If your marriage involves violence, threats, or control, the empty nest is not a reason to stay, and you don't need to wait for anything to get safe. The National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233. But for most couples who find the quiet house frightening, the problem is not that the marriage is dangerous. It's that it has been neglected, and neglect can be repented of.

## The last letting go

There's a deeper reason the empty nest matters, and it's the one we'd rather not think about. The children's leaving is a rehearsal. One day the house will be emptied again, not by a child moving out but by one of you dying. The marriage that survives the first letting go will face a second one, harder and final, and the couple who learn now to hold each other with open hands will be better prepared for the day when one of them has to let the other go entirely.

That sounds grim, but it isn't meant to be. It's simply true, and truth told early is a mercy. The empty nest is a reminder that every good thing in this life is given for a season and then released. The children were given and released. The marriage is given and will one day be released. And the Christian hope is that nothing given by God and released into his hands is finally lost.

So the question at the table, the one that arrives in the quiet after the dishes, is not really "Who are we without the children?" It's the older question underneath it: whether the two of you will be content to be what you were at the beginning, before there were any children at all, two people joined by God and given to each other for a while. The children were never the point of the marriage. They were one of its fruits. The marriage itself, with all its ordinary faithfulness and the long habit of turning toward each other, is still yours to tend. The bedroom down the hall will stay quiet. The question is whether the two of you will let the quiet become a room you share.
