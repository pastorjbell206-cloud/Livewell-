Most marriages do not die from a wound. They die from a draft.

We have the wrong picture of how it ends. We imagine the affair, the discovered text, the door slammed at one in the morning, the dramatic scene that splits a life into before and after. Those happen. But they are not how most marriages end, and they are usually not the disease. They are the fever that finally tells you the body was already sick. The thing that actually ends marriages is quieter, slower, and almost impossible to point to, because it never had a single day you could circle on a calendar.

It is drift. And drift does not feel like anything.

That is the whole terror of it. Betrayal at least announces itself. You can name the day, assign the blame, decide whether to forgive. Drift offers nothing to forgive. There was no Tuesday it happened. There were ten thousand Tuesdays, each of them unremarkable, each one a small decision to be slightly less present than the day before, and not one of them felt like a decision at all. You did not stop loving your spouse. You stopped turning toward them. The first is a betrayal. The second is just fatigue, and fatigue does not feel like sin.

I have sat with couples on the far side of this. They are not cruel people. They did not have affairs. They raised the kids and paid the mortgage and went on the occasional trip, and somewhere in the middle of all that competence they became roommates who share a name. When I ask what happened, they cannot tell me. There is no scene to describe. *We just grew apart* is the phrase, said with a shrug, as though apartness were weather. It is not weather. It is the sum of a thousand unwatered days.

Consider how it actually works. A man comes home tired and his wife is mid-sentence about something small, and instead of turning toward the sentence he turns toward his phone, and nothing happens. No fight. No consequence. She finishes the sentence to the air and files away, without knowing she filed it, the information that she is talking to a man who is not there. He files away, without knowing, that home is a place where he is permitted to leave while sitting still. Multiply that exchange by the years. That is the drift. It is built entirely out of moments too small to argue about.

The Hebrew Scriptures have a word for this that we have ruined by domesticating it. *Shamar.* To keep, to guard, to watch over. It is the word God gives Adam in the garden — to *shamar* the ground, to tend and protect it. It is the same word in the priestly blessing: the Lord *keep* you. To keep something is not to possess it. It is to stay awake over it. And the opposite of keeping is not hatred. The opposite of keeping is neglect, the slow withdrawal of attention from a thing that needed your attention to live.

We do not have an affair against our marriages. We simply stop keeping them. And an unkept garden does not stay the same. It returns to wilderness, not because anyone attacked it, but because no one watched it.

Jesus said the thing the church has somehow never applied to marriage: that it is the little foxes that ruin the vineyard, that a house can be swept and ordered and still be empty. He warned about the seed that fell among thorns, and what choked it was not a single thorn but the slow crowding of cares and the deceitfulness of ordinary life. *The cares of this world.* Not scandal. Not enemy. The mortgage, the calendar, the kids' schedules, the thousand small worthy things that fill the soil until there is no room left for the one thing the soil was for. Most marriages are not strangled by a great sin. They are crowded out by small goods that ask for everything and give nothing back to the covenant.

I need to be honest about my part, because drift is the one marital failure where no one is the villain and that is exactly why it wins. There is no moment I can point to where I chose distance from the people I love. There were only the moments where I chose the urgent over the present, the email over the eyes across the room, the productive evening over the unproductive conversation that was actually the marriage. I did not betray anyone. I was simply not there, repeatedly, with a clear conscience, because absence with a clear conscience is the most dangerous kind. The betrayer at least knows he has done something. The drifter feels like a hard worker.

There is a sociology underneath this that the church should have noticed and did not. Robert Bellah, studying American life forty years ago, found that we had become a people who treat every bond as provisional, sustained only as long as it meets a need, dissolved the moment the need outgrows the bond. He called it the language of the therapeutic, the slow replacement of covenant with arrangement. We did not arrive at our marriages immune to that. We carry it in. And the drift is what the therapeutic does to a covenant it cannot quite understand — it stops investing in a thing it secretly believes is temporary, and the believing makes it so. You cannot keep what you have already half-decided you might leave. The hand loosens before the mind admits it.

This is the lie at the center of it. We tell ourselves that providing is the same as presence, that the absence is temporary, that we will turn back toward them once the season eases. But the season never eases on its own; a new urgent thing always arrives to replace the last one, and the marriage learns to expect less, and lowered expectations feel like peace. The couples who frighten me most are not the ones fighting. The ones fighting are still turned toward each other, still convinced the other person's response matters enough to raise their voice over. The ones who frighten me are calm. They have stopped expecting. They have made a quiet truce with the distance and called it maturity.

I keep returning to the image of the vineyard because Jesus kept returning to it. In His parables the love of many grows cold not in a moment of dramatic rebellion but, as Matthew records it, because lawlessness is multiplied — small thing upon small thing until the warmth is simply gone and no one can say when. He does not warn us most often about the spectacular fall. He warns about the slow cooling. The lamps that run out of oil while the bridesmaids are merely tired and the bridegroom merely delayed. The servant who says in his heart that the master is taking his time, and begins, gradually, without ever deciding to, to live as though no one is coming home. Drift is the spiritual condition Jesus named most often and the church preaches least, because it makes a poor sermon. There is no villain to point at. There is only the slow withdrawal of a heart from the thing it was given to keep, and a clear conscience the whole way down.

So what do you do about a thing with no single cause and no single day.

You cannot fix drift the way you fix betrayal, with a confrontation and a reckoning. Drift is undone the same way it was done — by ten thousand small turnings, none of which feels like much. You turn toward the sentence instead of the phone. You ask the question and stay for the whole answer. You put down the urgent thing, which will not remember you, for the present person, who will. None of these will feel significant on the day you do them. That is precisely the point. The drift was built of insignificant days, and only insignificant days can rebuild what they took.

The vineyard does not need a hero. It needs a keeper.

Pay attention to the small turning away. It is never small. It is the marriage, ending one Tuesday at a time, in a room where nothing appears to be wrong.
