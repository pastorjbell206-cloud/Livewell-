Most marriages do not die from a wound. They die from a draft.

We have the wrong picture of how it ends. We imagine the affair, the discovered text, the door slammed at one in the morning, the dramatic scene that splits a life into before and after. Those happen. But they are not how most marriages end, and they are usually not the disease. They are the fever that finally tells you the body was already sick. The thing that actually ends marriages is quieter, slower, and almost impossible to point to, because it never had a single day you could circle on a calendar.

It is marriage drift. And the terrible genius of drift is that it does not feel like anything.

If you have been searching for why you and your spouse are growing apart in marriage, you have probably been looking for a cause — a fight, a season, a betrayal, a year it all went wrong. You will not find one. That is the first thing to understand and the hardest. **A marriage does not end because of a single Tuesday. It ends because of a thousand of them**, each unremarkable, each a small turn away, and not one of them felt like a decision at the time. The rest of this is an argument that the small turns are the whole story, and that only small turns can undo them.

## Why drift feels like nothing while it happens

Betrayal at least announces itself. You can name the day, assign the blame, decide whether to forgive. Drift offers nothing to forgive. There was no Tuesday it happened. There were ten thousand Tuesdays, each one a small decision to be slightly less present than the day before, and not one of them registered as a decision at all. You did not stop loving your spouse. You stopped turning toward them. The first is a betrayal. The second is just fatigue, and fatigue does not feel like sin.

I have sat with couples on the far side of this. They are not cruel people. They did not have affairs. They raised the kids and paid the mortgage and went on the occasional trip, and somewhere in the middle of all that competence they became roommates who share a name. When I ask what happened, they cannot tell me. There is no scene to describe. *We just grew apart* is the phrase, said with a shrug, as though apartness were weather. It is not weather. It is the sum of a thousand unwatered days.

Consider how it actually works. A man comes home tired and his wife is mid-sentence about something small, and instead of turning toward the sentence he turns toward his phone, and nothing happens. No fight. No consequence. She finishes the sentence to the air and files away, without knowing she filed it, the information that she is talking to a man who is not there. He files away, without knowing, that home is a place where he is permitted to leave while sitting still. Multiply that exchange by the years. That is the drift. It is built entirely out of moments too small to argue about.

The researchers who study how couples connect call these moments bids — the dozens of small invitations a day to turn toward each other. A comment about the weather. A hand on a shoulder. A sigh that means *ask me what is wrong.* John Gottman, watching couples in his Seattle laboratory through the 1980s and 1990s, found that the couples who lasted turned toward those bids the overwhelming majority of the time, and the couples who divorced turned away. Not in anger. Just away. The difference between a marriage that holds and one that dissolves was not the fights. It was thousands of tiny moments no one would ever remember, each one a fork where you either looked up or did not. The marriage is decided in the looking up, and almost no one knows the decision is being made.

## The Bible has a word for what you stopped doing

The Hebrew Scriptures have a word for this that we have ruined by domesticating it. *Shamar.* To keep, to guard, to watch over. It is the word God gives the man in the garden — to *shamar* the ground, to tend and protect it (Genesis 2:15). It is the same word in the priestly blessing: the Lord *keep* you (Numbers 6:24). To keep something is not to possess it. It is to stay awake over it. And the opposite of keeping is not hatred. The opposite of keeping is neglect — the slow withdrawal of attention from a thing that needed your attention to live.

We do not have an affair against our marriages. We simply stop keeping them. And an unkept garden does not stay the same. It returns to wilderness, not because anyone attacked it, but because no one watched it.

Jesus said the thing the church has somehow never applied to marriage: that it is the little foxes that ruin the vineyard, that a house can be swept and ordered and still be empty. He warned about the seed that fell among thorns, and what choked it was not a single thorn but the slow crowding of the cares of this world (Matthew 13:22). Not scandal. Not enemy. The mortgage, the calendar, the kids' schedules, the thousand small worthy things that fill the soil until there is no room left for the one thing the soil was for. Most marriages are not strangled by a great sin. They are crowded out by small goods that ask for everything and give nothing back to the covenant.

**Drift is the spiritual condition Jesus named most often and the church preaches least, because it makes a poor sermon. There is no villain to point at.** There is only the slow withdrawal of a heart from the thing it was given to keep, and a clear conscience the whole way down.

## The drifter feels like a hard worker

I need to be honest about my part, because drift is the one marital failure where no one is the villain, and that is exactly why it wins.

There is no moment I can point to where I chose distance from the people I love. There were only the moments where I chose the urgent over the present — the email over the eyes across the room, the productive evening over the unproductive conversation that was actually the marriage. I did not betray anyone. I was simply not there, repeatedly, with a clear conscience, because absence with a clear conscience is the most dangerous kind. The betrayer at least knows he has done something. The drifter feels like a hard worker.

This is the lie at the center of it. We tell ourselves that providing is the same as presence, that the absence is temporary, that we will turn back toward them once the season eases. But the season never eases on its own; a new urgent thing always arrives to replace the last one, and the marriage learns to expect less, and lowered expectations feel like peace. The couples who frighten me most are not the ones fighting. The ones fighting are still turned toward each other, still convinced the other person's response matters enough to raise their voice over. The ones who frighten me are calm. They have stopped expecting. They have made a quiet truce with the distance and called it maturity.

## The culture that taught us our marriages were temporary

There is a sociology underneath this that the church should have noticed and did not.

Robert Bellah and his coauthors, in *Habits of the Heart* (1985), found that Americans had become a people who treat every bond as provisional — sustained only as long as it meets a need, dissolved the moment the need outgrows the bond. They called it the language of the therapeutic, the slow replacement of covenant with arrangement. We did not arrive at our marriages immune to that. We carry it in. And the drift is what the therapeutic does to a covenant it cannot quite understand. It stops investing in a thing it secretly believes is temporary, and the believing makes it so. You cannot keep what you have already half-decided you might leave. The hand loosens before the mind admits it.

Jesus described the mechanism with a precision the sociologists only caught up to centuries later. In His telling the love of many grows cold not in a moment of dramatic rebellion but because lawlessness is multiplied (Matthew 24:12) — small thing upon small thing until the warmth is simply gone and no one can say when. The lamps run out of oil while the bridesmaids are merely tired and the bridegroom merely delayed (Matthew 25:1–13). The servant says in his heart that the master is taking his time, and begins, gradually, without ever deciding to, to live as though no one is coming home (Matthew 24:48–49). The danger Jesus warns about most is not the spectacular fall. It is the slow cooling, with a clear conscience the whole way down.

## How to turn back one ordinary day at a time

So what do you do about a thing with no single cause and no single day.

You cannot fix drift the way you fix betrayal, with a confrontation and a reckoning. There is no scene to have, no apology that covers it, because the injury was distributed across years and no single act caused it. Drift is undone the same way it was done — by ten thousand small turnings, none of which feels like much. You turn toward the sentence instead of the phone. You ask the question and stay for the whole answer. You put down the urgent thing, which will not remember you, for the present person, who will.

None of these will feel significant on the day you do them. That is precisely the point, and it is the part people refuse to accept, because we want repair to match the weight of the loss. We want one large act to balance the years. But the years were not built of large acts. They were built of insignificant days, and only insignificant days can rebuild what they took. The man who waits for a grand gesture to fix a marriage that died of small neglect has misunderstood both the disease and the cure.

This is slow, and it is humbling, and it will not satisfy the part of you that wants to feel you have done something. You will turn toward your spouse on a Tuesday and nothing dramatic will happen, and you will be tempted to conclude it did not work. It worked. The drift was invisible while it happened; so is its undoing. You will only know it took years later, when you look up and the person across the table is someone you know again.

There is a deeper reason the small turnings are the only cure, and it is theological rather than practical. God did not redeem the world with a single overwhelming gesture and then withdraw. He bound Himself to a people and stayed — through their grumbling in the desert, their idolatry, their slow forgetting, their generations of being merely tired and merely distracted from Him. The covenant did not survive because the people were faithful. It survived because God kept turning toward a people who kept turning away, day after unremarkable day, until the keeping itself became the love. A marriage that learns to do that in miniature is not merely saving itself. It is rehearsing the one faithfulness the whole Bible is about. The God who refused to drift from a people who drifted from Him is the only model that makes the daily turning bearable, because it tells you the small, unrewarded act of attention is not beneath the dignity of love. It is the entire substance of it.

So you do not need a strategy so much as a posture, the willingness to be present without applause, to keep a thing no one is watching you keep. The marriage will not thank you on the day. The spouse may not even notice for a long while, having stopped expecting. You keep turning anyway, because that is what keeping is, and because the alternative is the quiet you already know the shape of.

And do not wait until you feel the loss to begin. That is the final trap of drift — it numbs you to its own progress, so that by the time the distance is wide enough to alarm you, years of small turnings have already passed unspent. The couples who never reach the cold quiet are not the ones who caught it late and fought hard. They are the ones who never stopped doing the small thing in the first place, who kept turning toward each other on the ordinary Tuesdays when nothing was wrong and there was no reason to, which is exactly when it mattered most. You guard a marriage in the season when it does not appear to need guarding. By the time it obviously needs it, you are no longer guarding. You are rebuilding.

The vineyard does not need a hero. It needs a keeper.

Pay attention to the small turning away. It is never small. It is the marriage, ending one Tuesday at a time, in a room where nothing appears to be wrong — and it is the marriage being saved the same way, by a hand that finally refuses to let go.
