It rarely arrives as a crisis. It arrives on an ordinary weeknight, somewhere in your forties or early fifties, in a kitchen that looks the way it has looked for fifteen years. The children have a schedule that runs the household like a timetable. The mortgage is manageable. The job is fine. The person across the table is a good person, and you still love them in the way you love a familiar room. And a sentence surfaces that you would never say out loud: Is this all there is?

Nothing is wrong, and that's what makes it frightening. There was no affair, no betrayal, no violence, nothing you could name to a counselor as the problem. There's only a flatness, a sense that the story you were living has stopped going anywhere, and a growing suspicion that it might be the marriage's fault. Sometimes the suspicion takes the shape of a face: an old flame found online, a colleague who seems to see you, a life you imagine you could have had. More often it's just a dull ache and a question you don't know what to do with.

The claim this essay makes is that the ache is usually honest about something, but rarely honest about the marriage. It's a question about the soul that we have been trained to aim at the person across the table. And it's one of the questions a long marriage exists to answer, slowly, if we let it.

## Where the midlife crisis came from

The phrase itself is younger than most of the people who feel it. The Canadian psychoanalyst Elliott Jaques coined it in a 1965 paper titled "Death and the Mid-life Crisis," based on his study of artists whose work changed sharply, or stopped, somewhere in their late thirties. His argument was that midlife is the point at which a person first faces, not as an idea but as a felt reality, that they are going to die. The first half of life runs on the assumption of an open future. Somewhere in the middle, the future closes enough to be seen, and the self has to reckon with its limits.

Jaques gave the experience a name, but the experience is very old. Dante opened the *Inferno*, written in the early 1300s, with a line that puts his poem at exactly this point: "In the middle of the road of our life I found myself in a dark wood, for the straight way was lost." Dante was thirty-five in the year his poem is set, and he was counting by Psalm 90: "The years of our life are seventy, or even by reason of strength eighty; yet their span is but toil and trouble; they are soon gone, and we fly away" (Psalm 90:10). Half of seventy is thirty-five. The dark wood is the place where a person discovers that half the road is behind them and that they are lost on it. The same psalm asks for the one thing that makes such a discovery bearable: "So teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom" (Psalm 90:12).

Carl Jung, in his 1930 lecture "The Stages of Life," argued that the second half of life cannot be lived by the program of the first. The morning of life, he said, is spent establishing oneself in the world, building a career, a family, and a place. The afternoon has a different task, turned inward, toward meaning and toward the end. People who try to live the afternoon by the morning's rules end up grasping at youth and wondering why it keeps slipping through their fingers.

There's some evidence that the experience is widespread. In a 2008 paper, the economists David Blanchflower and Andrew Oswald reported that, across large surveys in many countries, reported well-being tends to follow a U-shaped curve over a lifetime, dipping to its lowest point in middle age and rising again afterward. Jonathan Rauch popularized the finding in *The Happiness Curve* (2018). The pattern is contested; other researchers have found it weaker or absent in some data sets, and nobody should treat it as a law. But it matches what many people report, and it suggests that the midlife ache isn't a personal failure or a sign that you married the wrong person. It's something that happens to a great many people at this point in the road.

What is true in all of this is worth holding onto. Midlife really does bring a reckoning. You see, for the first time, the lives you will not live, the careers you won't have, the places you won't see, the person you won't become. Your parents are aging or gone. Your body has started sending notices. Your children are becoming people who don't need you in the way they once did. Grief at the closing of possibilities is not a sin. It's the natural response of a creature who was made for more than one lifetime can hold.

## Why the marriage takes the blame

So why does the ache so often land on the marriage?

Partly because the marriage is the nearest thing. The job can be blamed, and often is, but the job doesn't sit across from you at breakfast. The body can be blamed, but the body can't be left. The marriage is the one large feature of a midlife that seems to come with an exit, and a restless heart always notices the exit first.

But there is a longer history underneath the reflex. For most of Christian history a marriage was expected to carry a household, a livelihood, children, and a shared faith. It was not expected to supply the feeling that life was going somewhere. Over the last century, and especially since the 1960s, Americans have come to ask marriage to be the place where the self is discovered and expressed, and to judge it by whether it still makes us feel like ourselves. [Why Married Couples Drift Apart](/writing/the-slow-drift-that-ends-marriages) traces that history and its witnesses in detail. What matters here is what it does at midlife. If the marriage is supposed to be the engine of my growth, then a season in which I feel stalled is, almost by definition, the marriage's fault.

There's something right in the desire underneath that assumption, and Christians shouldn't sneer at it. It isn't wrong to want your marriage to be a place where you grow. Scripture describes marriage as a companionship in which two people help each other become what God made them to be. The problem is not the desire for growth. It's the assumption that the spouse is the source of the growth, and that when you stop growing, the spouse has stopped working.

The midlife fantasy runs on a comparison, and the comparison is rigged. On one side is the real person across the table: changed from who they were at twenty-five, tired, known down to their worst habits, sometimes a stranger in ways neither of you chose. (What to do when a spouse has become a stranger is the subject of [I Don't Recognize My Spouse Anymore](/writing/when-you-married-someone-you-no-longer-recognize).) On the other side is an imagined person, a face from an old photograph or a colleague seen only at their best, who never has to fold laundry, never has bad breath in the morning, and never disappoints you, because they don't exist. The imagined spouse always wins the comparison. That's what makes the comparison a lie.

I need to be honest about something here. The ache is real, and so is the temptation to blame it on the person I live with. Most of us, when we feel stalled, look for the nearest cause, and the nearest cause is usually sleeping beside us. It's much easier to say "my marriage is empty" than to say "I am empty," because the first sentence gives me something to do about it and the second leaves me with myself.

## Grief, restlessness, or illness

The church has an older name for part of what we now call a midlife crisis. The desert monks of the fourth century called it acedia, and Evagrius of Pontus, in his *Praktikos*, pictured it as the noonday demon: the restlessness that makes the day feel endless, turns a monk against his cell and his work, and fills his head with the better life he could live somewhere else. (The drift essay linked above sets out his portrait at length.) Anyone who has felt the midlife ache will recognize it. The day that seems too long. The sense that the life you have is smaller than the life you could have. The fantasy of elsewhere. Evagrius's counsel was to stay in the cell, and to trust that on the far side of the temptation lay a peace the restlessness never knew.

But the old word has to be used carefully, because not every midlife ache is acedia, and naming it wrongly does harm. The midlife ache can be at least three different things. It can be grief, the honest sorrow of a creature coming to terms with limits and losses, which needs to be lamented rather than fixed. It can be acedia, the spiritual restlessness that tempts us to flee our own lives, which needs to be resisted with patience and prayer. And it can be depression, a real illness with physical dimensions, which needs medical care. If you have lost interest in almost everything, if your sleep or appetite has changed, if you feel hopeless or worthless, please see a doctor. This is not medical advice, and it's not a failure of faith to need treatment. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988. Misreading one of these as another can do real harm: treating depression as spiritual laziness, or treating acedia as a sign that you need a new spouse.

## What Ecclesiastes knows

There is a whole book of the Bible written by someone who felt something very like the midlife ache and refused to pretend otherwise. It begins: "Vanity of vanities, says the Preacher, vanity of vanities! All is vanity" (Ecclesiastes 1:2). The Hebrew word translated "vanity" is *hevel*, which means breath or vapor: something real but insubstantial, there one moment and gone the next.

The Preacher tried everything a person might try when the ordinary feels flat. "I made great works. I built houses and planted vineyards for myself" (Ecclesiastes 2:4). He accumulated wealth, pleasures, projects, and accomplishments. And then: "Then I considered all that my hands had done and the toil I had expended in doing it, and behold, all was vanity and a striving after wind, and there was nothing to be gained under the sun" (Ecclesiastes 2:11). The midlife fantasy promises that the right change, a new job, a new house, a new partner, will finally fill the emptiness. Ecclesiastes has already run that experiment, and its verdict is that nothing under the sun can fill what the ache is asking for.

But the book doesn't end in despair. It ends somewhere far more surprising. The Preacher says that God "has put eternity into man's heart, yet so that he cannot find out what God has done from the beginning to the end" (Ecclesiastes 3:11). The ache is real because we were made for eternity, and nothing temporary can satisfy it. And then, having said that nothing under the sun can fill the heart, the Preacher tells his readers to receive the ordinary things of life as gifts: "Go, eat your bread with joy, and drink your wine with a merry heart, for God has already approved what you do" (Ecclesiastes 9:7). And then, in one of the most remarkable verses in the Bible: "Enjoy life with the wife whom you love, all the days of your vain life that he has given you under the sun, because that is your portion in life and in your toil at which you toil under the sun" (Ecclesiastes 9:9).

Notice what the Preacher does. He doesn't tell his reader that the marriage will fill the emptiness. He's already said that nothing under the sun can do that. He tells his reader to enjoy life with the spouse God has given, precisely because life is vapor. The marriage is not the answer to the ache for eternity. It's a gift to be received inside that ache, a portion given for the days of a brief life. The Preacher's wisdom is to stop asking the spouse to be God, and then, freed from that impossible demand, to enjoy the spouse as a gift of God.

Augustine said it at the opening of his *Confessions*: God made us for himself, and our heart is restless until it rests in him. The midlife ache is the restlessness Augustine was talking about. It's not a sign that you married the wrong person. It's a sign that you were made for God, and that your spouse, however good, is not God. I came to faith from atheism, and I know this question isn't only a Christian one. The unbeliever feels the same ache and has to decide what it means without appeal to anything beyond the sun. The Christian answer is no, but not in the way the fantasy promises. The more you're longing for doesn't lie in another life under the sun. It lies beyond the sun, and it's already breaking into this one.

## What staying makes possible

If the ache isn't really about the marriage, what does the marriage have to do with it?

The vows themselves are part of the answer. A promise made at twenty-five binds you to a person neither of you could yet see, through difficulties you didn't choose and couldn't have predicted. At midlife that binding is usually felt as the thing standing between you and the life you might have had. But it works the other way too. Because leaving wasn't the first option, you had to find out things you would never have found if leaving had been easy: what you do when you're disappointed, what forgiveness costs, what it is to be known by someone who has seen the worst of you and is still at the table. The promise doesn't only restrict. It reveals.

That's why the midlife years, which feel like a dead end, can turn out to be the most important years of a marriage. The early years run on attraction, novelty, and the shared project of building a life. The middle years strip much of that away. What's left is two people who have to decide, without the help of novelty or romance or a clear project, whether they will keep choosing each other. That choice, made again and again in the flat years, is what turns a marriage into something neither person could have built alone.

What does that look like in practice? It usually looks like curiosity, the decision to get to know again a person you assumed you already knew. Your spouse has changed over twenty years, and so have you, and there's a stranger across the table worth meeting. It looks like repentance, the honest recognition that some of the flatness is your own doing, that you stopped paying attention, stopped pursuing, stopped telling the truth about what you wanted. It looks like shared purpose beyond the children, work or service or a new project that belongs to both of you. It looks like tending the physical side of the marriage, which often suffers in midlife and can often be renewed. It looks like guarding the marriage's boundaries: the midlife years are when emotional affairs quietly begin, often with someone who seems to see you as you wish your spouse did. And it very often looks like getting help, from a counselor, a pastor, or an older couple who have been through it.

One practical question comes early: should you say any of this out loud? Usually yes, though how you say it matters. "I've been feeling flat, and I'm afraid I've been blaming you for it" is a sentence a marriage can receive. "I'm not sure I love you anymore," said in the first raw weeks, often names the ache wrongly and wounds the one person best placed to help carry it. Tell the truth about the ache before you tell a story about its cause. And if the ache has already taken the shape of a particular face, tell someone you trust before it takes the shape of a decision.

None of this is a formula for feeling alive again. Some seasons of marriage are simply faithfulness, and the feeling comes back later or in a different form. But couples who come through the midlife years together often describe something on the other side that they didn't have before: a companionship that no longer depends on novelty, a love that has been tested and chosen, a friendship with someone who knows them fully and has stayed.

## The second half of the road

The honest thing to say at the end is that the ache will not entirely go away, and it isn't supposed to. The Preacher was right. Life under the sun is vapor, and a heart made for eternity will always feel the gap. The question isn't how to stop feeling the ache. It's where you will take it.

Paul wrote from the other side of a hard life: "So we do not lose heart. Though our outer self is wasting away, our inner self is being renewed day by day" (2 Corinthians 4:16). The body declines, the options close, the road runs out. But something else is happening underneath all of it, and it's happening now, in the ordinary days. The Christian hope is not that you'll find a better life under the sun. It's that the one who says "Behold, I am making all things new" (Revelation 21:5) is already at work in this one, in this kitchen, with this person.

The midlife fantasy says the more you're longing for is somewhere else. The gospel says it's coming, and that you can meet its first light in the place you are. The person across the table is the one you'll grow old with, if you stay. They'll see you at your worst, and you'll see them at theirs, and the two of you will carry each other through the losses the second half of life brings. That's not all there is. But it's more than the fantasy ever offered, and it's where most of us will have to learn to wait for the rest.
