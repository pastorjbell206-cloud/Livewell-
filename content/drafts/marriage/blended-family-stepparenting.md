Picture a woman standing at a kitchen counter at half past four on a Tuesday, making a second batch of the pasta her stepdaughter likes because the first one had the wrong sauce. She has been married to the girl's father for two years. She has driven to orthodontist appointments, sat through a school play, learned which friend is in favor this month and which one isn't. Tonight, when she asks the eleven-year-old to set the table, the girl looks at her and says, without much heat, "You're not my mom." Then she sets the table anyway.

The sentence hurts because it's true. Nothing the stepmother has done is undone by it, and nothing she can do will make it false. Her husband will come home in an hour and she'll have to decide whether to tell him, and if she tells him, whether he'll take her side or his daughter's, and whether there are sides at all. Somewhere across town the girl's mother is also making dinner, and the girl will be at that table on Thursday. The stepmother has no word for what she is to this child. Neither does the child. Neither, it turns out, does the culture they both live in.

People type the question into a search box in a dozen forms: how to be a good stepparent, what to do when your stepchild hates you, how long it takes a blended family to blend. Underneath all of them is a single hope, that there is some technique by which a stepparent can finally become a parent. This essay argues that the hope itself is the problem. The good stepparent is not someone who succeeds at replacing an absent or divided parent. It's someone who learns to love a child without taking anyone's place, which is harder, slower and, in the end, far more like the way God loves any of us.

## A family with no script

Blended families are not new, and it helps to begin there. Before modern medicine, death broke up marriages early and often. The historian Lawrence Stone argued in *The Family, Sex and Marriage in England 1500-1800* (1977) that the median length of a marriage in early modern England was short enough that modern divorce functions, in effect, as a substitute for the death that once broke households apart. Widows and widowers remarried quickly because a farm or a shop could not run with one adult and a houseful of children. Stepmothers and stepfathers were ordinary figures in every village.

The fairy tales remember them, and not kindly. In the first edition of the Grimm brothers' collection, published in 1812, the queen who wants Snow White dead is the girl's own mother. By the 1819 edition she had become a stepmother. The change tells us something about what a culture can bear to say. A mother's cruelty was unthinkable, so the story handed it to the woman who had married in. The wicked stepmother has lived in children's imaginations ever since, and many stepmothers report feeling her shadow the moment they walk into a room.

The twentieth century offered a second myth to set against the first. When *The Brady Bunch* went on the air in 1969, a widower with three boys married a woman with three girls, and by the end of the first season the household ran on jokes and group hugs. It's worth noticing when that show arrived. The same year, California passed the first no-fault divorce law in the country, and within a decade most states had followed. The stepfamilies that multiplied after 1970 were built differently from the ones the Grimms knew. They usually followed a divorce rather than a death, which meant the other parent was alive, often nearby, and still a parent. Children now lived in two houses, with two sets of rules, and moved between them on a schedule a court had set.

In 1978 the sociologist Andrew Cherlin published an article called "Remarriage as an Incomplete Institution." His argument was simple and has held up. First marriages come with a thick set of norms: everyone knows roughly what a husband is, what a mother does, who disciplines the children and what the children call their parents. Remarriages with children have almost none. There is no settled answer to whether a stepfather may punish a twelve-year-old, what the child should call him, how the stepmother relates to the children's mother, or even what to call the relationship between a stepchild and the stepparent's own parents. Cherlin argued that this absence of norms, not any defect in the people involved, accounts for much of the strain these families carry. Every blended family has to invent its own customs while living inside them.

That is the first thing a struggling stepparent needs to hear. The difficulty is not proof that you're failing. The Pew Research Center, drawing on Census data in 2014, found that fewer than half of American children were living with two married parents in a first marriage, and about fifteen percent were living with two parents in a remarriage. Millions of households are improvising at once, and most of them assume the others have figured it out.

## Every stepfamily begins with a loss

The couple standing at the altar is happy. That's the ordinary and good reason for a wedding. But a second wedding with children present is two events at once, and the children are usually attending the second one. For the couple it is a beginning. For the children it often marks an ending: the end of the hope that their parents would reunite, or the final confirmation that the parent who died is not coming back, or the loss of a season when they had one parent to themselves. They are asked to celebrate something that, from where they stand, took something away.

Pauline Boss, a family therapist, developed the idea of ambiguous loss in her 1999 book of that name. She was describing losses that lack the clarity of a death: a family member physically present but psychologically absent, as in dementia, or psychologically present but physically absent, as with a soldier missing in action. She noticed that such losses are harder to grieve because nothing marks them, and she observed that families living with them often cannot agree on who is in the family and who is out. A child moving between two houses lives with a version of this. The other parent is present in the child's heart and absent from this dinner table, and the stepparent is present at the table and, for a long time, absent from the heart. Nobody has died. Everybody is grieving.

Patricia Papernow, a psychologist who has spent decades working with stepfamilies, lays out the structure of what follows in *Surviving and Thriving in Stepfamily Relationships* (2013). In a first-marriage family, she observes, everyone starts from the same place. In a stepfamily the parent and the children are insiders who share a history, habits and private jokes, and the stepparent is an outsider who shares none of it. The outsider feels shut out and often responds by pushing for closeness or control. The insiders feel crowded and push back. The parent in the middle feels torn between the spouse and the children, and each side reads that torn feeling as betrayal. Papernow also describes the loyalty bind: a child who begins to like the stepmother may feel she is betraying her mother, and may be cruelest to the stepmother precisely when she likes her most. The girl who said "You're not my mom" may have been saying something closer to "I'm afraid of how much I like you."

Christian families often make a particular mistake here. We believe in new creation, in grace that makes all things new, and we can bring that hope to a blended family as though the wedding itself should wipe the slate clean. It doesn't, and the Scriptures never promised it would. Many of the households in Genesis were built from more than one mother, and the text is candid about what that cost. Abraham's household split along the line between Sarah's son and Hagar's. Jacob's family, built from two wives and their two servants, produced brothers who sold one of their own into slavery. The narratives don't hold these families up as models. They show that the wounds a household carries into a new arrangement don't vanish at the ceremony, and that God works through such households without pretending they are whole.

## Why blending takes years, and who should discipline

Ron Deal, a Christian family therapist, has made a career of telling stepfamilies that they are not a blender. In *The Smart Stepfamily* (2002) he contrasts the fast appliance most couples imagine with the slow cooker that actually describes the process: the ingredients stay distinct for a long time, and the flavors join only with low heat and patience. Papernow's research points the same direction. Families that integrate well tend to take several years to do it, and many take longer. Couples who expect the household to feel like a family within a year or two feel like failures at exactly the moment they're doing normal work.

The practical heart of the matter is authority, and here the research is unusually consistent. James Bray, whose long study of remarried families is summarized in *Stepfamilies* (1998), and E. Mavis Hetherington, whose Virginia Longitudinal Study of Divorce and Remarriage is summarized in *For Better or For Worse* (2002), both found that stepparents who moved quickly into the role of disciplinarian met resistance that often hardened into lasting conflict. Stepfathers who first built a warm relationship, supported the mother's discipline and waited to exercise authority of their own fared much better. Papernow puts the same finding as a working rule: in the early years, the parent disciplines and the stepparent connects. The stepparent is something like a trusted aunt or coach, an adult with real standing in the house, owed courtesy and respect, who does not yet carry the authority that only a relationship can confer.

Many Christian stepparents bristle at this. They've been taught that children must obey the adults in the home, and they read the advice as permission for disrespect. It isn't. The house can have rules, and the parent can require the child to treat the stepparent with courtesy. What the research warns against is the stepparent trying to take by position what hasn't yet been given by trust. That warning has deep biblical roots. "Fathers, do not provoke your children, lest they become discouraged" (Colossians 3:21). Paul wrote that to fathers who had every legal right over their children in a Roman household, and he told them to restrain the use of it. Authority that runs ahead of love provokes. A stepparent who insists on the full weight of a parent's authority in the first year is not being biblical. He's being impatient.

There is a genuine disagreement among Christians about a related question, and it deserves both sides. Much Christian teaching on marriage holds that the marriage must come first in a household, above the children, and cites Genesis 2:24: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh." In a first marriage that ordering usually protects everyone. Children are secure when their parents' marriage is. Stepfamily therapists often push back, and not because they undervalue marriage. In a stepfamily, they point out, the parent-child bond is older than the marriage, sometimes by a decade or more, and a parent who announces that the new spouse now comes first may leave a grieving child feeling abandoned twice. Both sides are protecting something real. The best answer seems to hold them together: the couple needs to be united and to protect time for their marriage, and the parent needs to guard one-on-one time with the children so they don't experience the marriage as a replacement. This is a question where faithful families will weigh things differently depending on the ages of the children and the history they carry, and where a wise counselor who knows stepfamilies is worth far more than a slogan.

What children call a stepparent belongs in the same category. Some stepparents want to be called Mom or Dad because it feels like acceptance. Most children, especially those whose other parent is living, experience that request as a demand to choose. The kinder course is to let the child decide, to accept a first name without offense, and to let whatever affection comes arrive on its own terms. Some stepchildren, years later, start calling a stepparent something warmer without being asked. Many never do. Neither outcome measures how well the stepparent loved.

## Joseph, who raised a son who was not his

The New Testament contains the most important stepfamily in history, and we rarely read it that way. Joseph was betrothed to Mary when he learned she was pregnant with a child he knew was not his. Matthew's account of his first response is brief: "And her husband Joseph, being a just man and unwilling to put her to shame, resolved to divorce her quietly" (Matthew 1:19). Then the angel came, and Joseph changed course. "When Joseph woke from sleep, he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him: he took his wife, but knew her not until she had given birth to a son. And he called his name Jesus" (Matthew 1:24–25).

Naming a child was a father's act in that world. By naming him, Joseph took legal responsibility for a boy whose origin lay entirely outside himself. He protected him, fled with him to Egypt, brought him home, and by every indication taught him his trade, so that the villagers of Nazareth could later ask, "Is not this the carpenter's son?" (Matthew 13:55). Luke, tracing the genealogy, adds a careful phrase: Jesus was "the son (as was supposed) of Joseph" (Luke 3:23). Joseph gave the boy a name, a home, a craft and a lineage, and the text never lets anyone forget that he was not the boy's Father.

The one scene that shows this family from the inside is the visit to Jerusalem when Jesus was twelve. After three days of searching, Mary and Joseph find him in the temple. "And his mother said to him, 'Son, why have you treated us so? Behold, your father and I have been searching for you in great distress'" (Luke 2:48). Mary calls Joseph his father. Jesus answers with a different father entirely: "Why were you looking for me? Did you not know that I must be in my Father's house?" (Luke 2:49). Luke says they did not understand the saying. Then he adds: "And he went down with them and came to Nazareth and was submissive to them" (Luke 2:51).

Every stepparent should read that passage slowly. The boy had a prior belonging, older and deeper than anything Joseph could give him, and he said so out loud. Joseph did not contest it. He did not demand to be the only father in the story. He went home to Nazareth and kept at the ordinary labor of raising him, and the boy, knowing exactly whose son he was, submitted to him. Joseph's authority was real, and it rested on his willingness to be a guardian rather than a replacement. The Gospels never record a single word he spoke. His whole vocation was faithfulness to a child whose deepest identity was not his to give.

The analogy has limits, and they should be named. Jesus's Father is God, and a child's other parent is a sinner like the rest of us, sometimes a dangerous one. But the shape of Joseph's calling carries over. Scripture teaches that every child belongs to God before belonging to any parent: "Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD, the fruit of the womb a reward" (Psalm 127:3). Biological parents forget this constantly. They come to treat their children as extensions of themselves. The stepparent can't forget it, because the child reminds them every day. In that sense the stepparent lives closer to the truth about parenthood than the rest of us. All of us are guardians of children who were never finally ours.

This is the distinction the whole question turns on. There is a difference between taking a place and filling a role. A stepparent who tries to take the place of the absent parent will fail, because the place isn't vacant, even when the parent is dead. The child's love for that parent stays, and the attempt to fill it is experienced as an attack. A stepparent who fills a role, the adult who cooks, drives, listens, sits through the recital, keeps the marriage steady and keeps faith with the child whether or not the child keeps faith back, adds something to the child's life without subtracting anything. A child can have one father and one stepfather, one mother and one stepmother, without a contest among them. The addition only works when the stepparent stops treating it as a competition.

## The other house

The hardest person in many stepfamilies is not the child. It's the other parent, and the other household with its different rules, different bedtimes and different account of what happened in the divorce. Stepparents often feel the other parent's presence as a threat, and some other parents mean it as one.

The child has an obligation here that the adults should protect. "Honor your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12) does not lapse when parents divorce, and a stepparent who speaks with contempt about the child's other parent is asking the child to break a commandment in order to please the adult at the dinner table. Children hear it that way even when no one intends it. They also know, at some level, that they are half made of the person being criticized. A stepmother who says the mother is irresponsible is telling the girl something about herself. The discipline required is simple to state and very hard to keep: say nothing about the other parent in the child's hearing that you would not say if that parent were in the room.

Paul's counsel to the Romans was written for a church, not a divided family, but it fits: "If possible, so far as it depends on you, live peaceably with all" (Romans 12:18). The verse has two qualifiers, and both matter. "If possible" admits that peace is sometimes impossible. "So far as it depends on you" limits the stepparent's responsibility to what is actually within reach. You cannot make an ex-spouse cooperative. You can refuse to escalate, communicate about logistics plainly, keep the child out of the middle and let the other house be different without treating the difference as war.

Sometimes the other house is not simply different but unsafe, or the other parent is absent in a way that leaves a wound. Then the stepparent is not a rival but a stabilizer, and the task is to be steady without disparaging. A child whose father has disappeared needs to grieve that father, and a stepfather who says "you don't need him, you have me" has taken the grief away from the child who needs to feel it.

The couple themselves carry their own history into this. For many Christians the question of remarriage after divorce is not settled, and some carry guilt about the end of a first marriage alongside the work of a second. That question is treated at length in [What Does the Bible Say About Divorce and Remarriage?](/writing/divorce-and-remarriage), and it shouldn't be relitigated at the dinner table. Where there is real sin in the story, confession and repentance are the way forward, and they are not a reason to treat a present marriage as second-class. The vows made in a second marriage are real vows. God holds people to them, and so should the church.

## When it isn't working, and when it isn't safe

Honesty requires two harder words. The first is that second marriages with children are more fragile than first marriages. Hetherington's long study found that remarriages involving stepchildren faced a higher risk of ending, especially in the early years, and most researchers who study stepfamilies say the same. The reasons are mostly the ones already described: no script, divided loyalties, disputes about discipline, and couples who expected a blended household to feel like a first family and read their disappointment as proof of a mistake. A couple in the second or third year of a stepfamily that feels like a war zone should know that this is common, that it often improves, and that help exists. Counselors who specialize in stepfamilies are worth seeking out, because well-meaning advice designed for first families often makes things worse.

The second word is about safety, and it must be said carefully. In *The Truth about Cinderella* (1998), the psychologists Martin Daly and Margo Wilson reported that young children living with a stepparent were at substantially higher risk of serious abuse than children living with both biological parents. Their evolutionary explanation for the finding is contested, and the finding itself concerns relative risk, not the character of stepparents in general. The great majority of stepparents never harm a child, and many give children a stability they never had. But the risk is real enough that no church and no parent should pretend it isn't. A parent bringing a new partner into a home with children has a duty to watch, to listen to what the children say and don't say, and to act if something is wrong. If you suspect a child is being abused, call the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline at 1-800-422-4453. If you are being hurt or threatened by a partner, the National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233. No teaching about the permanence of marriage requires anyone to keep a child, or themselves, in danger.

We in the church have not always helped. For a long stretch of the last century, many congregations treated remarried families with quiet suspicion, as evidence of someone's failure, and kept them at a little distance. More recently the opposite mistake has been common: churches that preach so warmly about family that blended families feel they are failing to match a picture that was never drawn for them. Pastors, and I am one, preach Mother's Day and Father's Day sermons every year that a stepmother can sit through wondering whether any of it applies to her. It usually does, and too often no one says so.

## The long table

The New Testament's own name for the church is a household built out of people who were not born into it. Paul writes to the Gentile believers in Ephesus, who had been "strangers to the covenants of promise" (Ephesians 2:12), and tells them: "So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19). The church is a family of people with different origins, different first loyalties and different memories, joined not by blood but by a covenant made on their behalf. It took the early church decades of hard argument, recorded across Acts and Paul's letters, to learn to eat at one table. They did not blend quickly either.

A stepfamily is a small version of that household, and it is held together by the same kind of love. It is love given before it's returned, often for a long time without being returned at all. The stepmother at the counter, making the second batch of pasta, is doing something that looks small and is not. She is loving a child who has no obligation to love her back and who may not for years. That is what God did for every one of us. "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). The love came first. The response came later, if it came at all.

That doesn't make stepparenting easy, and nothing in this essay should be read as a promise that it will turn out well. Some stepchildren grow into adults who call their stepfather the one who stayed. Some keep their distance for life. The outcome is not in the stepparent's control, and a stepparent who needs a particular outcome in order to keep going will eventually stop. What is in the stepparent's control is the faithfulness itself: sitting through the recital, keeping quiet about the other parent, letting the parent discipline, making the pasta again.

Joseph never heard the boy he raised preach the Sermon on the Mount. By the time Jesus began his ministry, Joseph seems to have died; he drops out of the story, and the Gospels never mention his death. He spent his life raising a son whose calling he could not fully understand and whose deepest loyalty belonged to someone else, and then he was gone before the meaning of it all came clear. The stepparent who wants to know whether it was worth it may not find out in this life either. What they will have done is taken a child who came to them through someone else's loss and kept faith with that child without trying to erase the loss. It is a slow and largely unseen work, and the eleven-year-old who set the table anyway may be the only one who knows, years from now, what it cost.
