Picture a young husband standing in his driveway on a Sunday afternoon, phone to his ear, while his wife waits inside. His mother is explaining, not for the first time, why the baby shouldn't be sleeping in that room, and why the two of them should really come for the whole week at Christmas, since his sister's family always does. He says something vague. He promises to talk to his wife. When he goes inside, his wife can tell from his face what happened, and she feels something she can't quite put into words: that in the contest between his mother and her, she has lost again, and he didn't even notice there was a contest.

Or picture it the other way around. A wife whose father still helps with the mortgage, and who therefore still has opinions about where they live, what they drive and whether her husband's job is good enough. Her husband feels judged every time they visit. She feels caught between two men she loves, and she can't see how to stop being the rope in their tug of war.

In-law trouble is among the oldest strains on a marriage and among the most common. When couples go looking for help with it, they tend to find one of two answers. The first, from the wider culture, is to set boundaries, and if the parents won't respect them, to cut the toxic people out of your life. The second, from many churches, is a phrase: leave and cleave. It's usually offered as though it settles things, and it's often used by one spouse as a weapon against the other. Both answers have something true in them. Neither quite understands what Genesis 2:24 is asking, and so neither can help the husband in the driveway know what to do.

## What Genesis 2:24 actually says

The verse comes at the end of the account of the creation of woman. The man has named the animals and found no fitting partner among them. God forms the woman and brings her to him, and the man breaks into the first poetry in the Bible: "This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh" (Genesis 2:23). Then the narrator steps back and comments: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24).

The first thing to notice is how strange this would have sounded in the world where it was first read. Ancient Israel, like most societies around it, was patrilocal. When a couple married, it was usually the bride who left her father's household and joined her husband's family, often living among his relatives on his family's land. Rebekah left her family to come to Isaac. Rachel and Leah went with Jacob. Yet Genesis says it is the man who leaves. Commentators such as Gordon Wenham have noted that the verse can't be describing a change of address, since in practice men usually didn't move. It is describing a change of loyalty. Even a man who stays on his father's land is to understand that his first allegiance is no longer to his father's house but to his wife.

The second thing to notice is the language. Walter Brueggemann argued in a 1970 article that the man's cry, "bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh," echoes a covenant formula found elsewhere in Scripture. When the tribes of Israel come to David at Hebron to make him king, they say, "Behold, we are your bone and flesh" (2 Samuel 5:1). The phrase is a pledge of loyalty, not only a statement of kinship. The verbs of Genesis 2:24 point in the same direction. The word translated "hold fast" is the same word Moses uses for Israel's loyalty to God: "You shall fear the LORD your God. You shall serve him and hold fast to him" (Deuteronomy 10:20). It is also the word used of Ruth, who refused to leave her mother-in-law: "Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth clung to her" (Ruth 1:14). Victor Hamilton and others have observed that "leave" and "hold fast" both belong to the vocabulary of covenant. To leave is to relinquish one primary loyalty. To hold fast is to take up another.

Jesus quoted this verse when he was asked about divorce, and he added a line of his own: "So they are no longer two but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (Matthew 19:6). The context was a debate about husbands putting away their wives, but the principle reaches further. God is the one who joins a husband and wife, and no one, whether a rival, an employer or a parent, has the right to pull them apart.

So "leave and cleave" is not a command to move away from your parents, or to stop caring for them, or to treat them as strangers. It is a covenantal reordering of loyalty. The spouse is now first. The parents are still loved and honored, but they're no longer the ones whose judgment governs the new household. The husband in the driveway doesn't need to stop talking to his mother. He needs to know, and to show, that his wife is the one he has promised to hold fast to.

## Honor that outlasts the leaving

If Genesis 2:24 were the only verse on the subject, it might be read as permission to leave parents behind altogether. But Scripture never lets the leaving cancel the honoring. "Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the land that the LORD your God is giving you" (Exodus 20:12). The fifth commandment is addressed to the whole people of Israel, adults included, and much of its force falls on grown children whose parents are aging and whose care now depends on them.

Jesus took this seriously. In Mark 7:10–13 he rebuked the Pharisees for a practice that allowed a grown son to declare his property "Corban," dedicated to God, and so avoid supporting his aging parents. Jesus said that such a tradition made void the word of God. Honoring parents, in his reading, was not a sentiment but a duty with material weight. Paul says the same: grown children and grandchildren should "learn to show godliness to their own household and to make some return to their parents, for this is pleasing in the sight of God" (1 Timothy 5:4). Martin Luther, expounding this commandment (the fourth in his numbering) in the *Large Catechism* (1529), insisted that honor includes not only respect but care, providing for parents when they are old, sick or poor, and treating them with gratitude for what they gave.

The distinction that makes sense of all this is between honor and obedience. "Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right. 'Honor your father and mother'" (Ephesians 6:1–2). Paul addresses obedience to children, still in their parents' household and under their authority. Honor lasts a lifetime. Obedience ends when a child leaves to form a new household. A married adult owes their parents respect, gratitude, care and a listening ear, but not obedience. Their parents' wishes about where they live, how they raise their children or how they spend their holidays are to be heard with respect and weighed with love, but they no longer settle the question. The new household decides.

Many in-law conflicts come from confusing these two. Sometimes the parents confuse them, expecting the obedience they received when their child was twelve. Sometimes the married child confuses them, treating every parental suggestion as either a command to be obeyed or an intrusion to be repelled, when it might be neither. A mother who says the baby should sleep in another room is offering an opinion. She may be right or wrong. She is owed a respectful hearing. She isn't owed compliance.

## In-laws in Scripture

It's worth noticing how much of the Bible's family drama involves in-laws, and how varied it is. Scripture doesn't offer a single model of the in-law relationship. It shows the whole range, from the tender to the destructive.

The most famous words of loyalty in Scripture, often read at weddings, were spoken by a daughter-in-law to her mother-in-law. "For where you go I will go, and where you lodge I will lodge. Your people shall be my people, and your God my God" (Ruth 1:16). Ruth was a Moabite widow. Her husband was dead, and she had every reason to go home. Instead she bound herself to Naomi, and that bond became the means by which God brought her into Israel. She became the great-grandmother of David, and Matthew names her in the genealogy of Jesus. The in-law relationship, in that story, is the channel of redemption.

Moses had a father-in-law who gave him the best advice of his life. When Jethro watched Moses judging the people alone from morning to evening, he said: "What you are doing is not good. You and the people with you will certainly wear yourselves out, for the thing is too heavy for you. You are not able to do it alone" (Exodus 18:17–18). And "Moses listened to the voice of his father-in-law and did all that he had said" (Exodus 18:24). Then comes a line that deserves more attention than it gets: "Then Moses let his father-in-law depart, and he went away to his own country" (Exodus 18:27). Jethro gave his counsel, it was received, and he went home. That's what good in-law advice looks like: offered with wisdom, received with humility, and not followed by a permanent residence.

Other stories run darker. Laban deceived Jacob on his wedding night and then exploited him for twenty years. Jacob told his wives, "your father has cheated me and changed my wages ten times" (Genesis 31:7). Saul gave his daughter Michal to David in marriage while plotting to have him killed. The tension ran the other way too. When Esau married two Hittite women, "they made life bitter for Isaac and Rebekah" (Genesis 26:34–35). Scripture doesn't pretend that in-laws are always wise or that children-in-law are always gracious. It tells the truth about both.

## How the question changed

Christians today face this question in a particular setting, and the history helps explain why it feels so charged. The historian Peter Laslett showed in *The World We Have Lost* (1965) that most households in pre-industrial England were nuclear, a married couple and their children, and that people married relatively late. The great extended households of nostalgia were less common than people assumed. But those nuclear households usually sat within a dense web of relatives and neighbors who lived nearby and shared the work of life. Couples lived apart from their parents, but not far from them.

Industrialization, and then the suburban mobility of the twentieth century, pulled that web apart. By the middle of the century, it was common for a young couple to live hundreds of miles from either set of parents, raising their children with no grandparents nearby. David Brooks argued in "The Nuclear Family Was a Mistake," published in *The Atlantic* in 2020, that the detached nuclear family of the 1950s and 1960s was a brief historical anomaly, and that the more resilient pattern across history has been extended families and networks of kin who share the burdens of raising children and caring for the old. Laslett and Brooks don't entirely agree, but together they show something important. Married couples have usually lived in their own households, and they have also usually lived close to family who helped. Modern couples often have the independence without the help.

That leaves the in-law relationship in an odd position. Distance makes every visit an event, heavy with expectation, where a week of close quarters is packed with years of unresolved feeling. It also means that when couples do live near their parents, they sometimes lack any shared idea of how that closeness should work. The psychologist Terri Apter, in *What Do You Want from Me?* (2009), found that in-law tension is common and often intense, and that the most difficult of these relationships is usually the one between a mother-in-law and a daughter-in-law, two women each trying to secure her place in a family they now share. She also found that husbands often failed to notice the strain at all.

It must be said, too, that Western Christians have sometimes read Genesis 2:24 through the lens of American individualism. Many cultures in Africa, Asia and Latin America, and many immigrant families in the West, expect far closer ties between married children and their parents, including shared homes, shared finances and a strong voice for elders in family decisions. Christians from those backgrounds sometimes read the fifth commandment more faithfully than Western Christians do. The leaving Genesis describes isn't a license for independence as the modern West imagines it. It is a reordering of loyalty that can be lived out in a multigenerational household as faithfully as in a suburb three states away. The question isn't how far you live from your parents. The question is who holds first place in your commitments.

The family systems theorist Murray Bowen offered a useful way to think about this in *Family Therapy in Clinical Practice* (1978). He described the goal of healthy development as differentiation of self: the ability to remain emotionally connected to one's family while still thinking and choosing as a distinct person. He contrasted it with two failures. One is fusion, in which a person can't separate their own feelings and decisions from their family's. The other is what he called emotional cutoff, in which a person tries to escape their family's influence by distance or silence. Bowen argued that cutoff is not the opposite of fusion but another form of it, since the person who has to flee their family is still being governed by it. Genesis 2:24 describes something like differentiation. The married child neither stays fused to the parents nor cuts them off. They leave, and they keep honoring.

## When the trouble is the parent, and when it's the spouse

In practice, most in-law conflict falls into one of a few patterns, and each calls for a different response.

Sometimes the problem is a parent who has not accepted the leaving. They call daily, give unsolicited advice on every decision, arrive without notice, or use money to keep a hand on the wheel. In these cases the wisest principle is usually that each spouse handles their own parents. The husband in the driveway should be the one to speak to his mother, kindly and plainly, not leave his wife to do it or make her the villain. Something like: we've decided, we love you, and we'd like you to respect it. Decisions about the household should be made by the couple first, then communicated together, so that no parent can divide them. If financial help comes with strings, the couple may need to decline it, even at real cost, because a gift that buys a vote in the household isn't a gift.

Sometimes, though, the problem is not the parent but the spouse who has not left. A husband who still calls his mother before he talks to his wife, a wife who still takes her father's side in every disagreement, a spouse who can't bear to disappoint a parent and so disappoints their partner instead. In these cases, the in-law's behavior may not be the real issue at all. The issue is a married adult who hasn't yet made the transfer of loyalty that Genesis describes. The spouse on the other side of that may need to say it gently and clearly: I need to know that I come first. And the spouse who hears it needs to take it seriously, not defensively, because they are being asked to keep the promise they made.

Sometimes the trouble is that one spouse's family is harmful. A parent may be abusive, addicted, manipulative or cruel, or may have been so during the spouse's childhood. Honor doesn't require submitting yourself or your children to harm. It can mean praying for a parent, speaking respectfully of them, meeting their basic needs if they are destitute, and still limiting contact, refusing to leave children alone with them, or stepping back entirely for a season. That is not a failure of the fifth commandment. It is a recognition that honor and safety can coexist, and that protecting the vulnerable is also a biblical duty. If a parent or in-law is threatening or harming anyone in your household, the National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233, and elder or child abuse can be reported to local authorities. A pastor or counselor can help you think through the harder decisions.

And sometimes the question is care. Many couples in middle age find themselves caring for aging parents while still raising children, and the question becomes not how to keep parents at a distance but how to bring them closer without losing the marriage. Taking an aging parent into the home can be a profound act of honor. It should also be a decision the couple makes together, with the spouse who isn't that parent's child given a full and equal voice. A marriage that absorbs a parent without both spouses' real consent will strain under a load one of them never agreed to carry. This site's essay on [honoring parents as they grow old](/writing/elder-abuse-invisible) takes up the harder end of that duty.

## The parents' leaving

Almost everything written about Genesis 2:24 addresses the married couple. But the verse makes a demand on the parents as well. If a son is to leave his father and his mother, then his father and mother have to let him go. For many parents, that is the harder part. They have spent twenty years or more at the center of their child's life, and now they must accept a quieter place: honored, loved, sometimes consulted, but no longer first.

The Gospels show this with unusual tenderness in the life of Jesus and his mother. When Jesus was twelve, his parents lost him in Jerusalem and found him in the temple after three days. "Did you not know," he asked, "that I must be in my Father's house?" (Luke 2:49). Even as a boy, he had a prior loyalty that his mother could not command. Yet the same passage says, "he went down with them and came to Nazareth and was submissive to them. And his mother treasured up all these things in her heart" (Luke 2:51). He honored her, and he obeyed while he was in her house, and his first allegiance was never hers.

At the end, from the cross, he made provision for her. "When Jesus saw his mother and the disciple whom he loved standing nearby, he said to his mother, 'Woman, behold, your son!' Then he said to the disciple, 'Behold, your mother!' And from that hour the disciple took her to his own home" (John 19:26–27). In the hour of his death, finishing what his Father had given him to do, he still cared for his mother's future. He had left, as every son must. He never stopped honoring.

Mary's part in this is instructive for every parent of a married child. She had to learn, over thirty years, that her son didn't belong to her. At Cana she learned it gently. At the cross she learned it terribly. Parents of married children are asked to make some version of the same surrender. The child they raised now belongs first to a spouse, and beyond the spouse, to God. The best in-laws are those who have made that surrender with grace, and so can offer counsel like Jethro, love like Naomi, and a home that their married children are glad to visit because it no longer tries to hold them.

Jesus once said of those who followed him, "whoever does the will of God, he is my brother and sister and mother" (Mark 3:35). The family of God relativizes every natural family, and it also gathers every family in. A mother-in-law and a daughter-in-law who both belong to Christ are sisters before they are rivals, and a son who has left his father's house has not left his Father's. The husband in the driveway doesn't have to choose between his mother and his wife. He has to put them in their right order, and trust that the love he owes each of them is better kept in that order than out of it. The harder thing he'll carry back into the house is the knowledge that the leaving is his to do, and that he hasn't finished it yet.
