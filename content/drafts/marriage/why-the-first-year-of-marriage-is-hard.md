Picture two people eight months into their marriage, standing in a small kitchen at eleven at night, arguing about a dishwasher. He loads it one way. She loads it another. The fight isn't really about the dishwasher, and both of them half know it, but neither can say what it is about. Later, lying awake, one of them thinks a sentence they would never say out loud: maybe we made a mistake. Everyone told them the first year would be hard. No one told them it would feel like this, or why.

Newlyweds hear two explanations, and both are partly true. The first comes from the culture: if it's this hard already, maybe you aren't compatible, and it is better to find out now than later. The second comes from well-meaning Christians: marriage takes work, so keep at it. The first explanation turns ordinary friction into a verdict. The second is right but thin, because it never says what the work is or why it's necessary. A couple told only to work harder doesn't know what they are working on.

The first year of marriage is hard for reasons that are neither a sign of failure nor a mystery. Two people are building one shared world out of two separate ones, and every small disagreement is a negotiation over whose world counts as normal. At the same time, each of them is slowly losing the spouse they imagined in order to find the one they actually married. Both of those losses are painful, and both are necessary. The couple in the kitchen is not in trouble because they are fighting about a dishwasher. They are in the middle of becoming married, which takes longer than a wedding.

## The year the Law set apart

There is a verse in Deuteronomy that almost no one preaches at weddings, and it ought to be read at every one. "When a man is newly married, he shall not go out with the army or be liable for any other public duty. He shall be free at home one year to be happy with his wife whom he has taken" (Deuteronomy 24:5). It sits among laws about loans, wages and the treatment of the poor, and like them it protects something vulnerable from a community that might otherwise overlook it.

Read in its setting, the law is startling. Israel was a small nation surrounded by enemies, and every able-bodied man mattered to its defense. Yet the Law exempted newly married men from military service and from other public obligations for an entire year. The community absorbed the cost so that a new marriage could take root. The Hebrew verb behind "be happy" can also carry the sense of making someone glad, which is why translations such as the NIV render it "bring happiness to the wife he has married." Either way, the first year was treated as a task in its own right, too important to be squeezed between other duties.

Compare that to what we usually give newlyweds: a week or two away, and then a return to full schedules, demanding jobs, and a church that is glad to have another couple to serve in the nursery. We treat the wedding as the finish line and the marriage as something that will run on its own momentum. The Law of Moses assumed the opposite. It assumed that a new marriage is fragile, that it needs time and attention to become what it will be, and that the community around it has a responsibility to protect that time. If the first year feels harder than you expected, one reason is that almost nobody around you has treated it as the serious, set-apart work it is.

That should bring some relief to newlyweds who feel they're failing. The difficulty they feel isn't necessarily a sign that something is wrong with their marriage. It may be a sign that they are doing hard work without the protection that work once had. It also puts a question to the rest of us, especially pastors and parents and older couples: what would it look like for the church to take this law's wisdom seriously, even if we can't exempt anyone from the army?

## Building one world out of two

In 1964 the sociologists Peter Berger and Hansfried Kellner published an essay called "Marriage and the Construction of Reality." "Marriage in our society," they wrote, "is a dramatic act in which two strangers come together and redefine themselves." Each person brings a whole world, formed by family, friends, habits and assumptions, and through an ongoing conversation that never really stops, the two gradually build a single shared world. Most of this happens without either person noticing. It happens through thousands of small conversations about how things are done.

That's the key to the dishwasher. Every household runs on unwritten rules about how the dishes go, when the bills get paid, how loud a disagreement can get, whether shoes come off at the door, what a Saturday morning is for. Each spouse grew up with one set of rules and absorbed them as simply the way things are. In the first year, those rules collide constantly, and each collision feels less like a difference of preference than like the other person doing it wrong. When you fight about the dishwasher, you are really negotiating whose idea of normal will govern the home you now share. That's why such small things can feel so large.

Martin Luther, who married Katharina von Bora in June 1525 when he was forty-one, is remembered in his *Table Talk* as saying something to the effect that the first year of marriage brings a great deal to get used to, that a man wakes up and finds a pair of pigtails on the pillow that were not there before. It's a small joke, but it names something true. Marriage asks two people who have each lived as the center of their own world to wake up, every day, beside someone who is the center of a different one.

Several things make this construction harder now than it once was. Couples tend to marry later than their grandparents did, often after years of living on their own, with habits that have had time to set. They frequently live far from the families and older friends who once helped newlyweds make sense of their disagreements. And many arrive at marriage with little experience of what an ordinary, long, imperfect marriage actually looks like up close. The world they are trying to build together has fewer models and fewer helpers than it used to, and more of the work falls on two tired people in a kitchen at night.

Genesis describes the result of this construction, not its ease: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). Notice the verb *become*. One flesh is not a state two people enter at the altar. It is something they grow into. The leaving and holding fast take time, and the becoming takes longer still. The first year is when most of the leaving happens, and it rarely feels graceful.

## The spouse you imagined and the one you married

The second reason the first year is hard goes deeper, and it is more painful to admit. Almost everyone marries a person partly imagined. Before the wedding, we know our spouse well but not fully, and we fill in the gaps with hopes. We assume he'll be as attentive after the wedding as he was during the courtship. We assume she'll love the things we love, or come around to them. We assume the flaws we notice are small and will fade. Then, in the first year, the imagined spouse slowly gives way to the real one, and the real one is always more complicated, more stubborn and more separate from us than we expected.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote about this dynamic in *Life Together* (1939), though he was writing about Christian community, not marriage. He warned against what he called the "wish dream," the idealized picture of community that people bring with them and try to impose on others. He argued that such dreams must be shattered for real community to begin, and that the sooner the disillusionment comes, the better. The person who loves his dream of community more than the actual community, he warned, will end by destroying it. The same thing is true in a marriage. If you love your picture of your spouse more than your actual spouse, you will spend the marriage trying to make the real person conform to the picture, and both of you will be miserable.

Disillusionment sounds like a bad thing, but it simply means the loss of an illusion. That loss hurts, and it is also the beginning of seeing clearly. The spouse you imagined was, in part, a projection of your own needs. The spouse you actually married is a real person, made in the image of God, with a history and a will of their own, and they're far more interesting than your projection could ever have been. Keller makes a related point in *The Meaning of Marriage* (2011): marriage brings you face to face with someone who is in some ways a stranger, and the work of love is to keep discovering and loving that person as they actually are.

Eli Finkel, a psychologist at Northwestern, sharpened the cultural side of this in *The All-or-Nothing Marriage* (2017). He describes American marriage as moving through three eras: an institutional model focused on survival and economic partnership, a companionate model centered on love and domestic roles, and, since roughly 1965, a self-expressive model in which people look to marriage for personal growth and self-discovery. The self-expressive marriage can be richer than anything before it, he argues, but it asks far more of each spouse, and many couples expect more from marriage than they have the time or energy to invest. Newlyweds often carry exactly these expectations. They want their spouse to be best friend, lover, co-parent, therapist and partner in self-realization, all at once and right away. When the real spouse turns out to be only a person, the disappointment can feel like a catastrophe.

Here, though, an honest caution is needed. Not every hard discovery in the first year is simply the loss of an illusion. Sometimes a spouse turns out to have hidden an addiction, a mountain of debt or a pattern of deception. Sometimes a marriage that was affectionate during the engagement becomes controlling, frightening or violent after the wedding. Those aren't the ordinary difficulties of building a shared life, and they shouldn't be treated as though patience alone will fix them. If you are afraid of your spouse, if you are being threatened, hit, controlled or cut off from the people who love you, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is available at 1-800-799-7233. Scripture's call to faithfulness in marriage never asks anyone to endure abuse. And if a spouse's addiction has come to light, the SAMHSA National Helpline answers at 1-800-662-4357. Knowing the difference between a hard marriage and a dangerous one is part of wisdom, and asking for help is not a betrayal of your vows.

## What the first year exposes in you

There is another reason the first year is hard, and it points inward. Most of us enter marriage with a quiet assumption that we'll be easy to live with. We know our spouse has flaws, and we're prepared to be gracious about them. We are much less prepared for what marriage reveals about us. Nothing exposes selfishness like sharing a bathroom, a bank account and a bed with someone whose needs are constantly present. Many newlyweds discover, within months, that they are more impatient, more controlling, more defensive and more self-absorbed than they had any idea.

The book of James asks the question every married couple eventually faces: "What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you? Is it not this, that your passions are at war within you?" (James 4:1). The fight about the dishwasher is not only about two different worlds colliding. It is also about two hearts that each want their own way and feel entitled to it. Marriage doesn't create that selfishness. It reveals it, by removing the distance that let it hide.

Augustine gave the church a way of thinking about this that still helps. In *The City of God* he describes virtue as rightly ordered love, the loving of each thing in its proper place. The problem with human beings, he argues, is rarely that we love bad things. It is that we love good things in the wrong order, putting lesser goods above greater ones and ourselves above everything. A newlywed who loves their spouse chiefly as a means to their own happiness has a disordered love, even if the feeling is intense. When the spouse fails to deliver that happiness, the love curdles into resentment, and the resentment feels justified.

Gary Thomas's *Sacred Marriage* (2000) put this question in its subtitle: what if God designed marriage to make us holy more than to make us happy? The line has become familiar, and it is sometimes used to dismiss real unhappiness in a way Thomas didn't intend. But the insight holds. The first year isn't only a trial to be survived. It is often the first sustained look a person gets at their own heart. For a Christian, that is a gift, even when it's unpleasant, because the self you can see is the self you can bring to God for repentance and healing.

This cuts in every direction at once, and it should. It indicts the spouse who is sure the problem is the other person. It indicts the older Christians who told the couple the first year is the hardest and then offered no help. It indicts those of us who preach, because the church has often prepared couples for a wedding far more carefully than for a marriage. None of us is standing outside the kitchen. We have all made the same discovery about ourselves, or we are still avoiding it.

## Learning to be married

So what does the first year ask of a couple, practically? There's no formula for it, but there are habits that let the shared world get built without either spouse being crushed in the building.

It begins with learning to repair. Couples in their first year will fight, and the goal isn't to stop fighting but to learn how to come back together afterward. (This site has a longer essay on [what healthy conflict in marriage requires](/writing/healthy-conflict-marriage).) That means learning to apologize specifically, without the word "but" attached. It means learning to forgive without keeping a ledger. And it means learning to notice when a fight is about something deeper than the thing being fought about, the dishwasher standing in for a fear of not being respected, or the holiday plans standing in for a question of loyalty. Paul describes the life of a Christian community in terms that fit a new marriage exactly: "Put on then, as God's chosen ones, holy and beloved, compassionate hearts, kindness, humility, meekness, and patience, bearing with one another and, if one has a complaint against another, forgiving each other; as the Lord has forgiven you, so you also must forgive. And above all these put on love, which binds everything together in perfect harmony" (Colossians 3:12–14).

The second habit is negotiating the shared world deliberately rather than by attrition. Many of the collisions of the first year can be defused simply by naming them. Each of you can say how things were done in the home you grew up in and why it matters to you, and then the two of you can decide together how they will be done in yours. Some things will be one person's way, some the other's, and some will be a new way that belongs to neither family. This is the ongoing conversation Berger and Kellner described, made conscious.

Patience with the body belongs here too. Many couples, especially those who waited for marriage, discover that their sexual relationship is awkward, uncertain or disappointing at first. That is normal and not a sign of failure. The one-flesh union Genesis describes, "the man and his wife were both naked and were not ashamed" (Genesis 2:25), is something couples grow into with tenderness, humor and patience. If there is pain, fear or a history that makes closeness frightening, a physician and a trained counselor can help, and this is not medical advice, only an encouragement not to suffer silently.

Then there is the matter of protecting the marriage from the demands that would crowd it out. Deuteronomy's newlywed was free from the army for a year. Modern couples can't opt out of their jobs, but they can decline extra obligations, guard some evenings, and be slow to take on commitments that would leave the marriage with only their leftover energy. They can also ask for help early, from an older couple they trust, a pastor, or a counselor, not in year seven when the patterns have hardened but in the first year while everything is still being formed. Asking for help at the beginning isn't a sign that your marriage is weak. It is a sign that you're taking it seriously.

Last, and easiest to neglect, is friendship. Many couples discover in the first year that they were better at being in love than at being friends. Friendship is the part of marriage that makes the ordinary days bearable: the shared jokes, the shared interests, the ability to be quiet together. It grows through time spent doing things side by side, not only through conversations about the relationship. Couples who make room for play in their first year tend to find that the hard conversations get easier, because they are having them with someone who is on their side.

## When the wine runs out

The first sign Jesus performed, according to John's Gospel, happened at a wedding. The celebration at Cana was in full swing when the wine ran out, a serious embarrassment in a culture where hospitality was a matter of honor. Jesus turned water into wine, and the master of the feast, not knowing where it had come from, told the bridegroom: "Everyone serves the good wine first, and when people have drunk freely, then the poor wine. But you have kept the good wine until now" (John 2:10).

Most newlyweds experience the first year as the moment the wine runs out. The feelings that carried them through the engagement and the wedding begin to thin. The celebration ends and the ordinary life begins, and it can feel like a letdown, even a loss. The culture tells them this is where the good part of love ends. Everything after is compromise and duty, a lesser wine served once no one can tell the difference.

The Gospel tells a different story. At Cana, the best wine came after the first had run out, and it came from Jesus, not from the resources of the bridegroom. Kierkegaard gave this intuition a philosophical form in *Either/Or* (1843). His character Judge William argues against the young aesthete, who believes romantic love is beautiful only while it's new and must die when it becomes daily. The Judge insists that marriage does not kill first love but takes it up into the resolve of a lifelong promise, where it can deepen and endure in ways that mere romance cannot. The Judge was right, and Cana is why. The feelings of the first months are real and good, but they are the first wine, and it's always the one that runs out. What comes after is not necessarily worse. It can be better, but only if the couple lets their marriage become more than the feelings that started it.

That doesn't make the first year easy. The couple in the kitchen will still have to finish their argument, apologize, go to bed, and wake up to try again. They will still have to lose the spouse they imagined and learn to love the one they married. They'll still have to discover, painfully, how selfish they both are. But they don't have to believe that the running out of the first wine is the end of the feast. It's often where the real one begins. The question every newlywed eventually faces is whether they'll keep drinking from what they brought, or let the Lord of that wedding give them what they cannot make for themselves.
