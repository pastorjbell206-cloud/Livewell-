You wake up one morning and the person beside you is a stranger.

Not literally. The face is the same, the coffee order is the same, the side of the bed has not changed in eleven years. But something underneath has shifted, and you cannot name when it moved. The convictions you fell for have softened or hardened into shapes you did not agree to. The laugh comes less. The opinions you used to predict now surprise you, and not the way they once did. You look across the kitchen and think the quiet thought almost everyone in a long marriage eventually thinks: I do not know who this is.

This is the moment most people misread.

The instinct is to treat it as discovery. As though the mask finally slipped and the real person stepped forward, and the real person is a disappointment. We say it that way to friends. *I feel like I never really knew them.* We make it a story about deception, theirs or our own, because that story lets us off the hook. If they changed into someone else, then the contract was void from the start, and grief becomes grievance, which is so much easier to carry. Grievance has a target. Grief just sits in your chest.

But change is not betrayal. Change is the thing you actually promised to stay for.

Consider what the vow assumes. *For better, for worse.* We hear that and imagine circumstance — money, sickness, the house, the diagnosis. We rarely hear it as a promise about the person. Yet the vow is built on a fact every honest theologian and every long-married couple eventually admits: the person you marry will not exist in twenty years. They will be replaced, cell by conviction by wound by joy, by someone the present version is busy becoming. You did not marry a fixed object. You married a person, and persons are verbs.

I have watched couples in my office sit eighteen inches apart and describe two different marriages. One mourns the spouse who used to be ambitious. One mourns the spouse who used to be present. Each has built a museum to a version of the other that no longer breathes, and each treats the living person in the chair as a forgery of the exhibit. They are not lying. They are grieving. They simply do not know that what they are grieving is the cost of being married to a human being instead of a photograph.

Scripture is not romantic about this. When Paul writes that love "bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things," he is not describing a feeling that survives change. He is describing a will that outlasts it. The Greek word for endure, *hypomenei*, is a military word. It means to hold the position. It does not mean to enjoy the position. Paul is telling a fractious church in Corinth — a church full of people sleeping with the wrong people and suing the right ones — that love is what stays when the reasons leave.

We have inherited the opposite gospel. The culture we breathe says the self is sovereign and discovery is sacred, that the highest loyalty is to your own becoming, and that any covenant which constrains that becoming is a cage. Charles Taylor named this the age of authenticity, the conviction that each of us has an original way of being human we are obligated to find and express. It is not all wrong. But it is acid on a marriage, because it teaches both spouses that the deepest betrayal is staying the same and the deepest virtue is leaving when you have outgrown the room. So they change, and they change apart, and they call the wreckage growth.

For years I did not understand the thing I now believe with my whole life. The stranger across the table is not the problem to be solved. The stranger is the marriage.

A covenant is not a promise to two people. It is a promise to every person they will become. When God cuts covenant with Abram in Genesis 15, He does not bind Himself to the man Abram was — the liar who handed his wife to Pharaoh, the doubter who laughed. He binds Himself to Abraham, to Israel, to a thousand generations not yet born and most of them faithless. The covenant precedes the people it will hold. That is the scandal of it. God says *I am yours* before He has met who you will be, and He does not flinch when you arrive as someone worse.

So the question changes. It is no longer *do I still recognize this person.* It is *am I willing to be married to who they are now, and not who they were when it was easy.*

That is a harder question, and it indicts me first.

Because the truth I avoid is that I also became a stranger. While I was cataloging the ways my spouse changed, I was changing — slower in some rooms, harder in others, more tired, more certain, less curious about the person I had stopped studying. The marriage did not have one mystery in it. It had two. And the one who feels abandoned by the other's transformation has almost always quietly transformed into someone who stopped paying attention. We grow apart in the dark, both of us, and then blame the one we can see.

I have seen this go the other way, and it is worth describing, because it is the only proof that the bleak can be sealed. The couples who survive the becoming-strangers are not the ones who changed less. They are the ones who treated the other's change as news rather than as a verdict. When one of them shifted — took a new conviction, lost an old certainty, grew quiet where they used to be loud — the other got curious instead of wounded. They asked about it. They followed the change into the room it came from. And by following, they were changed too, slightly, in the direction of the person they were married to, which is the only direction a marriage is allowed to grow.

The couples who do not survive it do something subtler and more human. They keep the old map and curse the new territory. They steer by a version of their spouse that has not existed for a decade, and then resent the actual person for being somewhere the map does not show. I have done this myself. I held an old picture of someone I love and was quietly offended that the living person kept failing to match it, as though the photograph were the truth and the breathing human were the counterfeit. It is a strange cruelty, to be angry at someone for being alive.

The remedy is not nostalgia. You cannot resurrect the person you married, and you should not want to; that person could not have carried what the years required. The remedy is the oldest and least romantic thing the faith offers. You learn them again. You become a student of a person you assumed you had finished reading. You ask the questions you stopped asking around year four, when you decided you already knew the answers. You let them be twenty years past the version you fell for, and you fall, if you are willing, for the one in the chair.

Eugene Peterson called pastoral work the cure of souls, the long unspectacular attention to a person over time. Marriage is the same vocation aimed at one person and pledged for life. It is not the management of a relationship. It is the cure of a soul you have been given, and who has been given yours, and neither of you is finished.

You did not marry someone who would stay the same. No one ever has. You married someone who would change, and the vow was always a bet that you could change toward each other instead of away.

The stranger in your kitchen is not evidence the marriage failed.

The stranger is the person the marriage is for.
