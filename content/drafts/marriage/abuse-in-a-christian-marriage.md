If you are in danger right now, stop reading and call 911. If you are not in immediate danger but you are afraid of your spouse, the National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233 at any hour, and you can also text START to 88788. The advocates there will not tell you what to do. They'll help you think through your safety, including how to leave safely if you decide to, because the time around leaving can be the most dangerous time of all. If you think someone checks your phone or computer, read this on a device they can't access, and clear your browsing history afterward if it's safe to do so. If you are thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988. None of what follows is legal advice; an advocate or an attorney can tell you what the law where you live allows.

That is the most important paragraph on this page. The rest is for the question underneath it.

The question usually doesn't sound like "Is my marriage abusive?" It sounds like "Am I allowed to leave?" or "Would God be angry with me?" or "What if I'm the problem?" Often it comes from a person who has been told, by a spouse and sometimes by a church, that the Bible requires them to stay, to submit, to forgive, and to keep trying. They have prayed more, apologized more, and tried harder to be the kind of wife or husband who wouldn't provoke what keeps happening, and it keeps happening. They are asking whether the God they love has really locked the door behind them.

The claim this essay makes is plain. No verse in Scripture requires anyone to stay where they are being harmed. The passages most often used to keep a victim at home say something very different when they are read in context, and the church owes an honest account of how it has used them.

## Conflict and control are not the same thing

Every marriage has conflict, and a great deal of conflict in marriage is ordinary. Two sinners share a bed, a bank account, and a set of children, and they will hurt each other. Some couples fight loudly and repair well. Some go quiet and drift. None of that is abuse, and it matters to say so, because the word loses its power to protect when it's stretched to cover every hard season.

Abuse is something else: not a fight between two people who can both speak, but a pattern in which one spouse rules the other through fear. The abusive partner isolates the other from friends and family, watches their phone and their movements, controls the money, makes rules about small things (what they wear, whom they talk to, when they sleep), and punishes any deviation, sometimes with violence and sometimes with rage, silence, or threats. The sociologist Evan Stark named this coercive control in a book of that title (2007), and his point was that the harm lies in the pattern as a whole, a theft of the other person's freedom, and not only in any single act. Some abusive marriages involve frequent physical violence. Some involve almost none. What they share is that one spouse lives under the rule of the other.

Nor is abuse, at root, a problem of anger. Lundy Bancroft, who spent years running programs for abusive men, argued in *Why Does He Do That?* (2002) that the abusive man is often calm and charming with everyone else and chooses where his rage appears. What drives it is a settled sense of entitlement. That's why the victim's efforts to be better, calmer, or more submissive never solve the problem. The problem was never in the victim's behavior. (Bancroft wrote about men because most of the people he worked with were men, and most severe abuse is committed by men. But women abuse too, and men who are abused face their own kind of shame and disbelief. If that's you, everything on this page applies to you as well.)

A simple question helps many people see where they are. Are you afraid of your spouse? Not afraid of an argument, or of disappointing them, but afraid of what they'll do? Do you plan your words and your day around keeping them from exploding? Do you find yourself hiding things from friends, not because you're ashamed of them but because you're protecting your spouse's image? Leslie Vernick, a Christian counselor who wrote *The Emotionally Destructive Marriage* (2013), describes this as the difference between a difficult marriage and a destructive one. In a difficult marriage, both spouses are sinners who hurt each other and can repair. In a destructive one, one spouse's pattern of control, deceit, or cruelty is steadily crushing the other, and the repairs never last because the one causing the harm won't face it.

## The verses used to keep you home

The most quoted verse is Malachi 2:16, usually in the wording of older translations: "I hate divorce." It has been read aloud to abused wives more times than anyone could count. Read it in context, in the ESV:

> Because the LORD was witness between you and the wife of your youth, to whom you have been faithless, though she is your companion and your wife by covenant. Did he not make them one, with a portion of the Spirit in their union? And what was the one God seeking? Godly offspring. So guard yourselves in your spirit, and let none of you be faithless to the wife of your youth. "For the man who does not love his wife but divorces her, says the LORD, the God of Israel, covers his garment with violence, says the LORD of hosts. So guard yourselves in your spirit, and do not be faithless." (Malachi 2:14–16)

The Hebrew of verse 16 is hard, and translators have rendered it more than one way. But whichever rendering you prefer, look at whom God is angry with. Malachi is preaching to men in post-exilic Judah who were discarding the wives of their youth, and God calls what they are doing violence. The prophet stands with the woman who is being treated faithlessly. To take this passage and use it to keep a woman in a house where her husband hits her is to turn God's defense of the victim into a weapon against her. It gets the text exactly backward.

The second passage is usually 1 Peter 3:1: "Likewise, wives, be subject to your own husbands, so that even if some do not obey the word, they may be won without a word by the conduct of their wives." Peter was writing to scattered and harassed believers across Asia Minor, many of them wives of unbelieving husbands in a society that expected a wife to follow her husband's gods. He is teaching Christian women how to live with integrity inside that situation, and the passage belongs to a longer section about suffering unjustly for the name of Christ. It is not a command to accept being beaten. Six verses later Peter turns to husbands: "Likewise, husbands, live with your wives in an understanding way, showing honor to the woman as the weaker vessel, since they are heirs with you of the grace of life, so that your prayers may not be hindered" (1 Peter 3:7). A husband who terrorizes his wife has made his own prayers a problem before God.

Whatever a Christian believes about headship and submission in marriage, and faithful Christians disagree about that ([What Does the Bible Say About Submission in Marriage?](/writing/what-the-bible-says-about-submission) takes the question up at length), every serious view agrees on its limits. Paul frames his instruction to wives inside a call to mutual submission, "submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ" (Ephesians 5:21), and he defines the husband's role by the cross: "Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25). Christ's headship is the headship of one who lays down his life. It is never permission to take someone else's. And when any human authority commands what God forbids, the apostles' answer is the same as it was before the Sanhedrin: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). No one is required to submit to sin.

The third argument is subtler and does even more damage: that suffering is part of the Christian life, that Jesus calls us to take up our cross, and that enduring abuse is a way of sharing in his sufferings. This confuses two different things. Scripture does call believers to suffer for Christ's sake, for righteousness and for the gospel. It never calls anyone to suffer the sin of another person for that person's convenience. Taking up your cross means following Jesus even when it costs you. It does not mean letting someone else crucify you in your own kitchen.

The fourth argument is forgiveness. You're told that a Christian must forgive, and that forgiving means taking him back. But forgiveness and reconciliation are different things. Forgiveness releases the debt, the desire to make the other person pay. Reconciliation restores a relationship, and it requires repentance that is real and proven over time. Trust, which is what you need to live safely with someone, is rebuilt only by changed conduct. You can forgive your spouse from a safe distance and never return to living with them. Jesus commanded forgiveness. He never commanded anyone to put themselves back within reach of a person who is still dangerous.

## What Scripture actually permits

If those passages don't lock the door, does Scripture say anything about leaving? It says more than most people have been told.

It permits fleeing danger. "The prudent sees danger and hides himself, but the simple go on and suffer for it" (Proverbs 22:3). When King Saul threw a spear at David, David "fled and escaped that night" (1 Samuel 19:10), and his wife Michal helped him out through a window. Saul was the Lord's anointed, and David refused to harm him, but he also refused to stay within reach. Paul escaped Damascus the same way: "I was let down in a basket through a window in the wall and escaped his hands" (2 Corinthians 11:33). When a crowd in Nazareth tried to throw Jesus off a cliff, "passing through their midst, he went away" (Luke 4:30). And Jesus told his disciples, "When they persecute you in one town, flee to the next" (Matthew 10:23). The Bible's saints ran from people who wanted to hurt them, and the Bible records it without a word of blame.

It permits separation, even under the strictest reading. Paul's most demanding sentence on marriage is this: "To the married I give this charge (not I, but the Lord): the wife should not separate from her husband (but if she does, she should remain unmarried or else be reconciled to her husband), and the husband should not divorce his wife" (1 Corinthians 7:10–11). Notice the parenthesis. Even while stating the Lord's charge against separation, Paul anticipates that a wife may separate and doesn't tell her to go back. He tells her what her status is. The Christians who hold the strictest view of divorce, who believe no one may remarry while the former spouse lives, have always read this verse as allowing separation. That means the argument that the Bible requires you to stay under the same roof as a person who is harming you has no support even from the tradition most committed to marital permanence.

Then there is 1 Corinthians 7:15, which has become central to Christian thinking about abuse. Paul is addressing a believer married to an unbeliever who wants to leave: "But if the unbelieving partner separates, let it be so. In such cases the brother or sister is not enslaved. God has called you to peace." In its first setting, the verse is about desertion by a spouse who does not share the faith. Many Christian teachers have argued that it applies by extension to a spouse who, while remaining under the same roof, has abandoned every obligation of the covenant through violence and cruelty. They point to Paul's words elsewhere that a man who does not provide for his household "has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever" (1 Timothy 5:8), and to Jesus' instruction that an unrepentant brother be treated "as a Gentile and a tax collector" (Matthew 18:17). On this reading, the abuser has departed from the marriage in everything but address, and the abused spouse is not enslaved. Others read the verse more narrowly and do not extend it this way.

David Instone-Brewer, in *Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible* (2002), makes a related argument from the Old Testament. Exodus 21:10–11 said that even a slave-wife, the least protected wife in Israel, was free to go if her husband withheld "her food, her clothing, or her marital rights." The rabbis reasoned from the lesser to the greater: if she had these rights, every wife did. Instone-Brewer argues that the New Testament assumes these obligations of care and that abuse is the gravest possible neglect of them, so that a spouse who suffers it is the victim of a broken covenant and free. His critics say he builds too much on the Gospels' silence. Both sides are serious.

It helps to be clear about what kind of disagreement this is. Whether abuse dissolves the marriage bond, and whether an abused spouse may later remarry, are second-order questions on which faithful churches differ; [What Does the Bible Say About Divorce and Remarriage?](/writing/divorce-and-remarriage) sets out the major views in their strongest form. But whether you may get yourself and your children safe is not a second-order question. It is not really a disputed question at all. Even the strictest of the historic teachings on marriage allows separation. If anyone tells you the Bible requires you to stay in danger, they are not stating a strict view. They are stating something the church's historic teaching on marriage, at its strictest, does not require.

## How the church has failed

This part has to be said with the same seriousness as the rest, because the church's failures are part of why so many people are asking this question alone.

In 1989 James and Phyllis Alsdurf published *Battered into Submission*, documenting how often pastors of that era counseled abused wives to submit more fully, pray harder, and stay. The sociologist Nancy Nason-Clark, who spent decades researching how churches respond to domestic violence, has described the silence that surrounds abuse in congregations as a kind of holy hush: a reluctance to believe, to name, or to intervene, rooted partly in a desire to protect the family and the church's reputation. Her work with Catherine Clark Kroeger, *No Place for Abuse* (2001), called the church to do better. It hasn't consistently done so.

If your church has failed you, the failure probably had a familiar shape. The pastor asked what you might be doing to provoke it. You were sent to joint counseling, where you were expected to speak honestly in front of the person you fear, and you paid for it on the drive home. You were urged to forgive quickly and return, and your reluctance was called bitterness. Or the failure looked gentler: you were told the right things, that you're not to blame and deserve safety, while the person harming you kept his place on the platform and no one called him to account. The first failure sends the victim home. The second leaves the abuser at home in the church. [Domestic Abuse and the Church](/writing/church-domestic-violence) traces how those habits formed and what a church that protects people does instead; what matters here is that none of it was the voice of God. We have sent people home to be hurt, and we have done it with a Bible open in our hands.

I say "we" deliberately. I am a pastor, and pastors are inside this indictment. It's easier to counsel reconciliation than to confront a man who might become angry at you, and it's easier to believe the calm and articulate spouse than the frightened and confused one. Every pastor who has been slow to believe a victim has chosen his own comfort over her safety, whether he knew it or not.

## What help looks like

If you're reading this and recognizing your marriage, here is what help usually involves, and none of it requires you to decide everything today.

It begins with safety, and safety begins with a plan. Judith Herman's *Trauma and Recovery* (1992), one of the foundational works on how people heal from sustained abuse, argued that recovery unfolds in stages and that the first is always establishing safety. You can't grieve, reflect, or rebuild while you're still in danger. An advocate at the Hotline, or at a local domestic violence program, can help you think through what a safety plan looks like for you: where you would go, what you'd take, how you would protect your children, how to gather documents and money quietly, and whom to tell. They can also explain protective orders and other legal protections in your area, though for your particular situation you'll want to speak to an attorney or a legal aid office. This is not legal advice.

It helps to keep a record, stored where your spouse can't find it: dates, what happened, any injuries, any threats. It helps to tell the truth to at least one person you trust, a friend, a family member, a counselor, or a pastor who has shown you they'll believe you. If your church has not believed you, that is not a verdict on your faith or your worth, and it doesn't have to be the end of your life in the church. There are pastors and churches that will believe you. Keep looking.

Counseling can help a great deal, but in an abusive marriage it should be individual counseling, with someone trained in domestic abuse, not couples counseling. Bancroft and many domestic violence advocates warn against joint sessions where there is abuse, because they treat one person's control as a shared communication problem and give the abuser a new room in which to exercise it. Your counselor's job is to help you, not to help the marriage survive at your expense.

And if your spouse says they've changed, pay attention to what they do rather than what they say. Real repentance in an abuser looks like full acknowledgment without minimizing or blaming, willingness to enter a specialized program for people who abuse rather than general counseling, submission to real accountability from people who know the whole story, no pressure on you to return or to hurry, and changed behavior sustained over a long time. A spouse who demands that you come home to prove your forgiveness has shown you that nothing has changed. Repentance is patient with the people it wronged. It doesn't make demands of them.

If you are the one who has been causing harm, and you're reading this with a sinking recognition, the way forward is not to ask your spouse for another chance. It's to tell the truth to a pastor and a counselor who will hold you accountable, to seek out a program built for people who abuse, and to let your spouse have the distance and safety they need, however long it takes. The gospel has room for you. It has no room for you to keep doing this.

## The God who hears the cry

There is one more thing to say, and it is the reason for everything above.

When Israel was enslaved in Egypt, God did not tell them that suffering under Pharaoh was a spiritual discipline, or that they should submit more cheerfully to their taskmasters so that Pharaoh might be won over. He said, "I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters. I know their sufferings" (Exodus 3:7). And then he brought them out. The central saving act of the Old Testament is a rescue from oppression. God's answer to a house of bondage was an exodus.

The Psalms know the particular grief of being harmed by someone close. Psalm 55 is the prayer of someone betrayed not by an enemy but by a companion, a familiar friend, and it describes that person with precision: "My companion stretched out his hand against his friends; he violated his covenant. His speech was smooth as butter, yet war was in his heart; his words were softer than oil, yet they were drawn swords" (Psalm 55:20–21). That's the experience of many people in abusive marriages, the smooth words in public and the drawn swords at home, and it sits in the church's prayer book. God is not embarrassed by it. He gave you words to pray it.

And the cross, which has so often been used to tell victims to endure, says the opposite of what abusers need it to say. At the cross, violence was done to the innocent one, and God did not call it good. He judged it, bore it, and overturned it in the resurrection. The cross doesn't sanctify the abuser's power. It exposes it. The one who was beaten and mocked by men who thought they had the right is the one who now stands with every person who has been beaten and mocked by someone who thought they had the right.

So if you are asking whether God would be angry with you for leaving to get safe, the answer of Scripture is no. The God who heard Israel's cry in Egypt hears yours. What remains is not a question about whether you're allowed. It's the harder, slower work of getting safe, and then of healing, and then, in time, of discovering who you are when you're no longer living under someone else's rule. That work takes courage and it takes help. Please don't do it alone. Call 1-800-799-7233. Tell someone the truth. And know that the God who led his people out of a house of slavery is not asking you to stay in one.
