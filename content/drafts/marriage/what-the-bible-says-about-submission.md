The word has been used to cover for so much that it can barely be heard.

Submission. Say it in a room of Christians and watch the faces close. Some brace for an argument they have had too many times. Some go quiet because the word was used on them, or by them, in ways they have not made peace with. A few men sit a little taller, because they have been taught that this verse is the one that finally puts them in charge. The text has been bent into a club, and the people most damaged by the swinging are usually the ones told it was for their good.

If you have searched for what the Bible says about submission in marriage, you have almost certainly been handed verse 22 of Ephesians 5, alone, stripped of everything around it. So let us go back and read the thing slowly, because almost no one does. My claim is plain, and the rest of this essay is me grounding it in the text: **the passage that has been used to make wives small was written, in its own century, to liberate them by half and to crucify the husband's ego the rest of the way.** Read in full, it does the opposite of what it is usually quoted to do.

## The verse begins one sentence before anyone quotes it

The verse everyone quotes is Ephesians 5:22. *Wives, submit to your own husbands, as to the Lord.* It gets printed on its own, preached on its own, weaponized on its own.

But in the earliest Greek manuscripts there is no verb in verse 22. None. The word *submit* does not appear there. It is carried forward grammatically from the sentence before it — verse 21, which says this: *submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ.* The instruction to wives is literally incapable of standing on its own. It is a participle hanging off a command given to the entire church. Whoever decided to begin the quotation at verse 22 did not merely drop a sentence. They dropped the frame that governs everything after it.

This is not a command to wives only. It is a command to the whole congregation — *submit to one another* — and then applied, in turn, to wives, to husbands, to children, to parents, to slaves, to masters. The mutual submission of verse 21 is the roof over the whole house. You cannot pull one room out from under it and call it the structure.

(My first exegetical choice, stated plainly: I am taking *hypotassomenoi* in verse 21 — "submitting to one another" — as genuinely reciprocal, the controlling command for the section, rather than reading it as code for "some submit to others according to rank." The grammar of the missing verb in verse 22 is the strongest evidence that Paul meant the wife's submission to be heard *inside* a mutuality, not as an exception to it.)

## Paul takes a pagan code and detonates it from inside

This matters far more than a grammar lesson. Paul is writing into a first-century household code — the *Haustafel*, a familiar genre in the Greco-Roman world that told the male head of the house how to govern his wife, his children, and his slaves as his property. Aristotle had written one. Every reader knew the shape of the genre. The wife submits, full stop, because she is owned.

What Paul does is take the genre his readers expected and detonate it from inside. He keeps the form. He inverts the content. First, he addresses the wife directly, as a moral agent capable of choosing — which the genre never did; it spoke only to the man about his property. Then he turns to the husband and spends roughly three times as many words telling him to do the one thing no household code in the ancient world had ever asked a man to do.

To die.

*Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her* (Ephesians 5:25). Read that against the world it was written into. The man with all the legal power is told that his model is not the emperor or the master but the crucified. Christ's headship over the church is cited not to justify the husband's authority but to redefine it entirely, because the head of this body got there by being nailed to a cross for it. Headship, in the only definition Paul gives it, means you go first into the dying. It is not the right to be served. It is the obligation to be spent.

**We took the most demanding word in the passage and handed it to the person it constrained the least.**

## Submission is a gift you give, never a tax someone collects

Now the harder honesty, and my second exegetical choice. Submission is in the text. Mutual submission does not erase the particular word addressed to wives; Paul says it, and the impulse to make it vanish entirely is its own kind of dishonesty with Scripture. I am not flattening the passage into perfect symmetry. Paul kept an order. He kept distinct words for husband and for wife. To pretend otherwise is to read our century back into his.

But notice what the word actually is. *Hypotassō* is voluntary. In verse 21 it is in the middle voice — a thing you do to yourself, an ordering of yourself under another. You cannot be commanded into it by the person who benefits from it; that is a contradiction in the grammar itself. The moment a husband demands his wife's submission, he has proven he does not understand the word, because submission that is extracted is not submission. It is subjugation, and the Bible has a different word for that, and it is not approving.

Submission is what you give. It is never what you take.

And it lives inside a mutuality the wider New Testament assumes. The same Paul who wrote Ephesians wrote to the Corinthians that the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does — and then, in the same breath, said the reverse (1 Corinthians 7:4). He distributed the radical claim in both directions. In a culture that granted a man total bodily authority over his wife, Paul handed her the same authority over him, and he did it in one of the most sexually broken cities in the empire. This is not a man building a hierarchy. This is a man dismantling one and leaving just enough structure standing that the marriage does not collapse into a bare negotiation.

It helps to remember what reverence the passage actually appeals to. Both the command to submit and the command to die are anchored in the same phrase: *out of reverence for Christ* (Ephesians 5:21). Neither spouse is told to order themselves around the other because the other has earned it, or proven worthy of it, or won the argument. The whole arrangement is vertical before it is ever horizontal. The wife's submission is offered to Christ through her husband; the husband's death is offered to Christ for his wife. Take Christ out of the center and the structure does become what its critics fear — a raw power arrangement dressed in religious language. Leave Christ at the center and it becomes something no power arrangement has ever been: two people competing to outserve each other, each ordering themselves under the other for the sake of a third party who outranks them both. That is not a ladder with a top rung. It is two people kneeling, and the only contest left is who kneels first.

## What survives when you read the whole passage

The objection comes fast here, and it deserves an answer rather than a dodge. If headship means dying and submission means a gift freely given inside mutual reverence, has the structure not simply dissolved into a slogan that means whatever the reader wants.

No. The structure remains; Paul did not erase it. What he did was fill that order with a content so costly that it can no longer serve the purpose orders usually serve — which is to secure the comfort of whoever sits at the top. An order that requires the one in authority to be the first crucified is not a ladder. It is a stretcher. The structure stands, but it has been turned to face the opposite direction, toward sacrifice rather than privilege, and a man who climbs it expecting to be served will find he has volunteered to be spent.

I have to name my own complicity here, because men in ministry have done enormous damage with this text and I will not pretend I have always read it well. It is convenient to be the head when headship means deciding. It is far less convenient when headship means you are the one called to absorb the cost, to lay yourself down first, to love a person the way a dying man loves the world he is leaving. The version of submission that asks everything of a wife and little of a husband is not the biblical version. It is the pagan version with a verse stapled to it. And we have preached the pagan version in churches for generations and called it God's design.

This is why the abusive use of the text is not a misapplication of a good principle. It is the precise inversion of it. The husband who cites Ephesians 5 to demand compliance has read the one verse addressed to his wife and skipped the four addressed to him — which is to say he has read the passage exactly backward, taking the burden meant for himself and laying it on the person it was meant to lift. Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote, in *Life Together* (1939), that the first service one owes another in community is to listen, and that the one who can no longer listen to his brother will soon no longer be listening to God either. A marriage works the same way. The man who cannot hear his wife because he is too busy ruling her has stopped, somewhere upstream, being able to hear the God who told him to die for her.

## What the text does not say

So what does the Bible actually say. It says that two people who fear Christ order themselves under one another (Ephesians 5:21). It says the one with cultural power is given the heavier command — to spend himself first and most. It says the wife's submission is a gift she offers a husband who has already laid down his life, not a tax he collects from one who has not. And it says, by its silence where we wanted a verb, that the man who needs this verse to feel in charge has missed the entire point of the One the verse is about.

What it does not say is that a woman must submit to abuse, to coercion, to a man wielding the text as a weapon. It does not say her will is the property of his. It does not say headship is a throne. There is no reading of *love her as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her* that arrives at a husband demanding silence, fear, or control. The man who produces those fruits is not exercising headship badly. He is not exercising it at all. He has named something else after it.

I want to be fair to readers who hold a complementarian reading and to those who hold an egalitarian one, because godly people land in different places on the *structure* of the verse. But there is a floor beneath both, and the floor is not in dispute: the cross is the only definition of headship Scripture gives, and any authority that does not look like self-giving is not the authority Paul described. Whatever order you believe the text preserves, it is an order in which the man dies first.

There is one more test the text quietly applies, and it is the simplest. Paul says a husband should love his wife as his own body, that no one ever hated his own flesh but nourishes and cherishes it (Ephesians 5:28–29). The standard is not a rule he imposes on her. It is a mirror he holds up to him. A man knows whether he is loving rightly by the same instinct with which he knows whether he is caring for his own body — automatically, attentively, without keeping score. The husband who has to be told his wife is suffering has already failed the standard, because he would have felt his own suffering long before anyone had to point it out. Headship, in Paul's image, is not a command center. It is a nervous system. The head feels what the body feels, or it is not the head of that body at all.

The cross is the only definition of headship Scripture gives.

If your reading of submission makes the husband comfortable and the wife small, you are not reading Paul. You are reading the world Paul was trying to bring down, and you have put his name on it.
