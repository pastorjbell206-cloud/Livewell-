Picture a couple three months from their wedding, sitting at a kitchen table with a printed list of questions a friend sent them. How many children do you want? Who handles the money? What are your career goals? They move through it quickly, relieved each time their answers match. Two children, maybe three. She'll keep the budget. He wants to stay near his parents, and she doesn't mind. By the end of the evening they feel reassured, as though they have passed an exam. They are compatible. The list said so.

Nothing about that evening was foolish. Couples who talk before marriage are wiser than couples who don't, and the questions on that list are real questions. But the evening rested on an assumption most engaged couples never examine: that the purpose of talking before marriage is to find out whether you fit. Underneath the questions sat a fear, and the fear was reasonable. Everyone at that table knows couples who seemed to fit and came apart anyway. They are hoping that enough matching answers will protect them from becoming one of those stories.

They won't. A great deal of what will test that marriage isn't on the list, because it hasn't happened yet: the diagnosis in year nine, the job that disappears, the child who struggles, the slow change in each of them that neither can foresee. The conversations that matter before a wedding are not mainly about finding out whether two people match. They are about learning how to make and keep a promise to someone you cannot yet fully know. That is a different skill than compatibility, and it is the one a marriage actually runs on.

## Why compatibility became the question

Engaged couples didn't invent their anxiety. They inherited it from a long change in what marriage is for. For most of history, in most of the world, marriage was an arrangement between families about property, labor, children and alliance. Love was hoped for, but it wasn't the foundation. Stephanie Coontz argues in *Marriage, a History* (2005) that the modern ideal of marrying for love, which spread through Europe and America from the late eighteenth century, made marriage both more satisfying and more fragile. Once love became the reason for marrying, the loss of love became a reason for leaving, and the same idea that raised marriage's promise raised its risk.

The sociologist Andrew Cherlin sharpened the point in *The Marriage-Go-Round* (2009). Americans, he argues, once treated marriage as a cornerstone, the foundation you laid early and built an adult life upon. Now they tend to treat it as a capstone, the achievement you add once everything else is in place: the degree, the career, the savings, the certainty that you have found the right person. A capstone has to be perfect, since it's the crowning piece. So couples wait longer, test more carefully and bring to the engagement a kind of inspector's eye. The question on their minds is whether this person is good enough to be the crowning piece of a life.

Some of that caution is simply wisdom, and it should be honored before it's questioned. Scripture itself warns against careless promises: "When you vow a vow to God, do not delay paying it, for he has no pleasure in fools. Pay what you vow. It is better that you should not vow than that you should vow and not pay" (Ecclesiastes 5:4–5). Jesus asked the crowds following him to think before they followed: "For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it?" (Luke 14:28). He was speaking about discipleship, not weddings, but the logic is the same one the church has always applied to every lifelong vow. You count the cost before you promise, because afterward the cost gets paid whether you counted it or not.

The trouble is what we now think we're counting. The tower builder counts his own resources, what he has to give. The compatibility test counts the other person's fit, what they will provide. That shift is small and nearly invisible, and it changes everything about the conversation. A couple asking whether they are compatible is asking whether this marriage will satisfy them. A couple counting the cost is asking whether they are ready to be faithful to someone who will sometimes disappoint them. Keller makes this point in *The Meaning of Marriage* (2011): the search for a perfectly compatible partner assumes a person who will require little change of us, and no such person exists, since marriage brings two sinners into a closeness where each will be exposed. Both questions deserve an answer. Only the second one gets you through the ninth year.

## What the church once asked before a wedding

It helps to remember that the church has always prepared people for marriage, though not in the way our questionnaires do. In 1215 the Fourth Lateran Council required that intended marriages be announced publicly in the parish church beforehand, the practice called the banns. The point was to ask the community whether anything stood in the way: a prior marriage, a close blood relation, a promise made under force. Compatibility never came into it. Marriage was treated as a public act with a public witness, and the community had a stake in whether two people were free to make it.

When Thomas Cranmer's Book of Common Prayer appeared in 1549, the service opened by telling the congregation what marriage was and how it ought to be entered: not lightly or carelessly, but "reverently, discreetly, advisedly, soberly, and in the fear of God." Every one of those words describes the people making the vow, not the fit between them. The service then asked each partner a question in the future tense. Wilt thou have this woman? Wilt thou love her, comfort her, honor and keep her, in sickness and in health? The vow didn't ask whether you already felt enough love to last. It asked whether you would bind your will to love, whatever came.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer understood this as well as anyone in the last century. In May 1943, from a cell in Tegel prison, he wrote a sermon for the wedding of his niece Renate and his friend Eberhard Bethge, which he couldn't attend. In it he wrote: "It is not your love that sustains the marriage, but from now on, the marriage that sustains your love." He was telling them where their love would find its footing. Feelings rise and fall with sleep, stress, illness and time. The covenant holds steady, and it gives love something to stand on while the feelings recover. That difference between a covenant and a contract runs through the whole Christian account of marriage, and this site traces it at length in [what covenant marriage is](/writing/covenant-vs-contract-what-marriage-is).

This is the distinction that should shape every conversation before a wedding. The world asks engaged couples to test their feelings and their fit, and that testing has value, but it is the smaller half of preparation. The church has always asked the harder thing: are you free, do you understand what you are promising, and are you ready to become the kind of person who keeps it? Those of us who preach have sometimes let premarital preparation shrink into a personality inventory and a lesson on communication styles. The older question was sterner and kinder at the same time. It assumed you would change, and it asked whether you'd keep the promise when you did.

## The conversations that matter

None of this means the practical conversations are unimportant. They matter a great deal, but for a different reason than couples usually think. The goal is to learn how each of you thinks, what each of you fears, and how the two of you handle it when the answers don't match, and a matching list can't show you any of that. What follows really is a list, because these are separate subjects, but each deserves an evening rather than a checkbox.

Begin with the families you came from. Every person enters marriage with a picture of normal, drawn by the home they grew up in, and most people don't know they have one until it collides with someone else's. Did your family shout and forgive, or go quiet for days? Was money discussed at dinner or never mentioned? Did holidays mean crowds or silence? The family systems theorist Murray Bowen, in *Family Therapy in Clinical Practice* (1978), described how patterns of anxiety and reaction pass from one generation to the next, often unexamined. You do not have to accept his whole theory to recognize the truth in it. The couple at the kitchen table is really four families at the table, and it is far better to meet them on purpose than to discover them in the middle of a fight about Thanksgiving. Talk about your parents' marriage, what you want to carry forward and what you hope to leave behind. If one of you grew up in a home marked by addiction, violence or abandonment, say so plainly, and consider talking it through with a counselor before the wedding rather than after.

Talk about money, and go past the budget. Debt should be disclosed completely, including the student loans and the credit card no one mentions. But the deeper conversation is about what money means to each of you. For one person, a savings account feels like safety. For another, it feels like hoarding, and generosity feels like faith. One of you may have grown up with scarcity and the other with plenty, and neither will understand the other's reflexes without being told. Ask who will decide, and how. Ask what you'll give away. Money is rarely the real subject of a money fight, and couples who have already talked about what money means to them fight about it less bitterly.

Talk about children, including the possibility that they won't come easily or at all. Ask how many, and when, and what each of you imagines a household with children will ask of you. But also ask what happens if you want them and can't have them, or have one who needs far more than you planned for. These are hard questions to put to someone you're excited to marry. They are much harder to face for the first time in a doctor's office.

Talk about faith, and not only whether you both believe. Two Christians can share every doctrine and still differ about how faith is practiced in a home. Will you pray together, and how? Which church, and what if you disagree? What will the Lord's Day look like? If one of you is more serious about faith than the other, name it now, because that difference doesn't shrink with time.

Talk about sex, honestly and without either shame or detail for its own sake. Each of you brings expectations, and often a history, and sometimes a wound. You don't owe each other a catalogue of the past, but you do owe each other the truth about anything that will shape your life together: a habit you're still fighting, an experience that still frightens you, a fear you've never said aloud. Many couples raised on silence enter marriage with no words at all for this part of it. Find some before the honeymoon, and if you find you can't, a counselor or a trusted pastor can help you start.

Talk about work and place: whose career, if either, will lead when a choice must be made, where you'll live, how near your families, what you would do if one of you were offered something far away. Talk about illness and limits. Ask what each of you would need if the other became sick for a long time. It is the part of the vow almost no one thinks about in the months before the wedding, and it is the part a great many couples end up living.

## How you disagree matters more than whether you do

Beneath every one of those subjects lies the question that shapes the rest: what happens when you don't agree? Engaged couples often treat disagreement as a warning sign, and they're relieved when their answers match. But John Gottman's research, gathered over decades of watching married couples and summarized in *The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work* (1999), found that most of the conflicts couples have are never finally solved. He reported that about 69 percent of marital conflicts are what he called perpetual problems, rooted in lasting differences of personality or need. Couples who last do not eliminate these differences. They learn to talk about them with humor, respect and some affection, year after year, without contempt.

That finding should change how engaged couples read their own disagreements. Discovering that you differ about money or about how often to see your families is not a sign that you shouldn't marry. It is a preview of the marriage. The useful question is how the conversation went. Did one of you go silent and the other push harder? Did either of you mock, dismiss or roll your eyes? Did anyone apologize? Could you laugh about it later? A couple that can disagree about something small and come out the other side still on the same side has learned more about their future than any matching list could tell them.

Scripture has been saying this for a long time. "If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame" (Proverbs 18:13). "Let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger; for the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God" (James 1:19–20). James wasn't writing about engagement, but he was writing to a community of people who couldn't simply leave each other, and marriage is the most concentrated form of that community there is. The habits of hearing, of slowness, of confessing quickly, are formed before the wedding or they are formed painfully after it.

This is also where honesty has to include a harder possibility. Sometimes what a couple discovers in these conversations isn't a difference to be carried. It's a danger. If the person you're engaged to controls where you go and whom you see, checks your phone, belittles you in front of others, or frightens you when angry; if there has been any shoving, grabbing or threatening, even once; if you find yourself managing their moods to keep the peace, those are not compatibility problems. They are warning signs of abuse, and marriage does not cure them. It usually deepens them. You are free to slow down or to stop, whatever has been spent on the wedding and whoever will be disappointed. The National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233 (or text START to 88788), and a wise pastor or counselor will take you seriously. An engagement ended for good reasons may be the most faithful thing you ever do, and no one should call it failure.

## Sliding toward a wedding

There is one more danger worth naming, because it's quieter than abuse and far more common. The psychologists Scott Stanley, Galena Rhoades and Howard Markman have written about what they call sliding versus deciding. In a 2006 paper in *Family Relations*, they argued that many couples move into major commitments by momentum rather than by choice. They start spending nights together, then move in to save rent, then acquire a lease, a dog, shared furniture and shared friends, until leaving becomes costly enough that staying feels like the only option. By the time a proposal comes, the decision was in some sense made long before, by inertia, and neither partner ever sat down to choose.

Their research concerned couples who lived together before marriage, and Christians have their own reasons for caution there. But the dynamic they describe reaches further than cohabitation. An engagement has its own momentum. There are deposits paid, invitations printed, parents excited and a date circled on everyone's calendar. The question of whether to marry can be quietly replaced by the question of how to pull off the wedding. Couples who notice a serious problem at month four sometimes say nothing, because saying something would stop a train that everyone is already riding.

The Christian tradition has always treated vows as acts of deliberate will. Ruth's words to Naomi are often read at weddings, and though she spoke them to her mother-in-law rather than a husband, they are the plainest example in Scripture of what a decided promise sounds like: "For where you go I will go, and where you lodge I will lodge. Your people shall be my people, and your God my God" (Ruth 1:16). Nobody slides into a sentence like that. It is chosen with open eyes, knowing what it costs. Naomi had just urged her, more than once, to go home, and she made her promise anyway.

So one of the most useful things an engaged couple can do is to stop, some weeks before the wedding, and choose again on purpose. Ask each other plainly whether you are marrying because you have decided to, or because it would be too hard now not to. If the answer is the first, say so, and let the saying of it matter. If there is doubt, say that too, while there is still time to listen to it. A wedding postponed is embarrassing for a season. A marriage entered against one's own conscience is a far heavier thing to carry.

## Promising what you cannot see

In the end, preparation for marriage runs into a limit that no conversation can remove. You are about to promise your life to someone you do not fully know, and whom you cannot fully know, because they are going to change. So are you. The person you marry at twenty-six will not be the same person at forty-six, and some of the changes will surprise both of you. The couple who believes they can know everything in advance will feel betrayed when the surprises come. The couple who understands that the vow was made precisely because they could not see the future will be better prepared to keep it.

Wendell Berry, in his essay "Poetry and Marriage: The Use of Old Forms" (1983), compares the vows of marriage to the formal constraints of a poem. A poet working within a strict form, he argues, often finds that the form itself leads him somewhere he couldn't have gone on his own, because it forces him past his first easy choices. Marriage works the same way. The promise doesn't simply hold two people in place. It gives them a shape within which they can discover what they didn't know about each other and themselves. Couples who have been married a long time often say they understand their vows better now than on the day they made them. That is the form doing its work.

Christians make this kind of promise because we have been on the receiving end of one. When God made his covenant with Abram in Genesis 15, he alone passed between the pieces of the sacrifice while a deep sleep lay on Abram, binding himself to a promise whose weight he would carry alone. The whole story of Scripture is God keeping his word to people who kept failing to keep theirs, until the promise came to rest in a Son who was faithful all the way to the cross. Paul could write, "if we are faithless, he remains faithful" (2 Timothy 2:13), because God cannot deny himself. A marriage vow is a small human echo of that faithfulness. It doesn't depend on knowing everything in advance. It depends on a decision to keep faith with someone whatever the future turns out to hold, and on a God whose own faithfulness makes such a decision thinkable.

So talk about everything: the families, the money, the children, the faith, the fears, the body and the future. Do it slowly, and don't be alarmed when your answers differ. But know what the talking is for. No amount of it buys a guarantee. What it can do is begin, before the wedding, the practice that the marriage will require for the rest of your lives: telling the truth, listening past your first reaction, confessing when you're wrong and choosing each other again. The couple at the kitchen table was right to talk. They were asking the wrong question. The one worth carrying to the altar is not whether you'll always fit together. It is whether you'll keep your word when you don't.
