Picture a married couple in bed on an ordinary weeknight. One of them reaches across, a hand on a shoulder that means what both of them know it means. The other goes still, then says, gently enough, that they're tired. The first rolls back to their side. Nothing more is said. One lies awake feeling unwanted and a little ashamed of wanting. The other lies awake feeling guilty, and underneath the guilt, a little resentful at being made to feel guilty. In the morning they are kind to each other over coffee, and neither mentions it. It will happen again next week, and the week after, and each time a little more is added to a ledger neither of them admits to keeping.

This is one of the most common experiences in married life, and one of the least discussed in church. Couples almost never want sex at exactly the same frequency, and the difference can be large. The spouse who wants more is not always the husband; in a great many marriages it's the wife. And the difference isn't fixed. It shifts with seasons of life, with children, stress, illness, age and the state of the marriage itself.

This site has a longer essay on [the sexless marriage](/writing/marriage-intimacy), on what Paul was correcting at Corinth and on how the church came to distrust desire. That essay deals with marriages where sex has gone quiet altogether. This one is about something more ordinary and in some ways harder to name: marriages where there's still a sexual relationship, where both spouses love each other, and where one of them simply wants more than the other, week after week. The difference itself is not a defect in either spouse. But it is a place where each of them is tempted to build a private theology. The spouse who wants more is tempted to turn want into a claim. The spouse who wants less is tempted to turn lack into a verdict, on themselves or on the marriage. Both theologies are false, and Scripture addresses both.

## How frequency became the measure of a marriage

Part of the weight couples feel comes from a relatively recent habit of mind: the idea that the health of a marriage can be measured by how often a couple has sex, and that there is a normal number against which everyone can check themselves.

For most of Christian history, this wasn't how people thought. The church's teaching on marital sex was often shaped by suspicion of desire, and its concern was more with restraint than with frequency. The shift came in the twentieth century, and it began with good intentions. In 1918 the British writer Marie Stopes published *Married Love*, which argued that wives had their own desire, with its own rhythms, and that husbands who ignored it were failing their wives. It was a genuine corrective to a culture that had often treated a wife's desire as irrelevant. In 1926 the Dutch gynecologist Theodoor van de Velde published *Ideal Marriage*, a manual that treated mutual sexual satisfaction as a skill husbands should learn and a mark of a successful marriage. Both books helped couples, and both began to make sexual fulfillment a measure of how a marriage was doing.

Then came counting. Alfred Kinsey's *Sexual Behavior in the Human Male* (1948), followed by a companion volume on women in 1953, presented American sexual life in tables and averages. William Masters and Virginia Johnson's *Human Sexual Response* (1966) measured the body's responses in a laboratory. These researchers had different aims, and some of their methods and conclusions have been sharply criticized. But together they taught a culture to think of sex as something that could be quantified, and to ask, often anxiously, whether their own numbers were normal.

Christians didn't escape this. When evangelical publishers began producing their own marriage manuals in the 1970s, most notably Tim and Beverly LaHaye's bestselling *The Act of Marriage* (1976), they did something good: they insisted that sex in marriage was a gift from God to be enjoyed, not merely tolerated. That was an important recovery after centuries of embarrassment. But they often kept the culture's measuring habit, and sometimes added a moral one. Couples learned that frequent sex was a sign of a healthy Christian marriage, and some teaching implied, or said outright, that a wife who wanted less than her husband was failing in her duty.

The result is that many couples come to their difference with a scorecard already in hand. The spouse who wants more measures the marriage against a number and finds it wanting. The spouse who wants less measures themselves against the same number and feels defective. Those of us who teach in the church have sometimes made this worse, reducing a whole theology of the body to a counsel to give your spouse what they need, usually addressed to wives. That counsel is not entirely wrong, but on its own it turns a gift into a quota and leaves the deeper questions untouched.

## What we've learned about desire

One of the most useful things couples can learn is that desire doesn't work the same way for everyone. For a long time, researchers and ordinary people alike assumed that sexual desire normally comes first, as a spontaneous urge, and that arousal and intimacy follow. People who rarely felt that spontaneous urge were thought to have low desire, and their spouses often concluded that they simply weren't interested.

In 2000 the physician Rosemary Basson proposed a different model in the *Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy*. She observed that for many people, especially women in long-term relationships, desire often doesn't come first. It emerges in response to emotional closeness, touch and the beginning of intimacy. Someone may begin without any strong desire at all, but with a willingness to be close, and desire follows. Emily Nagoski popularized this idea in *Come As You Are* (2015), distinguishing between spontaneous desire, which seems to arise on its own, and responsive desire, which arises in response to closeness and a context that feels safe and welcoming. Neither is abnormal. Many people experience mostly one or the other, and many experience both at different times.

Nagoski also drew on a model developed by the researchers John Bancroft and Erick Janssen, which describes sexual response as governed by something like accelerators and brakes. Some things turn desire on. Others shut it down: stress, exhaustion, anxiety, resentment, body shame, pain, fear, a sink full of dishes, a sense of being pressured. For many people who experience low desire, the problem isn't that their accelerators are weak. It's that their brakes are pressed hard. A spouse who wants more and responds to the difference by pushing harder may be pressing the brakes themselves.

This matters because it changes the question. The couple in bed may be interpreting their difference as a gap between someone who wants and someone who doesn't. It may actually be a gap between someone whose desire tends to arrive first and someone whose desire tends to arrive later, if the context allows it. That doesn't solve everything, but it makes room for hope, and it shifts attention from the moment of the request to everything that surrounds it.

Two other thinkers have shaped how many counselors approach this, and both deserve a fair hearing. Esther Perel, in *Mating in Captivity* (2006), argues that the security marriage builds can be in tension with desire, which feeds on some sense of otherness and mystery. Couples who become too fused, who know everything about each other and see each other only as caretakers and co-parents, may find that desire fades. She's right that desire needs some sense of the other as a distinct person, not merely a function. But her account tends to treat desire itself as sovereign, something to be preserved at almost any cost. A Christian account places desire within love, where it's precious but not ultimate.

David Schnarch, in *Passionate Marriage* (1997), made the provocative claim that in any couple the partner with the lower desire controls sex, since nothing happens without their agreement. He urged couples to grow in what he called differentiation, the capacity to stay close to a spouse while managing one's own anxiety rather than demanding that the spouse relieve it. There is wisdom in that. But framing the question in terms of control can make a marriage feel like a negotiation between rival powers. The truth is that both spouses hold power. The one who wants less can make the other feel like a beggar. The one who wants more can make the other feel like a debtor. The Christian question isn't who holds the power. It is how each will use it.

It also has to be said plainly that differences in desire sometimes have medical causes. Hormonal changes, certain medications including many antidepressants, pain during sex, the aftermath of childbirth, menopause, erectile difficulty and a range of other conditions can all affect desire or make intimacy difficult. A physician should be part of the conversation, and a counselor trained in sexual health can help with what a physician can't. This is not medical advice, only an encouragement to take the body seriously.

## A word to both spouses

The passage every Christian couple eventually meets is Paul's instruction in 1 Corinthians 7:

> The husband should give to his wife her conjugal rights, and likewise the wife to her husband. For the wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does. Likewise the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does. Do not deprive one another, except perhaps by agreement for a limited time, that you may devote yourselves to prayer; but then come together again, so that Satan may not tempt you because of your lack of self-control. (1 Corinthians 7:3–5)

These verses are often quoted to the spouse who wants less, as a command not to refuse. They are that, in part. Paul does treat sustained withholding as a real danger, and he wants married couples to come together regularly. But the passage is addressed to both spouses equally, and read in context it says more than its usual use suggests.

Notice first that it gives neither spouse unilateral authority. The wife's body belongs to her husband, and the husband's body belongs to his wife, in the same breath and the same words. That rules out the spouse who says, in effect, my body is mine and I will decide alone. It rules out just as firmly the spouse who says, your body is mine and you owe it to me. Neither spouse is the sole owner of the sexual relationship. It belongs to both of them together.

Notice second the phrase "by agreement." The only legitimate pause Paul envisions is one both spouses choose. That means Paul assumes married couples are talking about this, deciding together, reaching a shared understanding. The alternative to one spouse's refusal isn't the other spouse's demand. It is agreement, arrived at together, which requires the kind of honest conversation most couples avoid.

Notice third that Paul is realistic about desire. He speaks of "your lack of self-control" without contempt. He knows that a spouse whose desire goes unmet for long periods may be vulnerable to temptation. He doesn't shame that vulnerability. He treats it as a reason for the couple to take the sexual relationship seriously.

And notice what comes immediately after: "Now as a concession, not a command, I say this. I wish that all were as I myself am. But each has his own gift from God, one of one kind and one of another" (1 Corinthians 7:6–7). Commentators differ on exactly what the "concession" covers (many take it to be the agreed pause of verse 5), and the "gift" Paul has in mind is singleness or marriage, not a level of desire. But the tone is unmistakable. Paul is pastoral here, not legal. He knows that people are made differently, and he writes as someone trying to help different people live faithfully, not as someone handing either spouse a rule to enforce on the other.

That pastoral tone fits the way the New Testament speaks about love everywhere else. "Do nothing from selfish ambition or conceit, but in humility count others more significant than yourselves. Let each of you look not only to his own interests, but also to the interests of others" (Philippians 2:3–4). "Let each of us please his neighbor for his good, to build him up. For Christ did not please himself" (Romans 15:2–3). Neither of these is addressed only to the spouse who wants less or only to the spouse who wants more. Both are addressed to both.

One more thing must be said before going further. None of this ever permits pressure, manipulation or force. Marriage does not take away anyone's freedom to say no. Sex obtained by threat, guilt, persistent badgering or force is coercion, and coercion inside a marriage is abuse, whatever verse is quoted over it. Scripture never asks anyone to endure it. If that describes your marriage, the National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233 (or text START to 88788), RAINN's National Sexual Assault Hotline answers at 1-800-656-4673, and you deserve a pastor or counselor who will take you seriously. If you are in danger right now, call 911.

## The temptations of the spouse who wants more

The spouse who wants more sex is often the one who feels the difference most acutely, and their pain is real. Being turned down, again and again, by the person you love most can feel like being told you are not wanted. Many people in this position begin to wonder whether their spouse finds them attractive at all, whether they're loved, whether the marriage is in trouble. That pain deserves compassion, not dismissal.

But this spouse faces particular temptations. One is resentment, the slow accumulation of grievances that turns every refusal into evidence in a case. Another is self-pity, which can become a kind of sulking that punishes the other spouse by withdrawing warmth. That withdrawal is itself a form of pressure, and it often backfires, because it presses exactly the brakes it is trying to release. A third temptation is to treat sex as the only real proof of love, discounting every other way the spouse shows care. And a fourth, which can be very serious, is to seek relief elsewhere, whether in pornography, fantasy or another person. Job's resolution is worth remembering: "I have made a covenant with my eyes; how then could I gaze at a virgin?" (Job 31:1). The unmet desire is real, but it doesn't license betraying the covenant.

The deepest temptation is to turn want into a claim. Desire says, I want you. That's good, and it's a gift to a spouse to be wanted. But it can quietly become, you owe me, and at that point it is no longer desire for the other person. It is a demand for something from them. Paul's words about the husband's authority over his wife's body are sometimes used this way, but the same sentence gives the wife the same authority over his. Belonging is mutual, and it is received, not seized.

What does faithfulness look like for this spouse? It starts with finding the truer sentence underneath the frustration. Often it isn't really about frequency at all. It's something like, I need to know you still want me. That sentence can be said in daylight, with tenderness, and it invites a very different response than a complaint about how long it's been. It continues with learning what the other spouse's brakes are, and taking them seriously. If exhaustion is a brake, then sharing the work of the household is part of the sexual relationship. If resentment is a brake, then repairing old wounds is part of it. Keller describes sex in marriage as a kind of covenant renewal ceremony, a way of renewing with the body the promises made with words. If that is so, the covenant has to be kept outside the bedroom for the renewal to mean anything.

And it involves learning to receive a no without punishment. The Song of Songs contains a small poem that speaks almost directly to this. The woman is in bed. Her beloved knocks at the door: "I slept, but my heart was awake. A sound! My beloved is knocking. 'Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my perfect one'" (Song of Songs 5:2). She hesitates: "I had put off my garment; how could I put it on? I had bathed my feet; how could I soil them?" (Song of Songs 5:3). Their timing is mismatched, right there in Scripture's great love poem. And notice what the lover does not do. He doesn't break the door down. He knocks, he speaks tenderly, and when the door stays shut he does not force it. The spouse who wants more is invited to love like that: to ask, to wait, and to keep the door a place where no one is forced.

## The temptations of the spouse who wants less

The spouse who wants less sex faces different pain and different temptations. Many feel a heavy mix of guilt, pressure and inadequacy. They love their spouse, and they may wish they wanted more, but they don't, and they don't know why. Every approach from their spouse can feel like a test they're about to fail. That pain is real too, and it also deserves compassion.

One temptation for this spouse is to turn lack into a verdict. They may conclude that something is wrong with them, that they're broken, or that something is wrong with the marriage, that they must not really love their spouse. Neither conclusion necessarily follows. A lower level of spontaneous desire may simply be how they are made, or a response to circumstances that can change. Another temptation is avoidance: going to bed later, making sure to be asleep, never initiating, steering clear of any affection that might be misread as an invitation. Avoidance protects against the pressure of the moment, but it slowly starves the marriage of the ordinary touch and closeness that might, in time, make desire possible.

A third temptation is what might be called duty sex: agreeing without any willingness, simply to end the pressure or fulfill an obligation, and then resenting it. That can go on for years, and it tends to make desire even harder, since the body learns to associate intimacy with obligation. A fourth, rarer but real, is to use no as a lever, withholding sex to punish or control a spouse. Paul's instruction does speak to that directly. The body is not a weapon.

What does faithfulness look like for this spouse? It begins with honest investigation. What are the brakes? Is it exhaustion, stress, resentment, pain, fear, a history that makes closeness difficult, a medication, a hormonal change? Some of these can be addressed with a physician, some with a counselor, some with changes in the marriage. It continues with honesty toward the spouse, telling the truth rather than making excuses. There is a real difference between saying, I'm tired tonight, and saying, I've been struggling to feel desire and I don't know why, and I want us to figure it out together. The first ends a conversation. The second begins one.

It also involves learning the difference between not now and never, and saying which one is meant. And for many people it involves discovering what Basson and Nagoski described: that desire can follow willingness. A spouse may not feel strong desire at the beginning, but may find, if they're willing to begin with closeness, that desire comes. That willingness can be a genuine act of generosity, a way of looking to the interests of the other. But it must never become an obligation to endure something painful or frightening. If there is pain, fear or a history of abuse, willingness isn't the answer. Healing is, and it needs time and often professional help.

The Song of Songs speaks to this spouse too. After the woman hesitates, she does rise to open the door, and the poem records what she finds: "I opened to my beloved, but my beloved had turned and gone" (Song of Songs 5:6). The rest of the chapter is her searching the city for him. The poem doesn't condemn her, and it doesn't say she was wrong to hesitate. It simply lets both longings stand, his at the door and hers in the streets, and it treats each as worth grieving. The spouse who wants less is invited to take their spouse's longing seriously, as the longing to be wanted that it usually is, and never to mistake that invitation for a debt.

## Neither grasping nor withholding

Beneath all these temptations lies a single question. Will each spouse use their power over the other for the other's good, or for their own?

Paul's great hymn about the mind of Christ speaks to both at once. "Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus, who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men" (Philippians 2:5–7). The spouse who wants more is called to love that does not grasp. Christ had every right to what he did not seize, and he did not seize it. The spouse who wants less is called to love that empties itself, that gives itself freely, not under compulsion, for the good of the other. Both are called to the same mind, and both find it only by looking at Christ.

This doesn't produce a formula for how often. No verse gives a number, and couples who look for one will be disappointed. What it produces is a posture. Two people who belong to each other, each looking to the other's interests, each refusing to turn the difference between them into a weapon or a verdict, each willing to have the hard conversation in daylight and to keep having it as seasons change.

The first marriage in Scripture is described with a sentence that still names what couples want most: "And the man and his wife were both naked and were not ashamed" (Genesis 2:25). The couple in bed on that weeknight are both afraid of being exposed. One is afraid of being unwanted. The other is afraid of being inadequate. The difference in their desire has become the place where each of them hides. The way forward isn't for one of them to win. It's for both of them to be seen, the one who wants and the one who struggles to want, by a spouse who has decided not to turn away.

They will probably lie in the same bed next week, and the difference between them won't have disappeared. But they could talk about it before then, over coffee, when nothing is being asked. One could say, I need to know you want me. The other could say, I want to want you, and I don't always know how. Neither sentence solves anything. Both are true, and both are a kind of nakedness. What each carries into that conversation is not a claim or a defense but a question: whether they're willing to belong to each other in this too, and to let Christ's way of loving, which neither grasped nor withheld, become theirs.
