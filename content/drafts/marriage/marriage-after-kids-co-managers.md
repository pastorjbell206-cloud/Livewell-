Picture a couple at twenty to ten on a weeknight. The children are finally asleep, the dishes are more or less done, and the two of them sit down on the couch with their phones. What follows is a meeting. Who's taking the four-year-old to the dentist on Thursday. Whether the baby's cough needs a doctor. Who will buy the birthday present for the party on Saturday, and whether the car can wait another month for new tires. They're good at this. They divide the tasks quickly and fairly, and there's no fight. Then one of them goes to bed and the other stays up to finish an email, and it occurs to the one lying in the dark that they haven't asked each other a real question in weeks. Nothing is wrong, exactly. They've simply become colleagues.

This is one of the most common experiences in marriage, and one of the least discussed, because it doesn't look like a problem. The couple isn't fighting. They aren't unfaithful. They're raising their children with real care, and they've built a remarkably efficient partnership to do it. What they have lost is harder to name. They were once friends and lovers who happened to share a household. Now they're co-managers of a household who happen to have been friends and lovers once.

The advice they're likely to hear falls into two camps. One says to schedule date nights, protect the romance and don't let the kids take over. The other says this is a season, it will pass, and the children need you now. Both carry some truth, and neither goes deep enough. The trouble isn't that parents become a team. Scripture and common sense both commend teamwork, and small children need it. The trouble is when the team quietly replaces the friendship it was meant to serve, so that the marriage becomes only the management structure of a family, rather than the living bond at its center. That substitution is easy to miss, because on most days a good team and a good marriage look exactly alike.

## What a baby does to a marriage

Couples expecting their first child often expect that a baby will bring them closer, and in some ways it does. They share a love for this new person that no one else in the world shares in the same way. But researchers who have followed couples through the transition to parenthood have found that it almost always puts the marriage under strain.

Carolyn and Philip Cowan, psychologists at Berkeley, followed couples from late pregnancy into their children's early years and reported their findings in *When Partners Become Parents* (1992). They found that the arrival of a first child brought increased conflict and decreased satisfaction for most couples, and that one of the strongest sources of strain was the gap between what couples expected and what happened. Many planned to divide the work of the home and the baby evenly, and then found themselves sliding into more traditional divisions than either had intended, with resentment building on both sides. In 2000, Alyson Shapiro, John Gottman and Sybil Carrère published a study, titled "The Baby and the Marriage," that followed newlywed couples for several years. Of the wives who became mothers in that time, about two-thirds reported a decline in marital satisfaction after the first baby arrived, while about a third held steady or even improved. The couples who did not decline tended to share something specific: in the early years of the marriage, before the baby came, they had shown fondness and affection for each other and had stayed aware of what was going on in each other's lives.

That last finding matters most. The decline wasn't inevitable, and the difference wasn't how much the couples loved their child or how well they managed the logistics. It was whether they were still friends, still curious about each other and still delighted by each other, before the baby put that friendship to the test. Management can be learned under pressure. Friendship has to be tended before the pressure comes, and then kept alive through it.

None of this should frighten couples out of having children, nor should it make parents feel guilty for putting their children first in a season when children really do need nearly everything. Jesus rebuked his own disciples for trying to keep children away from him: "Let the children come to me; do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of God" (Mark 10:14). Small children are not an interruption of the Christian life. They're part of it, and caring for them is holy work. But holy work can still crowd out other holy things, and the marriage that holds the children is one of them.

## How the household became a management problem

It helps to remember that the co-manager marriage is not only a personal failure. It is also the result of a long historical change in what a household is and what children are for.

For most of human history, the household was a place of shared work. Farms, shops and trades were run from the home, and husband, wife, children and often extended family all contributed to the family's survival. Wendell Berry has argued, in essays such as "Feminism, the Body, and the Machine" (collected in *What Are People For?*, 1990), that the household was once an economy in the old sense, a place of production where marriage was bound up with shared labor, and that industrial society turned it into a place of consumption, from which each spouse goes out to work for someone else. When work left the home, husbands and wives lost one of the things that had held them together: a shared daily task they could see.

The meaning of children changed too. The sociologist Viviana Zelizer showed in *Pricing the Priceless Child* (1985) that between the 1870s and the 1930s, American children went from being economically useful, contributing labor to the family, to being, in her phrase, "economically worthless but emotionally priceless." Children stopped working and became the center of the family's emotional life. Much of that was a moral gain, since child labor was often brutal. But it also meant that raising children became less a matter of incorporating them into the family's shared work and more a project of cultivating each child's individual flourishing.

By the late twentieth century, that project had intensified. The sociologist Sharon Hays described in *The Cultural Contradictions of Motherhood* (1996) what she called intensive mothering: the belief that good parenting must be child-centered, guided by experts, emotionally absorbing, labor-intensive and expensive. Suzanne Bianchi, John Robinson and Melissa Milkie, drawing on time-diary studies in *Changing Rhythms of American Family Life* (2006), found that parents at the turn of the century spent at least as much time caring for their children as parents in 1965, even though far more mothers were working outside the home, and that fathers' time with children had grown considerably. Parents were doing more, not less. Jennifer Senior's *All Joy and No Fun* (2014) gathered much of this history into a portrait of modern parents who love their children deeply and find the work of raising them exhausting in ways earlier generations didn't.

Put all of this together and you have the co-manager marriage. Two adults who both go out to work, who return to a household that produces nothing together except the raising of children, and who have been told that raising children well requires constant, intensive, carefully coordinated effort. Of course they become managers. The household has become a small enterprise, and the children are its only product. The marriage, which once had a shared life of work and table and worship to sustain it, now has only the logistics. Another essay on this site deals at length with [the mental load in marriage](/writing/emotional-labor-in-marriage), the invisible work of tracking and anticipating that so often falls on one spouse. That question is real and it bears on this one. But even couples who share that load fairly can find themselves reduced to colleagues, because the problem runs deeper than fairness.

Christians disagree, sometimes sharply, about how the work of a household should be divided between husband and wife. Some hold that Scripture assigns distinct callings, with the husband carrying primary responsibility for provision and the wife for the home, and they point to the goods of that arrangement for young children. Others hold that Scripture's picture of the capable household in Proverbs 31 and of mutual submission in Ephesians 5 leaves couples free to divide the work as their gifts and circumstances require. This is a second-order question on which faithful Christians land in different places, and this essay doesn't settle it. What matters here is that no arrangement, traditional or egalitarian, protects a marriage from becoming a management partnership. Couples on both sides of that debate end up on the same couch at twenty to ten, going over the schedule.

## Side by side and face to face

Aristotle offers a way to name what has happened. In the eighth and ninth books of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, he distinguishes three kinds of friendship: friendships of utility, where people are useful to each other; friendships of pleasure, where they enjoy each other's company; and friendships of virtue, where each person loves the other for who they are and wishes the other's good. He observes that the friendship of husband and wife can include all three. The co-manager marriage has kept the friendship of utility and let the others wither. The spouses are extremely useful to each other. They are rarely delighted in each other, and rarely have time to know each other.

C. S. Lewis draws a related distinction in *The Four Loves* (1960). "Lovers are normally face to face, absorbed in each other," he writes, while friends stand "side by side, absorbed in some common interest." Parenting makes couples into side-by-side partners, united in their shared concern for the children. That's a real good. Couples who parent well together often feel a deep kind of companionship, and it's one of the gifts of the season. But a marriage needs both postures. When the side-by-side becomes the only one, the couple can go for months without looking at each other, and they can mistake their efficient teamwork for intimacy.

Scripture holds both of these together in its picture of marriage. The woman in the Song of Songs says of her beloved, "This is my beloved and this is my friend" (Song of Songs 5:16). She doesn't choose between lover and friend. The prophet Malachi, rebuking the men of Judah who were divorcing their wives, says the Lord was witness "between you and the wife of your youth, to whom you have been faithless, though she is your companion and your wife by covenant" (Malachi 2:14). *Companion* is a word of partnership and association, and it stands beside the covenant, not below it.

The book of Ecclesiastes offers an even more pointed word to tired parents. Its preacher spends twelve chapters on the frustration of toil under the sun, the labor that never seems to finish and never satisfies. Yet in the middle of that meditation he writes: "Enjoy life with the wife whom you love, all the days of your vain life that he has given you under the sun, because that is your portion in life and in your toil at which you toil under the sun" (Ecclesiastes 9:9). The command to enjoy one's spouse is set right inside the toil, not after it. The preacher doesn't wait for the season of hard work to end before telling his reader to delight in the one he loves. The delight is the portion given within the toil. For a couple with small children, that may be the most practical verse in the Bible.

## What the children are learning

There is another reason the co-manager marriage matters, and it concerns the children themselves. Parents who pour everything into their children and nothing into their marriage often believe they are making a sacrifice for the children's good. But children learn far more from what they see than from what they're given. They are watching their parents all the time, and one of the things they're learning is what love between a man and a woman looks like. If what they see is two efficient colleagues who never delight in each other, they learn that marriage is logistics. They may grow up with every advantage their parents could give them and no picture at all of what a marriage is for.

The Psalms offer a corrective to the idea that children are a project to be managed: "Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD, the fruit of the womb a reward" (Psalm 127:3). A heritage is received, not produced. Children are given, not made, and they don't belong ultimately to their parents. The intensive project of parenting that Hays described can quietly turn children into something like a portfolio, a measure of the parents' success, and when that happens the marriage gets reorganized around the project. Every conversation becomes a conversation about the children, because the children have become the thing the couple is building together, and the couple has stopped being a thing worth building.

This is where most of us who are parents stand inside the indictment. It is easy to feel virtuous about the sacrifices we make for our children, and some of them really are virtuous. But some are a way of avoiding the harder, more exposing work of staying close to a spouse. Children are, in one sense, easier to love than a husband or wife. They need us in obvious ways, they're delighted by small things, and they don't yet see through us. A spouse does. Pouring ourselves into the children can become a hiding place from the person who knows us best. The church has sometimes reinforced this, organizing its whole life around programs for children and families, and rarely asking whether the couples at the center of those families are still friends.

The opposite error is real too, and it should be named just as plainly. Some parents protect their marriage or their own fulfillment at the cost of children who really do need them. Paul warns fathers, "do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (Ephesians 6:4), and there are children who are provoked to anger precisely by parents too absorbed in their own lives to notice them. The answer to the child-centered household is not an adult-centered one. It is a household whose center is the covenant love of the parents, which holds the children rather than competing with them.

## Keeping the friendship alive with no margin

What, then, can a couple actually do, in a season when there is almost no margin? The honest answer is that they can't do everything, and they shouldn't expect this season to feel like the one before the children came. But they can protect the friendship in small ways that add up.

The most important is the daily turning toward each other. Gottman's wider research keeps returning to small moments: whether spouses notice each other's attempts to connect and respond to them. A question asked at the end of the day that isn't about the schedule. A few minutes of conversation after the children are in bed before the phones come out. A hand on the shoulder in the kitchen. These things take almost no time, and they're the substance of friendship in a busy season.

Sabbath can help as well. The fourth commandment in Deuteronomy extends rest to the whole household, "you or your son or your daughter or your male servant or your female servant" (Deuteronomy 5:14), and it grounds that rest in the memory of slavery: "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 5:15). A household that never stops working has in some sense returned to Egypt. Couples who set aside a day, or even part of one, when the logistics are laid down and the family simply rests and worships together, often find that the friendship has room to breathe again.

The body needs patience in this season too. Many couples find their sexual relationship changes dramatically after children, through exhaustion, the physical recovery from childbirth, the demands of nursing, or simply a body that has been touched all day by small hands and wants to be left alone. That is normal, and it calls for patience and honest conversation, not pressure or resentment. If recovery is painful or slow, a physician can help, and this is not medical advice, only an encouragement to seek care. Postpartum depression and anxiety are also common and serious, and they can look from the outside like a marriage problem when they are a health problem. The National Maternal Mental Health Hotline answers around the clock by call or text at 1-833-852-6262, and Postpartum Support International's HelpLine, 1-800-944-4773, will connect you with support near you. If thoughts of self-harm arise, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline answers by call or text at 988.

Couples in this season also need help, and the church ought to give it. For most of history, young parents were surrounded by grandparents, siblings, cousins and neighbors who shared the care of children. Many modern couples have none of that nearby. A church can become that extended family: older couples who offer to watch the children so the parents can have an evening together, friends who bring meals, a community that asks the parents how they are, not only how the kids are. Psalm 127 opens, "Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain," and it continues, "It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives to his beloved sleep" (Psalm 127:1–2). The anxious toil of trying to hold everything together alone isn't what God asks. The house is his to build, and he often builds it through the hands of others.

## The house the children live in

The same verse that describes marriage in Genesis also describes what the children will one day do. "Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife" (Genesis 2:24). Children are meant to leave. The whole work of raising them is a work of preparing them to go. When they do, the couple who have been co-managers for twenty years will find themselves alone together in a quiet house, and they will discover whether there's still a marriage underneath the management.

Many couples find, at that point, that there isn't much left. Their conversations were all about the children, their shared life was all logistics, and without the enterprise they have nothing to say to each other. This site's essay on [why married couples drift apart](/writing/the-slow-drift-that-ends-marriages) traces that slow emptying further back than the children. Others find, with relief, that they kept something alive through the busy years, and that the friendship which went quiet for a season is still there. The difference is rarely made in the empty nest. It is made in the years of small children, in the small acts of attention that seemed too slight to matter.

Paul, writing about marriage, says something remarkable: "This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). A Christian marriage isn't only a structure for raising children. It is a sign, however faint, of the love between Christ and his people, a love that doesn't reduce the beloved to a function. Christ does not relate to the church as a manager relates to an employee. He loves her, gave himself for her, and delights in her. When children watch their parents, they are watching, whether anyone intends it or not, a small picture of that love.

The couple on the couch will still have to go over the schedule tomorrow night. The dentist appointment and the birthday present and the car tires aren't going anywhere. But they could put the phones down for five minutes first, and ask each other something that has nothing to do with the children. It won't feel like much. It is the difference between a house the children live in and a home that will still be there when they leave.
