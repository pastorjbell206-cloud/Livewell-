## 32. Most of What You Fight About Is Not What You Are Fighting About

**Slug:** `most-of-what-you-fight-about-is-not-what-you-are-fighting-about`
**Track:** `marriage` · **Audience:** `couples` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** The dishes are never the dishes. The money is never the money. The schedule is never the schedule. The fight is always about the deeper question — whether you are seen, whether you are honored, whether you matter in the way you needed to matter. Until you can name the deeper fight, the surface fights will keep coming back.

**Argument beats:**
1. The pattern: most marriages cycle through the same three or four fights for years. The fights are presented as content disputes. They are not content disputes. They are wounds repeating.
2. The deeper architecture: Bowen family systems theory. The wounds we bring from our families of origin become the script we re-enact in the marriage.
3. The pastoral move: stop arbitrating the content of the fights. Start naming the pattern. The arbitration is impossible because the content is not what is at stake.
4. What it looks like to name the pattern in real time: "We are fighting about the dishes. The dishes are not the issue. The issue is — what?"
5. The cost of not doing this: the marriage that fights the same fight for thirty years and ends in resigned distance. The marriage that names the pattern: the marriage that grows.
6. Self-implication: I fought the same fight with Susanna for nine years before either of us named what we were actually fighting about. Once we named it, the fight changed.
7. Verdict: name it. Name the pattern. The pattern is the marriage's actual project.

**Closing weight:** The marriage that wins the argument loses the marriage. The marriage that names the pattern under the argument has begun the actual work. The work is not about being right. The work is about being seen — and seeing — at the level where the wound lives. Most of us flinch from that level. The marriage that does not flinch is the marriage that lasts.

**Sources:** Edwin Friedman (*Generation to Generation*; *A Failure of Nerve*); Murray Bowen; John Gottman (*The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work*); Sue Johnson (*Hold Me Tight*).

## 33. Sex in Marriage Is Not What You Were Taught

**Slug:** `sex-in-marriage-is-not-what-you-were-taught`
**Track:** `marriage` · **Audience:** `couples` · **Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** Purity culture taught a generation of evangelicals to view sex as a problem solved by marriage. It is not solved by marriage. It is opened by marriage. The opening is harder than purity culture admitted, and richer than the surrounding culture has any vocabulary for.

**Argument beats:**
1. What purity culture promised: save sex for marriage and your marriage will be sexually fulfilling. The promise was false in both directions. Many people who waited were not fulfilled. Many people who did not wait were.
2. The theological truth: sex in marriage is not primarily about pleasure (though it is pleasurable). It is not primarily about bonding (though it bonds). It is about being known and remaining — being seen as you actually are, by the person you actually married, and not leaving when the seeing happens.
3. The data on Christian marriages: long-term sexual satisfaction correlates with neither frequency nor technique. It correlates with emotional safety, with conflict resolution, with the marriage's overall health. The bedroom is downstream of the dinner table.
4. The hard pastoral cases: low-libido spouses; mismatched desire; medical disruption; the ordinary erosion of years. Each requires a theology of sex that is not centered on performance.
5. The constructive: sex as one of marriage's languages of remaining. Not the most important. Not optional. One language among several, all of which the marriage has to be fluent in.
6. The verdict: forget what purity culture said. Forget what the surrounding culture says. The theology of sex in marriage is older, slower, more demanding, and more rewarding than either of them admits.

**Closing weight:** Sex is not the goal of marriage. Sex is one of marriage's languages of remaining. The marriage that learns this is the marriage that has stopped panicking about either too much or too little, and has started to do what the language was always for — be known, be honored, remain.

**Sources:** Christopher West (*Theology of the Body Made Simple* — even if you're not Catholic, the framework is rich); Tim and Kathy Keller (*The Meaning of Marriage*, the chapter on sex); Linda Kay Klein (*Pure*); Beth Allison Barr (*The Making of Biblical Womanhood*); the recent work of Camille Williams.

## 34. The Marriage That Survives Your Parents

**Slug:** `the-marriage-that-survives-your-parents`
**Track:** `marriage` · **Audience:** `couples` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** Every marriage is inheriting the dynamics of two prior marriages. Most couples are unconsciously re-enacting their parents' marriages. The marriage that becomes its own thing is the marriage that has done the explicit work of refusing the inheritance.

**Argument beats:**
1. The pattern: in conflict, in stress, in parenting, in money — most of us default to what our parents modeled. The default is invisible to us. It is also extraordinarily strong.
2. The theological reading: this is what scripture means by generational sin. Not curses passed down magically. Patterns absorbed unconsciously and re-enacted before we have the consciousness to refuse them.
3. The cost: most of the surprises in your marriage's first ten years are not your spouse failing to be the person you thought you married. They are your spouse's family-of-origin pattern colliding with yours, and both of you not knowing the collision is what is happening.
4. The work: explicit naming of the patterns. Some of what your parents did was good and worth keeping. Some of it was not and is worth refusing. The discrimination is the work. Without the discrimination, the unconscious wins.
5. What it looks like in practice: the seven sentences I have repeated to myself about what I will not do in conflict that my father did when present. The seven sentences Susanna has repeated about what she will not assume that her mother assumed.
6. The verdict: marry your spouse. Refuse your parents' marriage. The refusal is one of the kindest things you can do for the next generation.

**Closing weight:** Your marriage is a chance to break a pattern that has been running for three generations. Or to keep it running. The keeping is automatic. The breaking is intentional. The breaking is most of what mature Christian marriage actually is — generational repentance, lived out in the small Tuesday-afternoon decisions that no one outside the marriage sees.

**Sources:** Edwin Friedman; Bessel van der Kolk (*The Body Keeps the Score*); Henry Cloud and John Townsend (*Boundaries* — for the family-system frame, treated with appropriate distance from the pop-psych excesses); Tim Keller; the Bowen tradition.

## 35. The Wedding Is Not the Marriage

**Slug:** `the-wedding-is-not-the-marriage`
**Track:** `marriage` · **Audience:** `couples` · **Reading time:** ~7 min

**Positioning sentence:** The wedding industry sells the day. The Christian tradition is interested in the next sixty years. The conflation of the two has hollowed both, and has set up a generation of couples to be disappointed in the first six months of the rest of their lives.

**Argument beats:**
1. The wedding-industrial-complex frame: the average American wedding now costs $30,000 and is treated as the most important day of the marriage. It is one day. It is not even an especially important day.
2. The theological frame: the wedding is the *beginning*. The marriage is what happens after. The wedding ceremony, properly performed, is mostly a public commitment that does not yet have content — the content comes in the years of remaining.
3. The pastoral cost of the conflation: couples who have spent two years planning the wedding and zero years planning the marriage; couples whose first significant fight is in the third month after the wedding because the wedding has ended and they did not know what came next.
4. What pre-marital counseling should actually do: prepare couples for the first five years. Not for the wedding day.
5. What weddings should actually be: short, public, honored, low-budget, theologically rich. The historic Christian wedding rite is one of the most beautiful documents in the tradition. Read it. Most contemporary evangelical weddings are not actually performing it.
6. The verdict: spend less on the wedding. Spend more on the marriage. The marriage is the thing.

**Closing weight:** The wedding lasts a day. The marriage lasts sixty years if you are fortunate. The wedding's beauty is the beauty of a moment, and a moment is not enough to live on. The marriage's beauty is the beauty of a long fidelity, accumulated over decades, and a long fidelity is what every wedding day is, in fact, promising. Promise it well. Then go live the promise.

**Sources:** The Book of Common Prayer's marriage rite; Wendell Berry (essays on marriage); Tim Keller; Mike Cosper (*Recapturing the Wonder*); the work of David Brooks on the romantic-companionate model.
