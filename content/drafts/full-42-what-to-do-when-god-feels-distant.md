There is a season in most serious Christian lives when God feels absent. The prayers feel hollow. The scripture reading produces nothing. The worship that used to move you leaves you cold. The God you encountered with confidence at twenty-five seems, at thirty-five or forty-five or sixty-five, to have receded into a silence you cannot interrupt no matter what you do. The condition is normal. The condition is also one of the most underaddressed pastoral conditions in contemporary American evangelicalism, and the not-addressing of it has cost a generation of Christians their faith.

I want to write this essay for the reader who is in the condition, or who has been in it, or who watched a friend or spouse go through it without adequate pastoral help, or who is afraid of arriving at it and has not been given any tools for what to do when it comes. The condition is real. The condition has names. The names are useful, and the historic Christian tradition has accumulated substantial pastoral wisdom about how to live well through what it has variously called spiritual dryness, the dark night, desolation, or the experience of God's hiddenness. I want to share what I have found useful, as a pastor and as a Christian who has been through versions of this myself.

## What the condition actually is

I want to be precise about what I am describing, because the contemporary evangelical vocabulary for this is impoverished and the imprecision has been part of the problem.

The condition I am addressing is not, primarily, an intellectual doubt about Christian claims. The intellectual doubt is a related but distinct condition, and I have written about it elsewhere in this writing project, particularly in the skeptic track. The intellectual doubt is the wrestling with whether the Christian story is true. The condition I am addressing is the experience of God's apparent absence within a faith that is still intellectually held. The person in this condition continues to believe what he has always believed. He continues to attend church and to read scripture and to pray. The intellectual structure of his faith is intact. What is missing is the felt presence, the sense of God's nearness, the affective dimension of the relationship with God that had been a consistent feature of his Christian life and has, for reasons he cannot identify, dissolved.

The condition can persist for a season, for a year, for several years, sometimes for decades. The condition is not always uniform; there are days when the felt presence returns briefly, followed by longer stretches of the absence. The condition often follows seasons of unusual closeness to God, which makes the contrast more painful. The condition does not respond to the standard evangelical responses — try harder, pray more, read more scripture, attend more services. The standard responses sometimes deepen the condition rather than relieving it.

## What the historic tradition has called it

I want to introduce three names from the Christian tradition that may help, because the naming itself is part of how the condition becomes bearable.

The first name is *the dark night of the soul*, from John of the Cross, the sixteenth-century Spanish mystic and Carmelite reformer. John's small book *The Dark Night* describes the condition in detail and offers a theological account of what is happening. John's argument, briefly, is that God permits — even initiates — the apparent withdrawal of felt presence in order to purify the soul of attachments that have grown around the early experiences of God's nearness. The early Christian, John argues, encounters God's presence and naturally develops an attachment to the experience of presence itself. The attachment can become idolatrous; the Christian begins to seek the experience rather than seeking God, who happens to give the experience. God permits the withdrawal of felt presence to break the attachment and to teach the soul to love God for God's own sake rather than for the experiences God provides. The dark night is, in John's reading, a movement of God's love that initially feels like God's abandonment.

The second name is *desolation*, from the Jesuit tradition of Ignatius of Loyola. Ignatius developed an elaborate vocabulary for the spiritual movements of the soul, distinguishing between *consolation* — the experiences of God's presence, peace, encouragement — and *desolation* — the experiences of dryness, distance, discouragement. Ignatius's pastoral counsel is precise. In times of consolation, the Christian should receive the gift without grasping at it. In times of desolation, the Christian should not make significant decisions, should maintain the practices he established during consolation, and should remember that the desolation will pass. The Ignatian framework is more pragmatic than John's mystical theology and may be more accessible for many contemporary readers.

The third name is *the hiddenness of God*, from a more recent philosophical literature engaging the question of why a loving God would permit the experience of his apparent absence. The philosophical literature, including the work of John Schellenberg, frames the hiddenness as a problem for Christian theism. The theological literature, including the work of Michael Rea and others, frames it as a feature of God's relationship with finite creatures rather than as a defect of the relationship. The philosophical conversation can help contemporary Christians make sense of the experience in terms that integrate with the broader intellectual framework of their faith.

These are three names. They name overlapping but not identical conditions. Each is worth knowing about. The historic Christian tradition has been wrestling with these phenomena for centuries, and the wrestling has produced a substantial resource that the contemporary American evangelical Christian rarely encounters.

## What does not help

I want to name the responses that, in my pastoral experience, do not help the person in this condition, because the responses have been the standard evangelical advice and the advice has often deepened the condition rather than addressing it.

The advice to *try harder* does not help. The condition is not the result of insufficient effort. The condition is, in the historic traditions, the result of God's withdrawal rather than the person's spiritual laziness. The advice to try harder produces only more frustrated effort, and the frustrated effort often produces additional spiritual exhaustion that compounds the original condition.

The advice to *check for unrepented sin* sometimes helps but often does not. The condition can occur in the absence of any particular sin that the person has not addressed. The advice to look for hidden sin can produce, in scrupulous Christians, a kind of obsessive self-examination that becomes its own spiritual problem.

The advice to *just believe what scripture says* does not help, because the person already believes what scripture says. The intellectual structure of his faith is intact. The condition is not in the intellectual structure but in the affective experience, and the appeal to intellectual structure does not address the affective absence.

The advice to *seek emotional experiences in worship* does not help in this condition and may actively deepen it. The worship that produces emotional response can feel, to the person in dryness, like a manufactured experience that does not match his actual interior state. The forcing of emotion produces, in many cases, additional alienation from the experience that worship had previously been.

These are the standard responses. They are well-intentioned. They are not adequate to the condition. The adequacy requires a different vocabulary and a different pastoral approach.

## What actually helps

I want to name the responses that, in my pastoral experience and in the testimony of the historic tradition, have actually helped Christians live well through this condition.

The first response is naming. The Christian who can name what he is experiencing — *I am in a season of spiritual dryness, this is the dark night the tradition has talked about, this is the desolation Ignatius described* — has already done significant work. The naming locates the experience within the larger Christian tradition rather than treating it as a personal failure or as evidence that the faith was not real. The naming is what the contemporary evangelical context most consistently fails to provide, and the simple provision of the names produces, in many cases, immediate relief.

The second response is the maintenance of the practices established during seasons of consolation. The Ignatian counsel applies. The Christian who reads scripture devotionally during seasons of felt presence should continue reading scripture devotionally during seasons of dryness, even when the reading produces no felt response. The Christian who prays during seasons of felt presence should continue praying during seasons of dryness, even when the prayer feels hollow. The maintenance of the practices is not the production of felt experience; the maintenance is the persistence in the relationship even when the relationship feels one-sided. The persistence is, in the historic tradition, what the relationship most needs during this season.

The third response is the recovery of historic forms of prayer that do not depend on felt response. The Daily Office, the Liturgy of the Hours, the praying of the Psalms in their full range including the laments — these forms allow the Christian to pray in conditions where his own spontaneous prayer has dried up. The historic forms are particularly valuable in this season, because the historic forms carry the prayer of the church across the centuries, and the person in dryness can participate in the church's larger prayer even when his own internal prayer feels insufficient. The Psalter's lament tradition is especially important. Psalm 88 ends without resolution; the church has been praying Psalm 88 for three thousand years, and the prayer is appropriate in this condition.

The fourth response is the willingness to seek pastoral help. The person in sustained spiritual dryness should not face the condition alone. A spiritual director, a wise pastor, or a Christian friend who has been through the condition themselves can offer the perspective and accountability that the person in the dryness often cannot provide for himself. The willingness to seek help is part of the maturity of Christian discipleship. The historic monastic traditions assumed that the soul in dryness would have access to such a guide; the contemporary individualized Christian life often does not include the guide, and the absence is part of what makes the condition harder than it has to be.

The fifth response is the recognition that the condition is a phase rather than a permanent state. The historic tradition is consistent on this. The dark night passes. The desolation gives way, eventually, to renewed consolation, often in forms different from the original experiences. The Christian who has lived through a season of dryness emerges, in most cases, with a faith that is deeper, less dependent on felt experience, and more capable of sustained obedience under various conditions than the faith he had before the dryness. The dryness is, in the historic reading, formative rather than punitive. The recognition of the formative purpose can sustain the person through the period when no formation is visible.

## The verdict

There is a season in most Christian lives when God feels distant. The condition is normal. The condition has names. The historic Christian tradition has substantial resources for living well through it, and the contemporary American evangelical context has, on the whole, failed to transmit those resources to the lay reader who needs them.

I am writing this for the reader who is in the condition and has been told he should not be, for the reader who has been told to try harder when trying harder is not what he needs, for the reader who has wondered whether his faith was ever real because the felt presence that originally drew him has gone. The faith is real. The condition is normal. The historic tradition has been here. The path through has been walked by saints across two thousand years, and the path is available to you.

Name what you are experiencing. Maintain the practices. Pray the Psalms. Find a spiritual director or a wise pastor. Remember that this is a season, not the rest of your life. The God who feels absent is the God who has been present through the entire history of his church, and who has been present in your own history, and who is present now in ways the felt experience does not register but the faith continues to know. The faith is the substance. The feelings come and go. The persistence in the faith through the dryness is the practice that most consistently produces the mature Christian life across decades.

The next essay in this writing project takes up the related condition of Christian doubt — not the active loss of intellectual conviction, but the lower-grade questioning that often accompanies the spiritual dryness I have been describing. The doubt and the dryness are related but distinct, and the distinguishing has been part of the pastoral work the historic tradition has done well.
