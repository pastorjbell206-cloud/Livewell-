Somewhere in the last century, hospitality became a personality type.

You are either a "hospitable person" — someone who naturally loves hosting, who keeps a clean house and knows how to make guests feel welcome — or you are not, and the church mostly leaves you alone about it. The word appears on church websites as a gift of the Spirit for the few, a ministry for the kitchen volunteers, a season of life for people whose children are grown and whose table is large enough. It is optional. It is for those who feel called.

This is not the biblical account.

The Hebrew word for hospitality, when it appears in the commands to Israel, is not about personality. It is embedded in covenant obligation. The stranger, the alien, the foreigner traveling through — these are to be welcomed because you were a stranger in Egypt, and the God who freed you has not forgotten what it means to be excluded (Leviticus 19:33–34). Hospitality in the ancient Near Eastern world was not sentiment. It was survival. To turn away a traveler was to leave them to die. To welcome them was to take responsibility for their life. The command does not ask whether you feel ready for the obligation. It assumes the obligation.

By the time Paul writes to the Roman church, the word he uses — philoxenia — means literally "love of the stranger" (Romans 12:13). Not tolerance of the stranger. Not accommodation of the stranger. Love. He places it in a list alongside blessing those who persecute you and weeping with those who weep — not as a lesser item, but as a posture so fundamental that the absence of it is the absence of the gospel.

I have stood in my own church and watched people walk in the door alone and leave alone, and I have told myself that we were a welcoming congregation because we had greeters at the door. We had a hospitality team. We had a system. What we did not have, in those moments, was hospitality — because hospitality is not a system. It is a posture of the self toward the other that says: you are not a visitor here. You are kin. Your presence makes this room more itself, not less.

The early church gathered in homes precisely because the home is the primary site of hospitality. When the church moved into basilicas and cathedrals, it did not simply change architecture. It changed the social grammar of welcome. The private home says: I am letting you into my life. The public building says: you may enter the facility. These are not the same posture. One is intimate. One is institutional. The church that only has institutional hospitality — programs for inclusion, diversity initiatives, welcome teams in branded shirts — has not yet practiced what the New Testament demands.

Stefan, a Congolese refugee who attended a church I know [cite — confirm], said that what he noticed first was not whether the church had a welcome process. He noticed whether anyone ate with him. Anyone can shake your hand at the door. The question is who sits down. The question is who shares the meal.

This is why hospitality is a justice issue, not merely a courtesy issue. The people most likely to be turned away — the homeless, the mentally ill, the recently incarcerated, the undocumented, the poor who do not dress for the building they are entering — are the very people the prophets name when they describe the test of faithful community. The widow, the orphan, the stranger. The ones without advocates. The ones whose presence makes the comfortable congregation slightly less comfortable.

The church that welcomes only the people who look like it is not practicing hospitality. It is practicing affinity. Affinity is pleasant. Hospitality is costly. The difference between them is the willingness to let someone who is not like you occupy your space, sit at your table, make demands on your time — and to receive that not as a burden but as the arriving of something holy.

The risen Christ, in the Gospel of Luke, makes himself known at table (Luke 24:30–31). Not in the synagogue. Not in a lecture. At a table, in the breaking of bread, with strangers who did not recognize him until the meal.

He is still doing this. The question is whether we are setting the table.
