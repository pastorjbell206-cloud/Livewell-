We didn't come to faith because we ran out of arguments. We came because the argument we were most proud of turned out to be the weakest one we owned.

Let me start by conceding the thing my own side keeps getting wrong, because it embarrasses me every time I hear it. The claim that an atheist cannot be good is false. It is not subtle-false. It is obviously, publicly, humiliatingly false, and the unbeliever reading this knows it in his bones, because he has watched a churchgoer lie and watched a secular humanist keep his word, and he has done the arithmetic. I have too. I spent years outside the faith, and I was not a monster. I gave blood. I told the truth when it cost me. I would have run into traffic for a stranger's kid without stopping to consult a worldview. When I hear a believer suggest that people like the man I was are one philosophy seminar away from the gulag, I want to leave the room. It is a slander. Drop it.

And it is not only false. It is strategically suicidal, because every skeptic can see the counterexample in his own kitchen. He has an atheist mother who is kinder than most deacons. He has read about the quiet unbelievers who hid the hunted while baptized Christians looked away. The moment a Christian argues that goodness requires belief, he has handed the honest doubter a reason to stop listening, and the doubter is right to take it. So I will say it as plainly as I can, from inside the church that keeps forgetting: you do not need God to be good, and anyone who tells you otherwise is not defending the faith. He is defending his own comfort. Those are not the same thing.

So that argument is dead. Good. Let it stay dead.

But killing the bad argument is not the same as ending the conversation, and I think the skeptic knows that too, because there is a different question underneath, and it is harder, and it does not go away just because the cheap version got buried. The cheap question was: can you behave well without God? Obviously yes. The real question is quieter and stranger. When you say something is *wrong* — not distasteful to you, not disfavored by your society, but wrong — what exactly are you saying? And why does it feel like a wall instead of a wish?

Here is the test case, and I want to hand it to you the way it was once handed to me, because it did not let me sleep. Somewhere tonight a man is hurting a child for his own pleasure. Now hold still and notice what you believe about that. You do not believe it is wrong *for you*. You do not believe it is wrong *in our culture*. You believe it is wrong — full stop, everywhere, in every century, and that it would remain wrong if every human being on earth were persuaded to applaud it. If the whole species voted tomorrow to call it good, you would not conclude that it had become good. You would conclude that the whole species had gone to hell. That conviction is not a preference. It has the texture of a fact. It presses on you from the outside like a law you did not write and cannot repeal.

I want to give the naturalist his strongest account of where that pressure comes from, because he has one, and it is not stupid, and Christians who wave it away have not read it. The account runs like this. We are social primates, shaped by a few million years of living or dying in groups. The creatures who felt a hot, non-negotiable revulsion at the betrayal of the vulnerable were the creatures whose bands held together, and held-together bands out-survived the bands that came apart. So evolution did not install a preference. It installed something that *feels* like a law, because a mere preference would have been too weak to hold a tribe through famine and war. On top of that biological floor we built social contracts — rules we agree to because a world where the strong may torture the weak is a world none of us would rationally sign up to live in, since any of us might end up the weak one. Add reason, add empathy, add the slow accumulation of moral progress, and you can explain the entire moral life without invoking anything supernatural at all. This is a serious position. It is held by serious people. It got a great deal right, and I will not pretend otherwise.

But I want to press it exactly where it is thinnest, and only there. Notice what that account has actually explained. It has explained why we *feel* the pressure. It has given a story about the origin of the sensation of obligation. What it has not done — what by its own logic it cannot do — is tell you whether the pressure is tracking anything real. There is a difference, and everything hangs on it. An account of why I experience the number seven as prime is not an account of whether seven is prime. If our horror at cruelty is fully explained as a survival mechanism that helped our ancestors pass on genes, then the horror is a very useful feeling, and the child's torture is disfavored by a feeling evolution gave us for reasons that had nothing to do with the child. On that story, the wrongness is not in the act. It is in us. And a thing that is only in us is a thing that could have been otherwise, and might yet be revised, and cannot finally judge a society that has revised it. This is not my clever objection. It is a worry raised by philosophers on every side of the God question — that a purely evolutionary account explains the moral emotions without underwriting the moral facts, that you can have the whole apparatus of conscience and still have no ground under it. The theme runs through Dostoevsky like a crack through a wall: pull the floor out, and everything is permitted — not because the atheist will suddenly start doing evil, but because he loses the standing to call anything evil in the last analysis.

Steelman it once more, because the naturalist has a reply and it deserves the room. He can say: I do not need a cosmic guarantee. The wrongness of torturing a child is more obvious to me than any theory you could offer me about its source, and I will trust the datum and let the metaphysics fall where it may. If my ethics floats free of a foundation, so much the worse for foundations. That is an honest answer, and it is a brave one, and I respect the man who gives it more than I respect the Christian who has never once felt the vertigo underneath his own certainties.

But notice what he has actually done. He has treated the wrongness as more certain than any naturalist explanation of it — which means he is trusting the moral fact and quietly demoting the theory that was supposed to account for it. He is behaving, in the one place it counts most, as though the wrongness were simply *there*, woven into the structure of things, binding whether he evolved to feel it or not. He is living as though morality were discovered and not invented. And that is precisely the intuition a naturalist worldview has the hardest time paying for. C.S. Lewis built the whole moral argument on this pivot in *Mere Christianity* — not that unbelievers behave badly, he never said that, but that the *ought* pressing on all of us behaves less like an appetite we happen to have and more like a command from somewhere we did not manufacture. You can reject his conclusion. I did, for a while. But you have to reckon with the shape of the thing, and the shape is stubborn.

Let me be careful about what this is and is not. This is not a proof. I have no interest in selling you a proof, because I do not have one, and a man who came to faith from atheism is allergic to being handed certainties he did not earn. This is evidence. It is the fact that when you and I stand over the worst thing in the world, we both reach for a word — *wrong* — that means more than "I don't like this" and more than "we don't do this here," and that neither of us can talk himself out of at three in the morning. Your naturalism can explain the feeling. It has a harder time explaining why you are right to trust it. That gap is not nothing. It is at least worth sitting in.

And I have to say the last part, or I have said nothing, because the theist can misuse this worse than the atheist ever could. Christians have hurt children too, and covered for the men who did, and quoted the God of the moral law while breaking it in the parish house. Having a foundation is not the same as standing on it. I know men with the whole architecture of grounded morality who are crueler than the honest unbeliever I used to be. So I am not handing you a trophy. I am handing you a question I could not answer as an atheist and can only half-answer now.

You already know some things are wrong even if everyone approves.

The only question is what that knowledge is resting on.
