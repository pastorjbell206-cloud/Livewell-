The argument I could never answer as an atheist was not the one about miracles, or the reliability of manuscripts, or whether a man can rise from the dead. Those felt like arguments you could win with enough reading. The one I could not get past was simpler, and it did not go away when I became a Christian. A child is dying somewhere as you read this. Slowly, in pain, watched by parents who would trade their own lives in an instant and are not given the option. If there is a God who could stop it and does not, then either he is not good or he is not there. That is the argument. I want to state it at full strength, because most of what passes for a Christian answer to it is a way of not looking at it.

So look at it. Not the abstraction, "the problem of evil," a phrase clean enough to put on a syllabus. The particular. The oncology ward. The refugee column. The phone call at two in the morning. Dostoevsky put this more fiercely than any philosopher, through the character of Ivan in *The Brothers Karamazov*, who piles up the suffering of children not to score a point but to refuse the whole arrangement, who says that if the price of some final harmony is the tears of one tortured child, the price is too high, and he would return the ticket. He does not deny God. He rejects the world God runs. I have never read a stronger version of the objection, and I have never met a Christian answer that Ivan could not have anticipated and swept off the table before breakfast.

Because the answers are too clean. Let me name them honestly, the way their best defenders would.

The free will defense says God made us free, and freedom that cannot choose evil is not freedom, so the cruelty in the world is the cost of the only kind of love worth having, love that could have refused. There is something true in that. A world of puppets would not contain love, and I would not want it. But stand in a children's hospital and try the argument out loud. The leukemia did not choose. The earthquake did not choose. The tumor pressing on a four-year-old's brain stem is not exercising its freedom. Free will covers the torturer. It does not cover the cells. And even where a human will is the cause, the drunk driver, the man who hurt you when you were small, "God valued the freedom to do that more than he valued protecting you" is not a comfort. It is a second wound.

The soul-making answer says suffering forms us, that no one becomes deep or brave or kind without something to press against, that the world is a place for growing souls and growth requires resistance. Again, partly true. I have watched grief make people larger. I have been made by things I would not have chosen. But say it at the graveside of an infant and hear how obscene it sounds. What soul was made? Whose growth required that? The argument works only for the suffering that produces a visible result, and most suffering produces no result you can point to. It just grinds people down until they are gone. A theodicy that only explains the useful pain has explained almost nothing.

The greater good answer says God permits each evil because it serves a larger purpose we cannot see, that the whole will one day be revealed as good and every tear will be justified in the accounting. This is the one that finally breaks in my hands. Because it turns the child into a means. It says the suffering was instrumental, a cost paid toward an end. And the moment you say that, you have agreed with Ivan. You have said the ticket was worth buying. I do not believe God balances his books in children. I would not worship him if he did.

Here is what I have to confess. I have preached versions of all three. I have handed grieving people a tidy explanation because the explanation protected me, from the silence, from my own helplessness, from the terror of standing next to a suffering I could not fix and having nothing to say. The theodicies comfort the person who is not suffering. They are what the well say to the sick so the well can leave the room. I did that. I told a widow that God had a plan, and I watched something close in her face, and I mistook it for peace. It was not peace. It was her deciding I was not safe to grieve in front of.

And then I went back and read the Bible actually, instead of the Bible I had been taught to quote, and I found something that undid my whole apparatus.

The Bible mostly refuses to do this. The book set aside for the problem of suffering, Job, is forty chapters long, and thirty-something of them are Job's friends offering theodicies. Every answer I just listed, they say better. And at the end God shows up and tells the friends they have spoken wrongly of him, and he tells Job nothing. He does not explain. He answers out of the whirlwind with questions of his own, "Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth?" (Job 38:4), and the strange thing, the thing that took me years to feel, is that Job is satisfied not because he got an answer but because he got a Presence. The book of Job is in the Bible to burn down the case the friends are making. It is the canon's rejection of the tidy answer, printed in full, endorsed by God.

And the prayer book of Israel is soaked in complaint. The Psalms of lament are not a minor genre, they are a large share of the whole book, and they do not resolve. "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?" (Psalm 13:1). That is in the hymnal. God authorized his people to say that to his face. Not to explain the suffering. To protest it, to him, at volume. The faith did not begin by answering the question of pain. It began by giving people permission to scream it upward and calling that prayer.

This is messier than a theodicy. It is also the only thing I have ever seen hold up at a hospital bed. Because a suffering person does not need the metaphysics of their suffering. They need to know they are not alone in it, and they need permission to be angry without being told they are failing God. Lament gives them both. It is honest about the pain in a way the explanations never are.

But lament alone would only be a permission to despair, and I will not leave you there, because the story does not.

The center of the Christian claim is not an argument about suffering. It is a crucified God. Whatever else Christianity is, it is the strange assertion that the God accused of standing at a safe distance did not stay there, that he came down into the specific human machinery of pain, the betrayal and the flogging and the slow suffocation, and that on the cross he cried the opening line of a lament psalm: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34, quoting Psalm 22). Read that again. In the hour the whole edifice of Christianity rests on, God himself is praying a complaint psalm. He is not explaining suffering from above it. He is inside it, using the language Israel used, the language of a man who feels abandoned by God, while being God.

I have to be careful here, because the cross can be weaponized into exactly the tidy answer I have been tearing down. "See, God suffered too, so stop complaining." No. That is the greater-good move wearing a crown of thorns. The cross does not explain your child's cancer. It does not tell you why. It tells you something smaller and harder and more livable, that whatever this is, God has not run it from the observation deck. He has been where you are. The shortest verse in the Bible is two words at the tomb of a friend the writer says he was about to raise from the dead anyway: "Jesus wept" (John 11:35). He knew resurrection was minutes away and he still stood in the graveyard and cried. The God of the Bible does not treat your grief as a problem to be corrected by better information. He weeps in it.

I cannot tell you why there is so much suffering. I have been a pastor for fifteen years and I have buried children and I have sat with men whose wives were disappearing into a disease that eats the mind before it takes the body, and I have nothing for the question that Ivan would not knock down. I have made my peace with not having it. What I have instead is a book that refuses to hand me the answer I keep reaching for, a genre of prayer that lets me be furious and still be praying, and a God with scars.

That is not a solution. If you came here for one, I have failed you, and I would rather fail you than lie. The pain is still pain. The child is still dying. The argument still stands where it stood.

But I no longer think the people who kneel in the wreckage are fools. They are not clutching an explanation. They are holding onto a hand in the dark, and telling the truth about how dark it is.
