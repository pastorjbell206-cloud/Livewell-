We built a world that runs without God, and it runs. That is the first thing an honest believer has to say to an honest atheist, and most of us will not say it. The lights come on. The antibiotics work. The bridge holds because a man who prayed to no one calculated the load correctly. For three hundred years the most powerful method human beings have ever devised for finding out what is true has proceeded on the working assumption that it need not consult heaven, and it has been spectacularly, humiliatingly right about almost everything it has touched. If you were an atheist because of this, you were not being lazy. You were noticing something real.

I was that atheist until I was twenty-four. And I want to grant the case at its strongest before I say anything else, because the church has a habit of arguing with a version of unbelief no serious unbeliever holds.

The strong case runs like this. Every time we did not know how something worked, we said God. Lightning, disease, the motion of the planets, the origin of species, the origin of the cosmos itself. And every time, when we finally learned the mechanism, God was not in it. Not disproven. Just unnecessary. The pattern is so consistent that betting against it looks like the only rational move left. The believer keeps retreating to the shrinking territory of the not-yet-explained and planting his flag there, and the explanation keeps arriving, and the flag keeps moving. The name for the tenant of that shrinking house is the God of the gaps, and he has been evicted so many times that a thoughtful person is right to stop renting to him. When Charles Taylor describes how the modern West came to inhabit what he calls the immanent frame — a world experienced as a closed order of natural causes, where transcendence is at most an option and no longer the air we breathe — he is not describing a mistake. He is describing an achievement. We can now give a complete account of the middle of the world without ever leaving the world. That is not nothing. That is the water we all swim in, believers included, and anyone who tells you he has stepped outside it is lying or has not noticed.

So I am not going to hand you a gap. I am going to point at four things that are not gaps at all, because they do not shrink when the science advances. They are the same size now as they were before we knew anything, and they will be the same size when we know everything a materialist account could ever deliver. I am not going to call them proof. I am going to refuse to let them be waved away.

The first is that the universe is intelligible at all. Science does not merely work; it works absurdly well, and it works by assuming in advance that reality is written in a language the human mind can read — mathematics, deep, elegant, and shockingly predictive. The steelman here is strong, so take it. Of course the universe is comprehensible to us. We are its products. Minds that could not track reality got eaten. Evolution tuned our cognition to the world because a false map gets you killed at the watering hole. Grant all of it. Then notice what it does not reach. Natural selection can explain why I can count the lions and judge the distance to the river. It has no obvious purchase on why the same organ that dodged the lion can also derive general relativity, describe the behavior of particles it will never touch, and predict, from an equation scratched on paper, a planet no one has seen. The survival value of abstract higher mathematics is zero on the savanna. We got a tool fitted for the watering hole and found it fits the deep structure of the cosmos, and no one ordered that fit. The physicist Eugene Wigner named it long ago — the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics — and naming a thing is not the same as accounting for it. The reasonable expectation was a mind that muddled through. We got a mind that reads the source code. That is strange. It should be allowed to stay strange.

The second is the one the materialist himself keeps circling back to, uneasy. Consciousness. Not intelligence, not behavior, not the information processing a machine can do. The fact that there is something it is like to be you. That the wavelength we label red is also, from the inside, the experienced redness of red, and no description of the wavelength, however total, contains that. The honest naturalist does not pretend this is solved. Thomas Nagel, an atheist, has spent a career refusing to pretend it, worrying in print that the standard reductive story leaves something out and might be leaving out something enormous. David Chalmers gave the difficulty its working name — the hard problem — and it is called hard for a reason that has not gone away. The steelman: consciousness is what a sufficiently complex brain does, we simply have not finished the science, and the confidence of the not-yet is a bad bet given the track record. Fair. But every other thing the brain does, we can at least say what a solution would look like. Here we cannot even say what would count as one. We can specify every neuron and still not know why the lights are on inside. A universe that started as dead matter and blind force has somehow, in us, opened its eyes and asked what it is. That either is the most ordinary fact in the world or the most extraordinary. It cannot be both. We have agreed to call it ordinary. I am not sure we have earned that.

The third is where I have to be most careful, because it is the one most easily cheapened. Not morality. Obligation. The materialist explains moral behavior with ease, and I concede it fully. Cooperation is fit. Empathy is selected. Kin altruism, reciprocal altruism, the whole apparatus by which a social animal comes to feel that cruelty is wrong — all of it can be told as a story about genes surviving, and told well. That is not the remainder. The remainder is that we do not experience morality as a feeling we happen to have. We experience it as a claim made on us from outside, one that stands over our feelings and judges them. When I say the man who tortures a child for pleasure is wrong, I do not mean I dislike it, or my tribe evolved to punish it, or it fails to maximize welfare. I mean he is wrong, and would be wrong if every human alive had evolved to applaud him. The steelman is the honest one: that conviction is exactly the illusion natural selection would install, because a creature that felt morality as mere preference would defect the moment defecting paid, and a creature that felt it as iron law would not. So the bindingness is a trick, useful and deep and false. That is a coherent view. Live in it. Live all the way in it, and watch what happens to the sentence the torturer is really, bindingly wrong. It does not survive. It becomes the torturer is something I was built to hate. And every one of us, the atheist included — I know, because I was him — goes on treating certain things as really wrong, wrong with a weight no story about our ancestors can hold up. We cannot get the obligation out of our mouths. We only learn to stop asking where it comes from.

The fourth is quieter and I will not press it hard. We treat truth and beauty as though they matter in themselves. The scientist who spends her life on a question that will never feed her, because the truth is worth having. The grief that a false thing is false even when the false thing was comforting. The way a theorem or a coastline or a piece of music can stop a person as if it were owed something. On the strict account these are refined appetites, byproducts, the mind's pleasure in pattern. Maybe. But we do not live as if they were appetites. We live as if we were answerable to them.

I want to say clearly what this is and is not. It is not four proofs. Each has a naturalist reply that is intelligent and might be right, and I have tried to give those replies in the form their defenders would sign. A God assembled from these four would be exactly the tenant of the shrinking house, and I already agreed he deserves eviction. This is something smaller and more stubborn. It is a refusal.

The remainder is real. The intelligibility we did not order, the light that came on in the dark, the obligation we cannot stop obeying, the truth and beauty we serve as if they could ask something of us. The mature response is not to solve them tonight. It is to stop pretending they are already solved, and to sit with the size of what a complete materialism would still have to carry.

You do not have to believe anything to admit the account is not yet closed. I only ask that when the remainder comes up, you not do the thing I used to do. I waved it away because I already knew there was nothing there. I never checked. The waving was the faith.
