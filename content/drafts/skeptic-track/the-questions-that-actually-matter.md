The questions apologetics answers are not the questions I was asking.

I know this because I spent six years as an atheist, and then I spent fifteen years as a pastor watching Christians answer the atheist I never was. Someone would hand me a book, and it would be four hundred pages proving that the universe had a first cause, that the fine-tuning of the constants was improbable, that a Galilean tomb was likely empty on a Sunday morning in the spring of a year around AD 30. Good arguments, some of them. Careful. And every one of them aimed at a man standing three feet to the left of where I was actually standing.

Let me concede the strongest version of the skeptic's complaint first, because I lived inside it. Most of what gets sold as a defense of Christianity is a defense against objections the believer imagines the unbeliever has. It is a war game played against a cardboard opponent. The apologist decides in advance that my problem is intellectual, that I have simply not seen the syllogism yet, and then he wins the syllogism and looks up expecting me to convert, and cannot understand why I have quietly left the room. I did not leave because the argument failed. I left because the argument was answering a question I had stopped asking, if I ever asked it at all.

So before this track earns the right to say anything, it owes you an accounting of which questions are real. Not the ones that make for a tidy debate. The ones you actually carry.

Here is the first honest admission. I did not lie awake at night over whether the cosmological argument holds. I want to be careful here, because the argument is not stupid, and pretending it is would be its own dishonesty, some of the sharpest people I know find the question of why there is something rather than nothing to be the crack through which everything else eventually leaks. But most nights, for most people, that question does not have teeth. It is a chess problem. You can lose the game and go make a sandwich. The questions that have teeth are the ones you cannot make a sandwich after.

I lay awake over other things.

I lay awake over the fact that I was going to die, and that everyone I loved was going to die, and that on the account I then believed, the only intellectually respectable account, I was sure, this was not a tragedy but a fact, no more mournful than water finding its level. I believed we were the temporary arrangement of atoms that had briefly become able to notice itself, and would soon stop noticing, and that the noticing would leave no trace the universe could read. I believed this. And I could not make myself feel it. Every honest atheist I have ever known lives in exactly that gap, between the death he affirms with his mind and the grief he cannot stop his body from staging at a graveside. That gap is not an argument. It is a wound the worldview cannot close, and it does not care whether you have read the right books.

That is the terrain. Not the debate stage. The gap between what we say we believe about ourselves and what we cannot stop acting like is true.

Consider meaning, which is the question apologists reach for and then ruin. They tend to argue that without God your life has no meaning, as if that were a threat, and the thoughtful unbeliever rightly answers that he makes his own, his children, his work, the person asleep beside him, the particular slant of light in October. He is right. He does make meaning, and it is real meaning, and I will not insult him by pretending the meaning he has built is a delusion. But there is a harder question underneath the easy one, and it is the question that actually keeps a person up. Not "does my life have meaning?" It is: why does the meaning I make feel like something I am discovering rather than something I am inventing? Why does love feel like a country I entered, not a thing I manufactured? An invented meaning should feel like mine. This one keeps feeling like it came from somewhere.

Which is the persistence of longing, and I think it is the most undertreated fact in the whole conversation. The philosopher Charles Taylor described the condition of the modern West as living inside what he called the immanent frame, a closed room of natural cause and effect, where the transcendent is not so much refuted as simply made optional, one setting among others. And his sharpest observation was not that belief has become impossible. It is that unbelief has not become easy. The person who has fully accepted that there is nothing beyond the material world still finds himself, at a certain hour, ambushed by a longing that points at nothing the material world contains. Taylor called this being cross-pressured, haunted from both directions, unable to fully rest in the closed room and unable to walk back out of it. I have lived on both sides of that pressure. It does not resolve by reading. It just sits there, aching, pointing.

C.S. Lewis built an argument out of exactly this ache, the observation that our hungers tend to correspond to real things, that thirst suggests water and hunger suggests food, and that we carry one desire nothing in the world will feed. I am not offering that to you as a proof; Lewis's argument has real holes and a skeptic can name them in his sleep. I am offering the observation underneath the argument, which no atheism I ever held could explain away: the longing did not go when I stopped believing. It got worse. A worldview that tells me my deepest and most persistent hunger is aimed at nothing at all is asking me to believe that I am, at my core, a mistake. Maybe I am. But that is a strange thing for evolution to spend so much energy building.

Then there is the weight of right and wrong, and here I have to be most careful of all, because Christians have made a bad argument out of a real thing so many times that skeptics now flinch when the subject comes up. The bad argument says an atheist cannot be good without God. That is false, and worse than false, it is insulting, and I have known unbelievers whose integrity shamed me in my collar. So set it aside entirely. The real question is not whether you can be good. It is why, when you watch a strong man hurt a child, your reaction has the shape of a fact and not a preference. You do not feel that cruelty is distasteful the way you find a food distasteful. You feel that it is wrong the way you feel that two and two is four, as if you have bumped into something that was already there, that would still be there if every human being on earth voted to approve it. Every serious atheist I know either bites the bullet and says that moral weight is an illusion evolution installed to keep us cooperating, or he refuses to bite it and lives, quietly, as if the child's dignity were carved into the structure of things. I could never bite the bullet. I could never fully believe that my horror at cruelty was only a feeling with good press.

And underneath all of it, love, the one I saved for last because it is the one that finally cornered me. The account I held said that love is chemistry with a story attached, a set of neurochemical events that bond mammals long enough to raise the young. I believed that. It is not obviously wrong. But I stood in a room once and felt, toward another person, something that behaved in every way like a fact about the universe and not a fact about my hormones, and I had to decide whether to trust the experience or the theory that dissolved it. Every honest person eventually stands in that room. You do not get to be a human being and stay neutral on whether the thing you feel toward the people you would die for is really only the sensation of your genes protecting their investment.

Notice what I have not done. I have not answered a single one of these. I have not told you the longing proves God, or the moral weight requires him, or that love descends from somewhere. Those are moves, and if I made them now I would be doing the exact thing I accused the apologists of, winning a game you did not agree to play. This first essay has one job, and it is smaller and more honest than that. It is to say: I know which questions are real. I know the difference between the ones that make a good debate and the ones that follow you into the dark. And I am promising you, for the six essays after this one, that I will not cheat, I will not pretend the closed room has exits it does not have, and I will not pretend it is airtight when we both know, at three in the morning, that it is not.

You do not have to believe anything yet. You only have to agree that these are the questions. That death is a wound the mind affirms and the body refuses. That the meaning you make feels found. That the longing does not leave. That cruelty feels like a fact. That love asks to be more than chemistry, and you have never quite been able to tell it no.

If those are your questions, you are not standing three feet to the left anymore.

You are standing exactly where I was.
