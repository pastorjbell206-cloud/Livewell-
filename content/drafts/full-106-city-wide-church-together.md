There is a city that the prophet Jeremiah addressed in a letter to the exiles, and the instruction he gave them was this: "Seek the shalom of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its shalom you will find your shalom" (Jeremiah 29:7). He did not say: seek the shalom of your particular congregation. He said the city. The whole city.

Most of us preach that text and then go back to competing with the church three blocks over for the same pool of transfer Christians.

I have done this. The competition is real, and it is almost never acknowledged honestly. Pastors will talk about the kingdom while keeping meticulous track of who left their church to attend the newer one with the better sound system. We dress it in the language of stewardship — we are responsible for our own flock — and there is truth in that. But the stewardship argument has a way of becoming cover for the thing it is covering: the provincialism of the local church at the expense of the body of Christ in a given place.

The early church in Jerusalem did not operate as a collection of independent congregations competing for brand loyalty. Acts 2:44-46 describes people who held things in common, who broke bread from house to house, who operated as a single community across geographic space. The development of distinct congregations with distinct identities was a later development, driven partly by growth and partly by schism — and the epistles of Paul are largely an effort to prevent those local communities from fragmenting into factions that owed their loyalty to a personality rather than to Christ.

What does it look like to take Jeremiah 29 and Acts 2 seriously in a mid-sized city in 2026?

At minimum, it looks like the pastors of that city knowing each other. Not performing unity at a ministerial breakfast while quietly regarding each other as competitors. Actually knowing each other — the struggles, the failures, the things that keep them awake. The referral network described elsewhere in these essays depends on this. But it is not primarily strategic. It is theological. Pastors who regard themselves as co-laborers in a single city-wide mission will make different decisions than pastors who regard themselves as CEOs of rival enterprises.

At least once a year, it ought to be visible to the congregation.

A city-wide gathering — churches from different traditions, different demographics, different theological emphases, meeting in a park or an arena or the largest sanctuary that will hold them — does something that no individual congregation can do alone. It makes visible what is otherwise invisible: that the church in this city is one thing, larger than any of its expressions, more durable than any single pastor's tenure, accountable to a story that began before any of us arrived and will continue after all of us have gone.

The practical objections are real. Theology divides — Reformed and Pentecostal, Baptist and Methodist, churches that practice closed communion and churches with open tables. Logistics are difficult. Egos are involved. Somebody will not want to share a stage with somebody else, and the reasons will be dressed in doctrinal language while being mostly personal.

I understand all of that. I have been the pastor who found reasons not to show up.

But here is what the gathering does that the objections cannot account for: it forms the congregation. When the people in your church see their pastor standing alongside the pastor of the Black Baptist church across town and the pastor of the Latino congregation in the strip mall, and they worship together to music that is not what any of them expected, they learn something about the body of Christ that no sermon can teach them at the same depth. They see that the church is bigger than their church. That the Spirit is not confined to their worship style. That the kingdom extends beyond the zip code they drive to on Sunday morning.

That formation is worth the awkwardness. It is worth the logistics. It may even be worth the theological discomfort.

Jeremiah sent his letter to people who wanted to go home. He told them instead to plant gardens and pray for the city they were in.

We have been planted in this city. The question is whether we are praying for it together.
