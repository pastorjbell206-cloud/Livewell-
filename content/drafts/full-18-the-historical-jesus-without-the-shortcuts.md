Most popular Christian arguments for the historical Jesus rely on shortcuts mainstream scholarship would not endorse. Most popular secular dismissals rely on shortcuts mainstream scholarship would not endorse either. The actual scholarly conversation is more interesting than both. I want to walk through it.

I have been writing this series for the version of me that was twenty-three, sitting in a university library, trying to figure out whether the Christianity I had been raised against had anything serious to say. The book that finally moved the question for me was N.T. Wright's *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003). It is a difficult book — eight hundred pages of careful historical argument — but it is the book that taught me that the question of the historical Jesus could be addressed honestly, without the shortcuts the pop apologetics had relied on, and that the honest reading of the evidence still pointed, in my final judgment, toward the resurrection as the best historical explanation of the data we have. I am going to summarize what I learned from that book, and from Mike Licona's *The Resurrection of Jesus* (2010), and from Richard Bauckham's *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* (2006), and from a fair amount of independent reading of the contemporary scholarly conversation. I will try to do it honestly. I will try to grant the strongest secular counter-positions where they are strong. I will name the limits of the case. The case is not a proof. The case is, in my reading, the best available historical explanation of the data that we have.

## What is granted by everyone

I want to start with what is granted by virtually everyone who has done serious historical work on the question, including the strongly skeptical secular historians.

Jesus of Nazareth existed. He was a first-century Jewish itinerant teacher with a following in Galilee and Judea. He was crucified under Pontius Pilate, sometime around AD 30, plus or minus a few years. His followers very rapidly came to believe that he had been raised from the dead, and on the basis of that belief, they began proclaiming him as Lord and Christ. The movement grew rapidly throughout the first century, despite the social and political costs of joining it, and within three centuries it had reshaped the religious world of the Mediterranean world.

These facts are not controversial. Even the most skeptical mainstream scholars — Bart Ehrman is the prominent contemporary example — grant them. They are attested by multiple early Christian sources (the Pauline letters, the four canonical gospels, Acts), and by non-Christian sources of varying reliability (Tacitus, Suetonius, Josephus in the Antiquities). The historical existence of Jesus, his execution under Pilate, and the rapid emergence of a movement worshipping him as risen Lord are part of the established baseline of first-century historical scholarship.

What is contested is the explanation. The Christian explanation is that Jesus was, in fact, raised from the dead, and that the rapid emergence of the church is the natural consequence of that historical event. The secular explanations vary, but they generally try to account for the data without granting a literal resurrection. The contested question is which explanation best fits the data we have.

## What the data actually is

Let me lay out the data, carefully.

The crucifixion is attested in multiple sources of varying types. The four canonical gospels. The Pauline letters, especially 1 Corinthians 15, which contains a creedal formula that most scholars date to within five years of the events. Acts. The non-Christian sources of Tacitus, Suetonius, and Josephus. The crucifixion is one of the most strongly attested events in ancient history.

The empty tomb is attested by the gospels, with significant variation in detail but consistency in the core claim. The claim is also implied by the early Pauline tradition; 1 Corinthians 15 lists the resurrection appearances in a way that presupposes the tomb was empty. The empty tomb is, notably, attested by sources that find the empty tomb embarrassing in certain respects — the first witnesses are women, whose testimony was not considered legally reliable in first-century Jewish or Roman contexts. The criterion of embarrassment, used by historians to identify probable historical kernels in religious texts, suggests that the empty tomb tradition is not a later invention; it has embarrassing features that an inventor would have avoided.

The resurrection appearances are attested by multiple independent sources. 1 Corinthians 15 — the Pauline creedal formula — names appearances to Peter, to the Twelve, to more than five hundred at once, to James the brother of Jesus, and to Paul himself. The gospels add appearances in Jerusalem and in Galilee, to various groups, including the women at the tomb and the disciples on the road to Emmaus. The appearances span weeks. They occur in different locations and to different witnesses, including witnesses who were hostile to the early movement (Paul, whose conversion from persecutor to apostle is well-attested).

The rapid emergence of a Christian movement worshipping Jesus as God in a monotheistic Jewish context is itself part of the data. Larry Hurtado's *Lord Jesus Christ* (2003) is the definitive treatment. The early Christian devotion to Jesus is not the kind of devotion you would expect to emerge slowly from a mythologizing process. It emerges within weeks of the events. The first Christians, who were almost all Jews, were worshipping a man as God — something Jewish monotheism would have resisted enormously — within a short enough time frame that the witnesses to the original events were still alive and could have corrected the worship had it been mistaken. The early high Christology is one of the most historically distinctive features of the early Christian movement, and it is difficult to account for without the resurrection.

The willingness of the first witnesses to die rather than recant is part of the data. The apostles are reported, in independent ancient sources, to have died for their faith, with varying historical reliability across the individual cases. The strongest case is for Peter and Paul, both of whom were almost certainly martyred in the 60s under Nero. The general pattern is well-established. The first witnesses, who would have known whether the resurrection appearances were genuine or invented, went to their deaths rather than denying what they had seen. This does not prove the resurrection — people die for false beliefs all the time. But people very rarely die for what they themselves know to be a lie. The first witnesses' willingness to suffer and die is consistent with their genuine belief that they had seen what they claimed to have seen.

## What the alternative explanations have to do

The secular alternative has to account for all of this data in a way that does not require a literal resurrection. Several alternatives have been proposed across the modern scholarly conversation.

The hallucination theory holds that the first disciples experienced grief-induced hallucinations of Jesus and mistakenly believed they had seen him risen. This account has some explanatory power but runs into significant difficulties. Hallucinations are individual experiences, and the early data describes group experiences — appearances to the Twelve together, to more than five hundred at once. Hallucinations do not, on standard psychological accounts, occur in groups in the way the data describes. The hallucination theory also has difficulty with Paul's conversion, which involved a hostile witness who had no grief-related expectation of seeing the risen Jesus. The theory has some life in it but is not generally considered to fully account for the data.

The legend-development theory holds that the resurrection accounts developed over decades through a process of mythologizing, and that the historical event was something more mundane — perhaps just the belief that Jesus's spirit lived on. This account has been increasingly difficult to maintain as the early dating of the resurrection traditions has been established. The 1 Corinthians 15 creedal formula is dated to within five years of the events. The Pauline letters generally are within twenty to thirty years. The earliest gospel is plausibly within forty years. The time frame is too short for substantial mythological development of the kind the theory requires, and the eyewitnesses were still alive when the early accounts were being formed.

The stolen-body theory and various conspiracy theories have been around since the first century, and were already being addressed by the Gospel of Matthew. They have difficulty accounting for the appearances, the conversion of hostile witnesses, the willingness of the first generation to die for what they would have known to be a lie, and the rapid emergence of high Christology.

The strongest contemporary secular alternative, in my reading, is some version of *we don't know what happened, but it probably wasn't a literal resurrection.* This is the position of Bart Ehrman and others. The position is intellectually honest. The position grants the data and declines to commit to a specific alternative explanation. The position is roughly: *something happened, the disciples believed they had seen Jesus, the movement grew from there, and the question of what actually occurred is beyond historical reconstruction.* The strength of this position is its modesty. The weakness of this position is that it leaves the historical data essentially unexplained.

## What the Christian explanation does

The Christian explanation is that Jesus of Nazareth was raised from the dead, on the third day after his crucifixion, in a transformed but embodied state, and was seen by various witnesses over a period of approximately forty days before ascending. This explanation accounts for the empty tomb, the appearances, the conversion of hostile witnesses, the early high Christology, the willingness of the first witnesses to die, and the rapid emergence of the movement. The explanation has explanatory power that the alternatives do not, in my reading, match.

The explanation is also, of course, extraordinary. Resurrections are not part of the normal furniture of the world. The skeptic is right to require strong evidence for an extraordinary claim. The question is whether the cumulative weight of the historical data is sufficient to make the extraordinary claim the best available explanation. My judgment, after twenty years of working on the question, is that it is. The honest skeptic who has done the historical work and concludes otherwise is not, in my view, making an obviously stupid judgment. The data does not compel a single answer. The data does, in my reading, point strongly in the Christian direction.

I want to be careful about what I am claiming. I am not claiming that the historical case for the resurrection is airtight. I am claiming that it is the best available explanation of the historical data we have, and that the alternative explanations each leave more unexplained than the Christian explanation does. This is a comparative judgment under conditions of incomplete evidence. The judgment can be made honestly in either direction. I am simply telling you which way mine goes, and why.

## The verdict

The resurrection of Jesus is either the worst-attested wild claim in ancient history or the best-attested one. The honest historical work has not been able to dismiss it. The honest historical work has also not been able to prove it in a way that compels every reasonable person. The decision the resurrection asks for is not a decision the historical evidence can finally close. It is a decision about what kind of universe you think you live in. That decision is yours, and it has consequences in both directions.

If the resurrection happened, then the man Jesus of Nazareth was who he claimed to be — the Messiah, the Son of God, the Lord. His teaching has authority. His death has redemptive significance. His resurrection has cosmic significance. The implications run in every direction, and they have, in every century since, transformed the lives of those who accept them.

If the resurrection did not happen, then the early Christian movement is one of the most puzzling phenomena in ancient history — a movement that emerged from nothing, on the basis of a belief that has no real foundation, and yet outlasted every empire of its time and shaped the moral and intellectual world of the West more deeply than any other movement in human history. The puzzlement does not disprove the secular position. The puzzlement is part of what the secular position has to live with.

I came to faith from atheism partly because the historical case for the resurrection, when I finally did the work, was stronger than I had been led to expect by the pop atheist literature I had been reading. The case was not airtight. The case was strong enough to make the resurrection the best available explanation, in my judgment, of the data. The judgment had consequences. The consequences are what I am still living out, twenty-two years later.
