The pastor chose this life. He felt the call, pursued the training, said yes to the church. His spouse said yes to *him* — and somewhere in that yes, often without fully reckoning the cost, said yes to a life she did not choose the way he did. That asymmetry sits underneath a great deal of quiet ministry-marriage pain, and most pastors are too close to it to see it.

So let me try to say what a pastor's spouse often wishes he knew, and rarely finds the words to tell him without it sounding like an accusation.

She lives in a glass house she did not build. The congregation watches the pastor's family — the marriage, the children, the home — and forms opinions, and the spouse feels the watching constantly. She cannot have an ordinary bad day in public. She cannot let the kids be ordinary kids without wondering who is drawing conclusions. She carries a scrutiny the pastor at least signed up for, and she did not, and it is wearying in a way he can forget because for him it comes with a calling that gives it meaning.

She shares you with everyone and is told it is holy. The needs of the congregation are real and they are endless, and they have a way of always outranking her. The crisis call comes during dinner and he takes it, and it is the right thing, and it is also the hundredth time, and she is left holding the meal and the children and the unspoken sense that she is competing with God's work for her own husband — a competition she is not allowed to win and not allowed to name.

She often has no pastor. Everyone else in the building has someone to go to with their burdens: the pastor. She is married to him, which means the one person everyone else confides in is the one person she cannot fully confide in about the thing that is hardest — the ministry itself, his exhaustion, the church politics, the wound a board member left. Her natural confidants are the very people she must be careful around. The result is a particular loneliness: surrounded by a congregation, belonging fully to none of it.

And she frequently feels like staff she didn't apply to be. The unspoken expectations arrive anyway — that she will play, host, lead, attend, model. Her gifts may lie elsewhere entirely. But the role came attached to the marriage, and declining it feels like failing him in front of his people.

I write this as a confession, not a lecture, because I have made my spouse carry versions of all of it and called the carrying *partnership* when it was really just cost I was offloading. The hardest thing to see, from inside the calling, is that my yes obligated her to a yes she never fully got to give — and that the least I owe her is to stop pretending the weight is symmetrical when it is not.

So what does she need? Less than you fear and more than you are giving. She needs you to *see* the asymmetry — to stop assuming she experiences this life the way you do. She needs a pastor of her own, someone outside the congregation she can be honest with, and your active help in finding one. She needs the freedom to not be the unpaid associate minister — to define her own role in the church, or to step back from a role that was assumed onto her. She needs some part of you that the congregation cannot have, reliably, not as leftovers. And she needs to hear, out loud and often, that her sacrifice is seen — because the congregation will never thank her, and the applause that sustains you does not reach her at all.

You were called. She came with you, into a house she did not build, to share you with a thousand people who will never know what it cost her. The least the called one can do is to stop being one more person who fails to see her.

Ask her what she wishes you knew. Then be quiet long enough to actually hear it.
