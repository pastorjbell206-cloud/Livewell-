Every CEO has a board. Every doctor has a licensing body. Every pilot answers to a tower. The pastor — who handles people's souls, money, marriages, and secrets — often answers to no one who could actually stop him. We have built a profession of unusual spiritual power and unusual practical isolation, and then we are surprised, every few months, by another name in the news.

This is not mainly about scandal, though it ends there often enough. It is about the ordinary erosion that happens to any man with no one above him. Authority without accountability does not stay neutral. It curdles. Not because pastors are worse than other people, but because they are not better — and we have arranged the job as though they were.

So build the thing the job did not give you. Not a committee. A board — a small, deliberate set of people whose relationship to you is not managed by you.

You need a handful, and they do different work. You need someone who knew you before the title, who would still take your call if the church closed tomorrow, and who is therefore immune to your platform. You need someone older in the faith who is allowed to ask the questions no one on your staff can ask — about your marriage, your money, your private appetites — and who will not flinch at the real answer. You need a peer, another pastor outside your own system, who understands the work from the inside and owes you nothing. And you need at least one person who is not impressed by you at all, because a man surrounded only by people who admire him will slowly lose the ability to hear the truth.

The defining feature of a real board is this: they can say no to you, and it costs you something to ignore them. Accountability that you can overrule at will is not accountability. It is theater you perform for your own conscience. The men who fall are almost never the men with no one watching. They are the men who arranged their watchers carefully — chose the ones who would not look too closely, kept the relationships shallow enough to stay comfortable, and called it community.

I have had to ask myself the harder version of this. Not *do I have accountability* — every pastor will say yes — but *is there a single person who could tell me to stop, and would I actually stop?* For long stretches the honest answer was no. I had admirers I called advisors. I had a board on paper and no one in my life with the standing to wound me for my own good. That is not safety. That is the appearance of safety, which is more dangerous than none, because it lets a man relax exactly where he should be vigilant.

Scripture is not romantic about this. *Where there is no guidance, a people falls, but in an abundance of counselors there is safety* (Proverbs 11:14). The wisdom literature assumes that a man left to himself drifts, and that the remedy is not more willpower but more voices — real ones, with permission to contradict him.

So choose them on purpose. Tell them plainly what you are asking: not encouragement, which you have plenty of, but the freedom to ask anything and the expectation of a true answer. Give them access to the parts of your life that scandal usually grows in — the calendar, the finances, the private internet, the marriage. Meet often enough that the relationship is real before the crisis, because a board assembled after the fall is just a jury.

The man at the top of an organization with no one able to stop him is not strong. He is exposed, and the exposure is invisible to him right up until it is the only thing anyone can see.

Build the board now. Before you need it. While you can still choose the people honest enough to hurt you.
