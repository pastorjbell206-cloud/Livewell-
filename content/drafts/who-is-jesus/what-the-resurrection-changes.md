There is a question about the resurrection that gets all the attention. *Did it happen?* It deserves the attention; it is the right place to put your effort, and others have made the historical case more carefully than I could in an essay. But there is a second question that almost no one asks out loud, and it is the one I want to sit with here. Not *did it happen,* but: *if it did, what changes?*

That second question turns out to be the more revealing one. Because a surprising number of people, including many who call themselves Christians, treat the resurrection as a happy ending tacked onto the real story — Jesus died for our sins, and then, as a kind of bonus, he came back to show it all worked out. A nice epilogue. The first Christians did not treat it that way at all. For them the resurrection was not the epilogue. It was the hinge on which the whole future of the world had just turned.

**If Jesus rose, then a piece of the world's promised future has already happened, in advance, in the middle of ordinary history — and that single fact rearranges everything else: what death is, what your body is for, and what an ordinary day is actually part of.** Let me try to show you the size of it, taking the resurrection as true for the length of the essay, and asking only what follows.

## First: it means the death made the difference it claimed to

Paul puts the stakes more bluntly than any preacher I have ever heard would dare. *"And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile; you are still in your sins"* (1 Corinthians 15:17). Read that twice. He does not say *you would be missing a nice bonus.* He says *you are still in your sins* — the whole thing is undone, the cross was just a sad death, and the believers are, in his words a verse later, of all people most to be pitied.

Why so absolute? Because the resurrection is God's verdict on the cross. Anyone can die claiming their death means something. The claim is only validated if it is vindicated, and the vindication is that God raised him. The empty tomb is the receipt that says the debt described in the last essay was actually paid — that the rescue went through, that the one who went under death came back out the far side, that it worked. Without it, the cross is an assertion. With it, the cross is an accomplished fact. *"He has given us new birth into a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead"* (1 Peter 1:3). Living, Peter says — a hope with a pulse, because the one it rests on has one.

So the first thing that changes is everything the previous essay was about. The resurrection does not add to the cross. It confirms it.

This is also why the first Christians spoke of the resurrection as something that happened *to* Jesus, done by the Father, and not as something Jesus achieved by his own surviving strength. The grammar of the New Testament is careful here: *God raised him.* The point is not that a remarkable man proved too vigorous for death. The point is that the Father rendered a verdict — overturned the world's judgment, reversed the sentence the cross had seemed to pass, declared in the most public way available that this death was not a defeat but the decisive act of God. Easter is, in that sense, a courtroom scene as much as a graveyard one. The world tried Jesus, found him guilty, and executed him. Three days later the higher court reversed the ruling. Everything the believers went on to say about him rests on that reversal.

## Second: it means death is now a defeated thing

Here is where you need to understand what the first Christians actually claimed, because we tend to shrink it. They did not claim Jesus' soul went to heaven, or that his memory lived on, or that his spirit was somehow still with them in their hearts. They claimed his *body* was raised — transformed, no longer subject to death, but unmistakably physical. He ate. He was touched. The tomb was empty because the body that had been in it was now up and walking.

N. T. Wright, in *Surprised by Hope*, makes the point that we have badly misread this. The Christian hope was never about escaping the body and the physical world to float away as souls. It was about the physical world itself being raised and renewed — and Jesus' resurrection was the first instance of it, the down payment, what Paul calls the *firstfruits.* *"But Christ has indeed been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep"* (1 Corinthians 15:20). Firstfruits is a farming word: the first sheaf of a harvest, brought in early, that guarantees the rest of the field is coming. Jesus is not the exception. He is the first one through a door that now stands open.

Which means death is no longer the wall it appears to be. For the person who reads this in the ordinary terror most of us carry quietly about our own ending and the endings of the people we love, the claim is staggering in its specificity: death is real, death is an enemy, death took even him — and death did not keep him, and will not keep those who are joined to him. Richard Bauckham, one of the most careful New Testament scholars alive, has pressed how thoroughly this reframes the human situation. We are not, on this account, spirits passing through a doomed material world on the way out. We are bodies awaiting the same raising that has already happened to one of us. Death is not the door out of life. It is a defeated checkpoint on the way to more of it.

It is worth being precise about what kind of body is promised, because the resurrection accounts are careful in a way that resists both of the easy misreadings. The risen Jesus is not a ghost — he eats, he is touched, the tomb is genuinely empty. But he is also not simply resuscitated, a man revived to pick up his old life and die again later, the way Lazarus would. He passes through the ordinary limits of bodies; his closest friends sometimes do not recognize him at first. Paul reaches for the image of a seed and the plant that comes from it: there is real continuity — the same person, the same body, raised — and real transformation — no longer perishable, no longer subject to decay. The future the resurrection promises is therefore not escape from your body and not a return to your body exactly as it is, with its failing joints and its appointment with the grave. It is *this* body, the real you, raised imperishable. Whatever you have lost to age or illness or accident is not the final word on the form you will wear. That is a more concrete hope than the vague, disembodied afterlife most people imagine when they imagine anything at all, and it is the hope the first Christians actually held.

## Third: it means your body and this world are not disposable

This follows directly, and it overturns something a lot of people assume Christianity teaches. If the future is *resurrection* — bodies raised, creation renewed — and not *escape* — souls leaving matter behind — then the material world is not a temporary stage to be abandoned. It is the very thing God intends to save.

That changes how you hold your own body, and the bodies of others. It means physical suffering is not beneath God's concern, a mere distraction from spiritual things; it is precisely what the resurrection targets. It means caring for a sick body, feeding a hungry one, easing a dying one, is not a lesser, worldly work while the real work is "spiritual." It is work aimed straight at the future God has promised. The hungry fed, the sick tended, the grieving sat with — these are not waiting-room activities. They are early gestures of the world that is coming, the same kind of thing the miracles were, now handed to us to do.

It also dignifies the ordinary in a way little else can. Paul ends his great resurrection chapter not with a meditation on the afterlife but with a sentence about daily work: *"Always give yourselves fully to the work of the Lord, because you know that your labor in the Lord is not in vain"* (the closing of 1 Corinthians 15). *Not in vain* — because in a universe headed for resurrection, nothing good is finally lost. The kindness no one saw, the faithfulness that came to nothing in your lifetime, the work that seemed to wash away — if the dead are raised, none of it evaporates. It is gathered up into the world that does not end.

Set that against the only honest alternative, which deserves to be stated at its strongest. If there is no resurrection — if the universe is winding down toward a cold and final silence, and every life and every loving act is eventually erased without remainder — then in the very long run nothing you do persists. The kindness, the courage, the building and mending and loving, all of it is sandcastle work ahead of a tide that always comes in. You can still choose to do good, and many honest people do, but you do it knowing the cosmos will not remember. The resurrection makes the opposite claim, and the contrast could not be sharper. It says the tide does not finally win. It says the good you do is not written in sand but in something that lasts, because the God who raises the dead is keeping the account and intends to raise the world along with its Lord. That is either a comforting fantasy or the most important fact there is, but it is not a small difference. It changes whether your life is a candle in a room that will inevitably go dark, or the first light of a morning that does not end.

## Fourth: it means a new kind of life is available now, not only later

The resurrection is not only a promise about your future. The first Christians insisted it had already begun to change the present, and they reached for a startling image to say so: baptism as a death and a rising. *"If we have been united with him in a death like his, we will certainly also be united with him in a resurrection like his"* — and so, Paul says, *"count yourselves dead to sin but alive to God in Christ Jesus"* (Romans 6:5–11).

The claim underneath that is concrete. The same power that raised Jesus is the power now at work in a person joined to him — which means the patterns you are sure are simply who you are, the resentments and compulsions and fears you have given up trying to change, are not the final word. *"Since, then, you have been raised with Christ, set your hearts on things above"* (Colossians 3:1). Notice the tense. Not *you will be raised someday* but *you have been raised* — the future has reached backward and taken hold of your present, and you are asked to live now as a person who belongs to a world that has not fully arrived. Resurrection is not only what you wait for. It is the source of a life you can begin to live before you die.

This is also what changes how a Christian can face suffering, and it is worth saying plainly because the alternative on offer is so thin. The resurrection does not teach you to deny that things are bad, or to paper over a loss with the assurance that everything happens for a reason. It does something harder and more honest. It lets you look straight at the worst the world can do — the diagnosis, the grave, the marriage that ended, the years that came to nothing — and refuse to grant it the last word, not because the pain is unreal but because the pain is not final. Grief and hope can occupy the same room. Jesus wept at a tomb he was about to open, and the order matters: the weeping was real, and then the tomb opened. A faith built on the resurrection does not skip the weeping. It simply knows what comes after, and so it grieves, as Paul says, but not as those who have no hope. That is not optimism. Optimism expects things to go well. Hope expects God to raise the dead, which is a far stranger and far stronger thing to lean your weight on.

## What it does to a Tuesday

Put the four together and stand back.

If Jesus rose, then the death he died accomplished what it claimed. Then your own death is a defeated enemy and not a wall. Then your body and this whole physical world are not trash to be discarded but the very thing slated for renewal. Then a power not your own is available to change you now, and the most ordinary faithful thing you do today is not lost but gathered into a future that does not end.

That is what hangs on the second question, and it is why the first Christians could not treat the resurrection as an epilogue. It was not the happy ending of Jesus' story. It was the early arrival of the world's ending — the future breaking into the middle of history, in one garden, on one Sunday, so that everyone after could know how the story comes out and start, even now, to live like it.

So set the historical question where it belongs, which is in front of you to examine honestly. But do not let anyone tell you the stakes are small. If it did not happen, then we are, as Paul said, of all people most to be pitied, and you should walk on. But if it did — if a tomb really came up empty and a real body walked out of it into a transformed life that death can no longer touch — then it is not one religious claim among many. It is the single most important fact about the universe you are standing in, and about the grave you are walking toward, and about the ordinary, unremarkable, infinitely consequential Tuesday you are living through right now.
