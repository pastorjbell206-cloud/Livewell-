Ask ten people on a sidewalk who Jesus was and you will get a remarkably consistent answer. A good man. A wise teacher. Someone who told people to love each other and got killed by the authorities for being too kind. The answer is so common that it has stopped sounding like a claim and started sounding like a fact, the way the boiling point of water is a fact. Everyone agrees, so it must be settled.

It is not settled, and the reason is simple. The man that answer describes is not the man in the documents. The Jesus of the sidewalk consensus is a figure we have assembled out of greeting cards and half-remembered Christmas pageants, and he has almost nothing to do with the person you meet when you actually read what the earliest sources say he said.

**What the consensus quietly removes is the only thing that explains his death: Jesus did not present himself as a teacher with good material. He presented himself as the one the entire story of Israel had been waiting for — and, more than that, as someone who could only be accounted for by talking about God.** That is not a devotional flourish I am adding from the outside. It is the historical problem at the center of the New Testament, and it is the reason he was killed. People do not execute a moral teacher for recommending love. They execute a man they believe has said something unforgivable.

To see it, you have to put him back where he stood.

## The world he was standing in

First-century Jewish faith had one immovable center. There is one God. He made everything. He is not a force or a feeling but a person, holy and separate, who had bound himself to a particular people and given them his name. That name — written in Hebrew with four letters, spoken aloud almost never — was so weighty that devout Jews would not pronounce it. They said other words in its place rather than risk it crossing their lips.

This matters more than almost anything else if you want to hear Jesus correctly. He was not speaking into a vague spiritual culture where everyone believed a little bit of everything and "God" meant whatever you needed it to mean. He was speaking into the most rigorously monotheistic culture the ancient world produced. To a first-century Jew, the worst thing a human being could do was blur the line between the creature and the Creator. That was the original sin, the serpent's offer, the temptation underneath every other temptation: *you will be like God.* The whole moral architecture of the faith was built to keep that line bright.

So when a man stood inside that architecture and started speaking and acting as though the line ran through him, it was not heard as inspiring. It was heard as the most dangerous thing a person could say.

Keep that in mind, because it dissolves a comfortable modern assumption. We tend to picture the ancient world as credulous — as a place where claims of divinity were cheap, where emperors called themselves gods and everyone went along, where one more holy man saying lofty things would barely raise an eyebrow. That was true enough out in the wider pagan world. It was emphatically not true among the Jews, and the Jews are the people Jesus was speaking to. They had spent centuries being killed rather than burn a pinch of incense to a Roman emperor's supposed divinity. They were the one people in the empire constitutionally unable to shrug at a man claiming the place of God. Which means the reaction Jesus provoked is exactly the reaction you would expect if his claims were as large as the Gospels say, and exactly the wrong reaction if he were merely a teacher of ethics. The hostility is a kind of evidence. It tells you they heard something we have trained ourselves not to hear.

## "Before Abraham was born, I am"

Watch one sentence do its work. In John's Gospel, Jesus is arguing with a hostile crowd about Abraham, the founding father of the nation, dead for nearly two thousand years. They are mocking him: you are not even fifty, and you talk as if you knew Abraham. His answer breaks the grammar on purpose. *"Very truly I tell you, before Abraham was born, I am"* (John 8:58).

Not "I was." *I am.* The tense is wrong, and it is wrong deliberately, because those two words were the words God spoke from the burning bush when Moses asked his name — *I am who I am.* Jesus has reached into the holiest sentence in the Hebrew Scriptures and used it of himself. The crowd does not stand there puzzled by his syntax. The next verse tells you exactly how they heard it: they picked up stones to throw at him. They understood him perfectly. In their world there was one penalty for a man claiming the name of God, and they reached for it on the spot.

You can decide he was wrong to say it. You cannot decide he did not say something enormous. The sidewalk Jesus never says a line that gets him stoned. This one does.

And it is not an isolated outburst. The same Gospel records him saying, more plainly still, *"I and the Father are one"* (John 10:30) — and again the response is immediate and identical. The crowd gathers stones. When Jesus asks which of his good works they are stoning him for, they answer with perfect clarity: not for any work, *"but for blasphemy, because you, a mere man, claim to be God."* That sentence is worth memorizing, because it is his opponents — the hostile witnesses, the people with every reason to minimize what he said — telling us exactly how they understood him. They did not hear a teacher exaggerating. They heard a man claiming to be God, and they were not confused about it. When even your enemies agree on what you are claiming, the claim is not in doubt. Only its truth is.

This is the difference between Jesus and the gentle figure of cultural memory that almost no one wants to lose. The figure of cultural memory says wise and tender things and dies admired. The man in the documents says things that empty a room of its mercy and fill its hands with rocks. You can call him mistaken. You cannot call him mild.

## The title he actually used

Strangely, the title Jesus seems to have preferred for himself is the one we now find least impressive. He called himself "the Son of Man." It sounds humble, almost self-deprecating — son of man, just a human being. For a long time that is how it was read, and it is exactly backward.

The phrase comes from the seventh chapter of the book of Daniel, a vision given to a people under the boot of empire. Daniel sees the thrones of the world set up, and then "one like a son of man" comes on the clouds of heaven — clouds being, throughout the Hebrew Scriptures, the signature of God's own arrival — and approaches the Ancient of Days, and is given everlasting dominion and glory, and all nations and peoples worship him. This is not a modest figure. This is the one to whom worship is given, sharing the throne of God.

The scholar who has done the most to recover this is N. T. Wright, whose *Jesus and the Victory of God* (1996) argues at length that "Son of Man" was not Jesus hiding his significance but encoding it — claiming, in language his own tradition supplied, to be Daniel's heavenly figure, the one who would receive the kingdom. When he used it, the educated among his hearers did not relax. They leaned in. He was telling them where in the story he believed he stood, and it was the highest place in the vision.

## The night it cost him his life

All of this comes to its point on the night he is arrested. The most senior religious court in the nation has him in custody and cannot make a charge stick. Witnesses contradict each other. Then the high priest asks the question directly: *"Are you the Messiah, the Son of the Blessed One?"*

Mark records the answer without softening it. *"I am, and you will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of the Mighty One and coming on the clouds of heaven"* (Mark 14:61–62). He has fused two of the heaviest passages in his Scriptures — the enthroned figure of Psalm 110 at God's right hand, and Daniel's son of man coming on the clouds — and applied them to himself, standing in chains in front of the men who could kill him. The high priest tears his clothes, the ancient gesture for hearing blasphemy, and the court has its verdict in a single sentence.

This is the load-bearing fact that the "good teacher" reading cannot carry. Jesus was not executed for a misunderstanding, or merely for being a political nuisance, though Rome handled the execution and had political reasons of its own. At the level of the religious charge, he was condemned for the specific thing he said about himself. The cross is the receipt for the claim. A moral teacher does not generate this. Only a particular kind of claim generates a courtroom tearing its robes.

## Not the same as flattering yourself

Now, a fair objection, and it is the one I once made myself. People say grand things about themselves all the time. Every century produces men convinced they are chosen, that destiny runs through them, that God is on their side in a special way. Why is Jesus different from any other charismatic with an inflated sense of his own importance?

Two things separate him, and both come from careful historical work rather than piety.

The first is *how early the worship started.* Larry Hurtado spent his career on this question, and his books — most thoroughly *Lord Jesus Christ* (2003) — document something that ought to be more famous than it is. Within a handful of years of the crucifixion, before any of the New Testament was written, Jewish followers of Jesus were doing with him the one thing their entire tradition forbade doing with anyone but God. They were praying to him. Singing to him. Baptizing in his name. Including him in the place where, for a Jew, only the one God could stand. Hurtado calls this a genuine mutation in monotheism, and the historically arresting part is the speed. This was not a legend that grew over generations as the real man faded from memory. It was the immediate response of people who had known him, many of whom died rather than take it back.

The second is the *texture of how he held it.* Men who claim divine status tend to grasp at it — they need the crowd, the throne, the confirmation. Paul, writing what may be an even earlier hymn, describes Jesus as the one who, being in the form of God, did not grasp at equality with God but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant. Whether or not you can yet believe that, notice that it is not the psychology of a megalomaniac. The claim and the lowliness sit together in a way that does not fit the ordinary profile of a man overrating himself.

There is a further detail in *how* he claimed it that historians find hard to explain away. Jesus did not mostly say "I am God" in the bald form a later inventor would have put in his mouth. He claimed divine prerogatives sideways, by doing the things only God was permitted to do, and letting people draw the conclusion. He forgave sins that were not committed against him — which, as his critics instantly noted, only God can do. He spoke as though the Sabbath, God's own day, were his to define. He told people their eternal standing turned on their relationship to him personally. He received worship without the horror that any faithful Jew, or any angel in the Scriptures, shows when worship is misdirected. Taken one at a time, each could be argued about. Taken together, they form a pattern that the earliest sources did not invent and could not easily have invented, because it runs against everything their own monotheism trained them to expect. The simplest account of why the sources are so strange is that the man himself was that strange.

What this means for the "good teacher" theory is quietly fatal. A good teacher, in that world, would have spent his energy keeping the line between God and man bright, because protecting that line was the heart of the goodness. Instead Jesus spent his energy walking across it. Whatever he was, he was not a teacher reinforcing the faith of his people. He was doing something to it that no merely good man in that setting would ever have dared.

## The question he asked first

Halfway through his work, Jesus turns the whole matter around and puts it to his closest followers as a question. *"Who do people say the Son of Man is?"* They report the rumors — a prophet, a returned hero, one of the old voices come back. Then he narrows it to the only version that finally matters. *"But what about you? Who do you say I am?"* Peter answers: *"You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God"* (Matthew 16:13–16).

I think that is exactly the right structure, and not only for the disciples. There is the Jesus people say. The sidewalk Jesus, the cultural rumor, the good man assembled from things we half-remember. And then there is the question that does not let you stay in the rumor, because the documents will not let you. The man in them claimed to be the hinge of the whole story, the one in whom the one God of Israel was somehow present and acting. Either he was right, or he was wrong, but he did not leave open the comfortable middle where he is merely admirable.

That is the door this whole series walks through, and I will not pretend the door is small. He made the line between the creature and the Creator run straight through one particular man. Everything else about him — the miracles, the death, the empty tomb — is only worth examining because of that claim, and the claim is the thing the consensus has quietly thrown away.

You inherited a Jesus who is easy to admire and easy to leave. The one in the documents is neither. He stood in the strictest monotheism the world has known and spoke as though the name from the burning bush belonged to him, and the people closest to him went and worshiped him before the ink of his story was dry. You can still walk away from that. But you should at least know it is what you are walking away from.
