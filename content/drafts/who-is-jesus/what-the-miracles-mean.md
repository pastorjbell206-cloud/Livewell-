When most of us picture the miracles, we picture proof. Jesus needs to convince a doubting crowd that he speaks for God, so he does something no ordinary man can do — walks on the sea, multiplies the bread, calls a dead man out of a tomb — and the impossible act functions like a signature, a credential, a flash of divine ID that ends the argument. Believe me, the miracle says, because look what I can do.

If that is what they were, they did a poor job. Plenty of people watched Jesus heal and walked away unmoved. Some watched him raise the dead and went straight to the authorities to have him killed. A miracle, it turns out, convinces almost no one who has already decided. As proof, the whole enterprise is strangely ineffective, and the Gospel writers seem entirely untroubled by this. They never present the miracles primarily as evidence. They are after something else.

**The miracles were not magic tricks performed to prove a point. They were previews — small, local outbreaks of the world as it is supposed to be, breaking into the world as it actually is.** The right word for them is the one John uses: not "wonders" but "signs." A sign is not the destination. It is a pointer to the destination, a sample of where the whole road is going. Read them that way and they stop being the embarrassing part of the story and become the part that tells you what the story is for.

## What a sign is pointing at

To see it, you need the one idea Jesus talked about more than any other, the idea modern readers most often skim past: the kingdom of God. He did not mostly preach about going to heaven when you die. He announced that the reign of God — the long-promised setting-right of everything that has gone wrong — was breaking into the present through him. *"The kingdom of God has come near,"* he said, and then he started acting like it.

N. T. Wright has spent much of his career on this point, and it reframes everything. The miracles, he argues, are not interruptions of the natural order designed to impress. They are what it looks like when the kingdom arrives in a particular place — a foretaste of the future God has promised, happening early, in one room, on one afternoon. When Jesus heals a body, he is not breaking the rules of the world. He is showing you the rules of the world to come, where bodies are not broken. When he stills a storm, he is showing you the peace that the renewed creation will live under. The miracle is a window. Through it you see, for one moment, the place this is all heading.

That is why the Gospels are so unbothered by the fact that miracles do not compel belief. They were never meant to function as compulsion. A preview does not force you into the theater. It shows you what is playing.

## The wine that should not have been there

Start where John starts, because he chose his first sign carefully. At a village wedding the wine runs out — a small, ordinary humiliation, the kind that ruins a family's reputation in a face-to-face society. Jesus has his servants fill six stone jars with water, and what is poured out is wine. Not adequate wine. The master of the feast, who does not know where it came from, complains that the host has saved the best for last (John 2:1–11).

Notice what kind of miracle this is. Nobody was dying. No crowd was demanding a sign. It happens almost privately, to spare a family embarrassment and keep a party going. If the miracles were credentials, this is a strange one to lead with — it proves nothing to nobody. But as a sign it is perfect. The prophets had long described the coming age of God as a wedding feast overflowing with the best wine, the day when the joy of the world would no longer run out. Jesus' first act is to make that promise visible in a single room. He is not showing off. He is saying: the feast the prophets promised — it starts now, with me, and it is more abundant than you dared hope.

John tells you exactly how to read it. He calls it the first of the signs through which Jesus *revealed his glory.* Not proved his power. Revealed his glory — showed who he is and what he came to do.

## The healing that came with a harder word

Sometimes the sign points past the body to the deeper wound underneath. A paralyzed man is lowered through a roof by friends desperate to get him to Jesus. Everyone in the room expects a healing. Instead Jesus says, *"Son, your sins are forgiven"* — and the room goes cold, because the scholars present know that only God can forgive sins, and here is a man doing it as casually as a doctor writing a prescription.

Then Jesus says the line that tells you what the whole event is for. *"Which is easier: to say to this paralyzed man, 'Your sins are forgiven,' or to say, 'Get up, take your mat and walk'?"* And he heals the body precisely so that they will know he has the authority to heal the deeper thing (Mark 2:1–12). The visible miracle is in service of the invisible one. The healed legs are a sign pointing at the forgiven sins. He is telling you that the kingdom does not only mend bodies; it goes after the thing that broke the world in the first place.

This is the pattern under all of them. The miracle is never the whole point. It is the readable surface of a deeper rescue.

It is worth lingering on who tends to receive these signs, because that too is part of the message. Jesus does not reserve his healings for the influential, the deserving, or the religiously impressive. He goes to a leper, a man no one was allowed to touch. To a despised tax collector's table. To a foreign woman, a blind beggar by the road, a centurion of the occupying army. The kingdom he is previewing does not arrive first among the people the world would expect. It arrives among the overlooked, the unclean, the written-off — which tells you something about the shape of the world to come. It is not a tidier version of the present hierarchy. It is a reversal of it. When the prophets dreamed of God's reign, they dreamed of exactly this: the last made first, the broken mended, the excluded brought in. The miracles are not only previews of a healed creation. They are previews of a healed *society*, and the preview keeps landing on the people the present has no use for. If you have ever suspected that any kingdom worth having would have to begin there, the Gospels agree with you.

## The grave he wept in front of

The hardest and greatest of the signs is the raising of Lazarus, and what makes it great is not the power. It is what Jesus does before he uses it. His friend has been dead four days. The sisters are wrecked. And the Son of God, who is about to undo the death with a word, stands at the tomb and weeps (John 11). The shortest verse in the Bible — *Jesus wept* — sits two sentences away from the greatest display of his authority over death.

Take that in, because it dismantles the magic-trick reading completely. A magician feels nothing; the trick is routine. Jesus, who knows exactly what he is about to do, still grieves, because death is genuinely his enemy and he hates it even in the moment he is about to defeat it. Then he calls the dead man by name and Lazarus walks out, still wrapped in his grave clothes — alive again, though he will die again someday. This is the largest preview of all. It points straight at the resurrection, his own and then ours, the day when death is not merely interrupted for one man but finished for good. The weeping tells you it costs him. The raising tells you he wins.

## Why this matters even if you cannot yet believe them

Here a fair reader stops me. This is all very moving as literature, but it assumes the events happened. If miracles do not occur — if the universe is a closed system of cause and effect with no door for anything to come through — then there is nothing to interpret, just stories.

That objection deserves a real answer, and the cleanest one I know came from a man who used to share the objection. C. S. Lewis, who spent his early adulthood as a confident atheist, wrote a whole book on this in 1947, simply titled *Miracles.* His central move is to notice that the case against miracles usually smuggles in its conclusion. People say miracles cannot happen and then conclude that the reports must be false — but "miracles cannot happen" is not something you learn by studying history or examining evidence. It is a belief about the universe you bring to the evidence before you look. If nature is all there is, then of course nothing can come in from outside, because there is no outside. But whether nature is all there is happens to be the very question Jesus is raising. You cannot settle it by assuming it.

It is also worth noticing how restrained the Gospel miracles are, because the restraint is itself a clue to their purpose. If the stories were invented to dazzle, they are oddly disciplined about it. Jesus refuses, more than once, to perform on demand. When his opponents ask for a sign to settle the argument, he will not give one. When the crowd, fed on the hillside, wants to make him king on the strength of the free bread, he withdraws from them. When he is mocked on the cross and told to come down and prove himself, he stays. A man manufacturing a reputation for power would have done the opposite at every turn — staged the spectacle, taken the crown, answered the dare. The pattern of the Gospels is a man with power who keeps declining to use it the way power is usually used. That fits the sign reading exactly. A sign points away from itself to what it signifies; a spectacle points back at the performer. Jesus keeps refusing to let the wonders become about the wonders.

Lewis's deeper point, though, is the one that meets this essay. The miracles of Jesus, he argued, are not random violations of nature. They are nature run at a speed and to a depth we are not used to. God turns water into wine every year through vines and rain and sun; at the wedding he simply did it without the vine. He multiplies bread every season through fields of grain; on the hillside he did it without the field. The miracles are not God breaking into a world that is not his. They are the world's true owner doing openly, in one place, what he is doing slowly everywhere — which is exactly what a sign of the kingdom should be.

## The answer Jesus gave when asked

There is a moment when even John the Baptist, in prison and about to be executed, sends to ask whether Jesus is really the one. It is the honest question, and Jesus does not answer with a claim. He answers by pointing at the signs and what they are doing. *"Go back and report to John what you have seen and heard: The blind receive sight, the lame walk, those who have leprosy are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the good news is proclaimed to the poor"* (Luke 7:18–23).

Every item on that list is lifted from the prophets' descriptions of the day God would come to heal the world. Jesus is not saying *believe me because I am impressive.* He is saying *look at what is happening — the very things the prophets said would mark the arrival of the kingdom are happening around me, and now you know what time it is.* The miracles are the evidence not of a powerful individual but of an arriving world.

Notice the strange last line of his answer, too: *and the good news is proclaimed to the poor.* He sets it alongside raising the dead as though it belongs in the same list, as though words spoken to the overlooked were a wonder on the order of a corpse standing up. To Jesus it was. The healed bodies and the good news for the poor are the same act in two forms — the kingdom reaching the people the present had given up on, in their bodies and in their hearing at once. The miracle and the message are not separable. Both announce that the world is changing hands.

So the part of the story we are quickest to dismiss turns out to be the part that tells you what the whole story is reaching toward. Not a man proving he is special. A world beginning to be healed, one room at a time, by the one who made it and means to have it back. The blind man's sight, the dead man walking out of his grave, the wine that should not have been there — these are not the surprising exceptions to an indifferent universe. According to the Gospels, they are the rule of the world that is coming, arriving early, where you can see it.

And if that is what they are, then the question they put to you is not *can I make myself believe six impossible things.* It is a quieter and harder one. If a world like that is real and on its way — a world with no more graves, no more running out, no more bodies that betray us — do you want it. The signs were never trying to win an argument. They were trying to make you homesick for a place you have not seen yet.
