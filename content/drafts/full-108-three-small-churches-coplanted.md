The conventional wisdom about church planting runs in a single direction: a sending church, large enough to absorb the cost, releases a team and a planter and carries the financial weight of the first several years. That model works. It is also unavailable to the vast majority of churches in America, which are small, under-resourced, and led by pastors who are stretched thin already.

What happens when three churches that cannot afford to plant alone decide to plant together?

That is not a theoretical question. The Pastors Connection Network has seen versions of this arrangement take shape in communities across the Midwest, and what those arrangements reveal about the nature of the church is at least as interesting as what they reveal about church planting strategy.

The first thing that happens when small churches attempt a collaborative plant is that every assumption about ownership has to be renegotiated. A single sending church knows who is in charge. The lead pastor of the sending church carries the authority, and the planter is accountable to him. Three churches sharing a plant means three pastors, three governing structures, three sets of expectations about what success looks like — and no obvious single authority. The negotiation of that shared governance is difficult. It is also, ironically, precisely the kind of difficulty that the church needs to practice.

Paul's description of the body in 1 Corinthians 12 is not a description of how organisms work under a single head in the organizational sense. It is a description of how differently gifted communities need each other — how the eye cannot say to the hand, "I have no need of you" (1 Corinthians 12:21). Three small churches bringing different strengths to a shared plant begin to practice that interdependence in ways that the single sending church never has to.

One church brings financial stability but no young families. One church brings young families but no financial margin. One church brings a neighborhood presence in exactly the place where the plant needs to take root but no capacity for anything beyond their own Sunday morning. Together they have something. Separately they have nothing but good intentions.

The plant that emerges from that collaboration carries a different genetic material than the plant that emerges from a single strong sender. It is accountable to multiple bodies. Its planter navigates three sets of pastoral relationships instead of one. The governance is more complicated. The decision-making is slower. And the congregation that forms — if the collaboration holds — has been planted into a posture of inter-church dependence that is very hard to teach later.

What makes these collaborations fail is almost always predictable: ego, theological disagreement that was papered over in the early enthusiasm, and the gradual calcification of the partnership into a transactional arrangement where the churches are contributors rather than co-laborers. The three pastors who shook hands on a shared vision in year one are, by year three, navigating the differences that the excitement originally obscured.

What makes them survive is harder to name precisely. The collaborations that hold seem to share one characteristic: the pastors involved have submitted to something larger than their own church's brand. They have decided, at some level, that the plant succeeds if the gospel advances — not if their particular congregation gets credit for it. That posture is not natural. It requires the kind of regular conversation, the shared accountability, the genuine friendship between pastors that is described elsewhere in this collection.

It also requires humility about failure. Some collaborative plants do not survive. The governance collapses, the planter leaves, one of the sending churches withdraws its support. That failure is painful and instructive. The instruction is not that collaboration was a mistake. The instruction is that collaboration is hard enough to require more than good intentions and enthusiasm to sustain it.

The three churches that plant together and survive have learned something about the body of Christ that cannot be taught in a church planting conference.

They have practiced it.
