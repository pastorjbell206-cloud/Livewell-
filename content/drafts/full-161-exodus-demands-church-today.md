The Exodus is not one story among many in Israel's memory. It is the story that all the other stories refer back to. When the prophets need to say something about who God is, they return to the Exodus. When the psalmists need to argue for God's faithfulness, they return to the Exodus. When Paul needs to describe the nature of salvation in Romans, he uses the Exodus pattern. When John needs to describe the end of history in Revelation, he saturates the vision with Exodus imagery. The story of God freeing slaves from Egypt is the paradigm that Israel and the early church used to understand everything else that God was doing.

This means the church cannot relegate the Exodus to the category of interesting ancient history. It is load-bearing.

The Exodus begins with God hearing. "God heard their groaning and he remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac and with Jacob. So God looked on the Israelites and was concerned about them" (Exodus 2:24-25). Before any act of liberation, there is attention. God notices. God hears. The cry of the oppressed reaches him, and he is not indifferent to it. This is a theological claim about God's character that the church affirms in its creed and largely ignores in its practice.

If God hears the cry of the oppressed, then the church that does not hear it is the church that has structured its life around not hearing it. The Israelites were heard because their suffering was real and their cry was genuine. The suffering of the poor in our cities is real. Their cry is present. The question is whether the church has constructed a sanctuary quiet enough that the cry does not reach it.

The liberation God enacts in the Exodus is comprehensive. It is not only spiritual. The Israelites are freed from physical bondage, from economic exploitation — they had been building treasure cities for Pharaoh without wages — from the crushing daily weight of a system designed to extract their labor and reproduce their subjugation. Liberation, in the Exodus, touches every dimension of human life. The reduction of the Exodus to a spiritual metaphor for individual salvation is not wrong — the New Testament develops that typology — but it is incomplete. The typology does not replace the original. The God who freed Israel from Egypt is the same God who is concerned about physical suffering, economic exploitation, and the systems that perpetuate them.

Walter Brueggemann [cite — confirm] argues that the Exodus creates what he calls an "alternative consciousness" — a community formed by a different story than the dominant story, capable of imagining a world organized on different principles than the empire's principles. The people of God are, in Brueggemann's reading, always in some sense a counter-cultural community, shaped by the memory of liberation rather than the logic of power. When the church forgets that memory, it loses its capacity to see what the empire cannot see.

The church has forgotten that memory in direct proportion to the degree it has become comfortable within the empire. The church that has political access, cultural prestige, and economic security does not easily identify with the slave. The slave's story is not its story. It has, in the language of the Exodus, moved into the house of Pharaoh.

I have moved into the house of Pharaoh in ways I have not named. I have benefited from structures that the Exodus pattern would name as oppressive. I have preached liberation theology from a position of comfort, which is not a disqualification but is a reason for particular caution about whether my preaching has any purchase on reality.

What the Exodus demands is not simply that the church develop programs for the poor. It demands that the church form an alternative consciousness — a community that genuinely believes the cry of the oppressed reaches God, that God's response is liberation rather than comfort for the oppressors, and that the church's calling is to participate in that liberation rather than to maintain its own position within the existing order.

God heard their groaning. He remembered the covenant. He was concerned.

The church that has no concern is the church that has forgotten the story it was built on.
