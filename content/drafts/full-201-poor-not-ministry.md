Churches have food pantries. They have mercy funds. They have occasional mission Sundays where a guest speaker from a local shelter describes the work, the offering plate comes around, and the congregation feels the satisfaction of having helped. All of this is good. None of it is what the Bible means.

The biblical position on the poor is not ambiguous, and it is not structured as a ministry option. It runs from Exodus to Revelation as one of the most consistent themes in all of Scripture — so consistent, so repeated, so theologically central that the church's tendency to treat it as a specialized calling rather than a baseline requirement deserves examination.

In Exodus, the gleaning laws embedded care for the poor into the fabric of the economic system: you did not harvest the edges of your field, you did not go back for what you dropped, because the widow and the sojourner and the fatherless needed it (Leviticus 19:9-10). This was not charity. It was law — the structural acknowledgment that the community's prosperity was not solely the property of whoever earned it. The margins belonged, by God's design, to the vulnerable.

The prophets escalated this. Amos does not call Israel to increase its charitable giving. He calls out the wealthy who "trample on the heads of the poor as on the dust of the ground" and "deny justice to the oppressed" (Amos 2:7). Isaiah announces what God thinks of worship performed by people who ignore the poor: "Your burnt offerings are not acceptable to me" (Isaiah 1:11-17). The worship, however liturgically correct, is rejected. Because justice has been withheld. The two cannot be separated.

Jesus does not soften this. Matthew 25 is the most direct statement of the matter in the Gospels — not a parable with a spiritual application layered on top, but a direct identification: what you did to the least of these, you did to me (Matthew 25:40). The Lord of the final judgment is identified with the hungry, the sick, the imprisoned, the stranger. Not metaphorically. As a structural claim about where Christ is located in the world and therefore about where the church must be oriented.

James — whose epistle reads like the New Testament's prophetic literature — asks a question that should permanently destabilize the church's comfortable relationship with its resources: "Suppose a brother or sister is without clothes and daily food. If one of you says to them, 'Go in peace; keep warm and well fed,' but does nothing about their physical needs, what good is it?" (James 2:15-16). The answer is: no good at all. The faith that does not result in this kind of care is not faith. It is vocabulary.

I have been in churches — I have led in churches — where the poor were a ministry category: managed by a dedicated team, funded by a line item, referenced in the annual report as evidence of community engagement, and kept at a sufficient structural distance from the main life of the congregation that the wealthy members did not have to alter their actual lives to accommodate them. We called it mercy ministry. We felt good about it. What we were doing was organizing compassion in a way that kept it from becoming costly.

That is not what the Scripture describes.

The question is not whether your church has a food pantry. The question is whether the poor shape the actual priorities of the congregation — where resources go, who sits in leadership, whose neighborhood the church considers its parish, what decisions get made about the building budget versus the mercy budget. The test of genuine Christian devotion, across the entire canonical witness, is not the quality of the worship service. It is the quality of care for the vulnerable.

This makes many comfortable Christians uncomfortable, which is one piece of evidence that it is true.

The poor are not a ministry category to be managed by the people who have time for that kind of thing. They are the measure of whether the church has understood what it is. They are, in Matthew 25, the face of Christ — and the church that walks past that face while congratulating itself on its worship has made an error it cannot afford to leave unexamined.

Look again. More carefully. See who is there.
