The text is not ambiguous. It never was.

Leviticus 19:33-34: "When a stranger sojourns with you in your land, you shall not do him wrong. You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt: I am the Lord your God."

That last phrase — I am the Lord your God — is not ornamental. It appears throughout Leviticus 19 as a divine signature, an assertion of authority. When the Lord signs a command with his own name, he is not offering a suggestion. He is binding the covenant community to a standard that is inseparable from who he is. The treatment of the stranger is not a secondary application of the law. It is a direct expression of the character of the God who gave it.

The church that has spent recent years debating the ethics of immigration while treating Leviticus 19 as a historical curiosity has made a theological error of the first order.

## The memory at the center

The command does not stand alone. It is rooted in a specific historical memory: you were strangers in the land of Egypt. This is not analogy. This is the mechanism of the command. Israel is commanded to protect the stranger because Israel knows from inside what it means to be a stranger — to be powerless, to be subject to the decisions of a host society that can exploit or expel at will, to have no legal standing, no advocate, no protection except whatever the powerful choose to extend.

God took note of what Egypt did to Israel in that condition. God intervened. The entire narrative of Exodus — the deliverance, the wilderness, the law at Sinai — flows from that intervention. And then God commands: because you know what that is, because you were in that place, you will not be Egypt to the stranger in your land.

The American church exists in a country whose founding mythology includes the Exodus. Puritan settlers identified themselves explicitly with Israel fleeing Egypt, with the Atlantic as the Red Sea and the New World as Canaan. The metaphor has been deployed again and again in American religious history, and I am not going to argue its accuracy here. But if the church has borrowed that metaphor, it has also inherited the command at its center. You do not get to be Israel without being obligated by what it means to have been a stranger.

## What the crisis reveals

Every border crisis — and this one is not the first — reveals what the church actually believes about the stranger, as opposed to what it preaches about him.

The debate around immigration policy involves genuine complexity. I am not arguing that open borders are the obvious and only faithful policy position, or that every element of border enforcement is categorically wrong. Nations have responsibilities for order and security that are not illegitimate. A careful reader can hold those concerns alongside the commands of Leviticus 19.

What is not defensible is the silence. What is not defensible is the church that has weeks of internal discussion about worship style, budget priorities, and children's programming, and has had no serious conversation about the families separated at the border, the asylum seekers in detention, the undocumented worker in the congregation who is afraid of what the next executive order means for her children. The silence is a position. It is just a position we have not been honest enough to name.

Jeremiah 22:3 summarizes the prophetic demand: "Do justice and righteousness, and deliver from the hand of the oppressor him who has been robbed. And do no wrong or violence to the resident alien, the fatherless, and the widow." The resident alien appears again. He always appears. He is not a policy footnote. He is a prophetic test.

## What we have done instead

I have watched the American evangelical church, in recent years, perform elaborate theological gymnastics to avoid the plain reading of texts like Leviticus 19. The gymnastics usually take one of two forms. The first is dispensationalism applied to ethics: that was Israel's theocratic law, it does not apply to a secular nation-state, we live in a different covenant era. The second is the separation of justice and politics: I care about the immigrant as a person, but immigration policy is a political question and the church should not be political.

Both moves fail.

The first fails because the New Testament does not abandon the logic of Leviticus 19. Jesus quotes Leviticus 19:18 — love your neighbor as yourself — as one of the two greatest commandments, and the parable of the Good Samaritan expands the definition of neighbor to include precisely the person the religious establishment had decided to walk past. The stranger is still in the text.

The second fails because the prophets were relentlessly political in the specific sense that they named what political and economic systems were doing to the poor and the stranger. Avoiding politics was not an option available to Amos or Isaiah or Jeremiah. It is not available to us.

## The word that remains

Love him as yourself. Not love him as you would a distant beneficiary of your charitable giving. Not love him as you would a mission project. As yourself.

That standard is demanding. It requires asking what I would want, what I would need, what I would be owed, if I were in his position. It does not resolve every policy question. But it tells me where to start, and it tells me what silence costs.

We were strangers once. We were not forgotten. The command is: do not forget.
