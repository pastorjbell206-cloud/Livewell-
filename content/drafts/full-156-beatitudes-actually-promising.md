The Beatitudes are the most domesticated passage in the New Testament. We have managed to read them as a comfort without allowing them to be a claim.

"Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 5:3). We read this and feel warm. We do not read it and ask what it means that the kingdom belongs to the poor — not to those who are spiritually sensitive enough to recognize their need, but to those who have been ground down by the weight of a world that excludes them. We spiritualize quickly because the alternative is to take the Beatitudes seriously as a political announcement.

Luke's version is less easy to domesticate. "Blessed are the poor" — without the qualifier "in spirit" — "for yours is the kingdom of God" (Luke 6:20). And immediately following: "But woe to you who are rich, for you have already received your comfort" (Luke 6:24). Jesus is not delivering this from a retreat center. He is standing on a level place, surrounded by a crowd that includes the sick, the demon-possessed, the economically desperate — people who had come from great distances because they had nothing else to try (Luke 6:17-18). He is announcing, to specific human beings in specific circumstances, that God's account of their situation differs from the world's account.

The Greek word translated "blessed" is *makarios* — a word that in its pre-Christian usage described the state of the Greek gods: those who were free from the anxieties and limitations of ordinary human life. Jesus takes this word and places it over the poor, the mourning, the hungry, the persecuted. He is not saying: God will eventually make it up to you. He is saying: the order of reality that God is bringing into existence has already arrived, and in that order, these are the ones who are standing in the right place.

This is a political claim. It destabilizes the arrangements of the present world by announcing that they are not ultimate. The person who is powerful and comfortable and respected under the present order receives none of the Beatitudes. The person who is poor, meek, hungry, and persecuted receives them all. This is not accidental symmetry. It is the announcement of a reversal — the same reversal that runs through the Magnificat, through the servant songs of Isaiah, through the prophetic tradition from Amos forward.

I have preached the Beatitudes as a comfort for people experiencing difficulty. This is not wrong. It is incomplete. The comfort the Beatitudes offer is not primarily psychological — hang in there, God sees you, relief is coming. The comfort is political — the arrangements of the present world are not the arrangements of the coming world, and the coming world is already interrupting this one.

To take the Beatitudes seriously is to sit with the discomfort of the woes. Jesus says woe to the rich. Woe to those who are well-fed. Woe to those who are laughing. These are not warnings for people who love money too much or who laugh at the wrong things. They are warnings for people who have arranged their lives so comfortably within the present order that the announcement of a different order is threat rather than relief.

The Sermon on the Mount is not a morality lecture. It is a kingdom announcement. It describes the life of the community that has accepted the announcement and is beginning to organize itself around the new reality rather than the old one. The poor are not spiritually superior. The meek are not morally better. They are, in the language of the kingdom, positioned. They are closer to what God is doing than those who have everything the present world offers.

This does not make poverty a virtue. The Beatitudes do not romanticize destitution. But they refuse to accept the world's account of who is flourishing and who is not. They announce that God's account differs. And they call the community of Jesus to organize its life around the divine account rather than the human one — to be the place where the poor are treated as belonging to the kingdom, because, on Jesus's telling, they do.

That is a more demanding claim than a comfort. It requires something. The church that reads the Beatitudes and changes nothing has not yet heard them.
