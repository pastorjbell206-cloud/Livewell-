Somewhere in the formation of American pastoral identity, we absorbed a theology of the indispensable pastor. The man who is first to arrive and last to leave. The man who handles the hospital calls and the sermon prep and the board meeting and the counseling caseload and the building project, because no one else can do it the way it needs to be done. We have baptized exhaustion and called it calling.

I have been that man. I know exactly what it tells itself at night — that the congregation needs this, that delegation means settling for less than the best, that the pastor's job is to give everything. The self-story sounds like sacrifice. It is mostly control.

What the book of Exodus shows us, in a passage that does not get preached nearly enough, is a man in exactly that position and a mentor unafraid to name it. Moses was settling disputes from morning until evening, the whole nation lined up in front of him, while his father-in-law Jethro watched. Jethro's assessment was direct: "What you are doing is not good. You will surely wear out, both yourself and these people who are with you" (Exodus 18:17–18). He was not questioning Moses's devotion. He was questioning Moses's theology — his operating assumption that bearing the whole weight alone was a sign of faithfulness rather than a failure of leadership.

Jethro's solution was not to make Moses lazy. It was to build a structure. Capable men over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens. Moses would handle the hard cases. The rest would be distributed to people trained and trusted to handle them. The work did not suffer. Moses did not become irrelevant. The mission became sustainable.

Paul understood the same thing. His letters are full of names — Titus, Timothy, Epaphras, Priscilla, Aquila — people he trusted with specific work in specific places. He was not managing from a distance out of convenience. He was operating from a theological conviction that the body of Christ is not designed to run through a single person. Each member has a function. The eye cannot say to the hand, "I have no need of you" (1 Corinthians 12:21). The pastor who decides he has no need of anyone has misread the text that gives his office its meaning.

Here is what delegation actually requires: it requires you to believe that other people can be trusted with things that matter.

That belief is harder than it sounds. I have heard pastors say they tried delegating and it didn't work. What they often mean is that someone else did the thing differently than they would have — not worse, differently — and they could not live with the difference. That is not a delegation problem. That is a control problem dressed in pastoral clothing. The task was not done badly. It was done without me, and I could not tell the difference between that loss and actual failure.

The ministry that depends entirely on you is not a strong ministry. It is a single point of failure. When you are sick, it struggles. When you travel, it suffers. When you eventually cannot sustain the pace — and you will not be able to sustain the pace — the whole structure comes down because it was built around one person rather than around a mission that outlasts any individual.

Leadership is not the accumulation of responsibilities. Leadership is the development of people who can carry responsibility. Moses had to become a leader of leaders. So do you.

The man who will not give anything away has not learned to trust — not people, and not the God who placed those people in the congregation for reasons that include their usefulness to the mission. Delegation is, underneath the management language, an act of faith. It says: God has provided enough people, and enough grace in those people, that this does not all have to run through me.

You are not the church. You are a servant of it. Serve it by building it to work without you at the center.

That is the hardest thing I have had to learn. I am still learning it.
