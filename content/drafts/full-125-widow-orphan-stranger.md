Count them through the canon. The widow, the orphan, the stranger — these three appear together in the law, the prophets, the wisdom literature, and the New Testament with a consistency that demands explanation. Exodus 22. Deuteronomy 10, 14, 16, 24, 26, 27. Zechariah 7. Malachi 3. James 1. They are not incidental examples of God's generosity. They are the recurring measure by which Israel's faithfulness to the covenant is assessed.

The measure is specific. Not: did Israel worship at the appointed festivals? Not: did she maintain the correct liturgical forms? But: what did she do with the people who had no advocate? What happened to the woman whose husband had died and whose legal standing died with him? What happened to the child with no father to speak for him in the gate? What happened to the immigrant who lived among them without the protections that citizenship would have afforded?

This is not peripheral ethics. It is the core of the covenant requirement. Deuteronomy 10:17-18 places care for the orphan and the widow alongside the most fundamental claims about God's own character: *For the Lord your God is God of gods and Lord of lords, the great God, mighty and awesome, who shows no partiality and accepts no bribes. He defends the cause of the fatherless and the widow, and loves the foreigner residing among you, giving them food and clothing.* The community of faith is called to reflect what God is. God defends the widow. God loves the stranger. Therefore.

The prophets understood this with terrible clarity. Isaiah 1 is a comprehensive indictment of Israel's religion — the sacrifices, the appointed feasts, the prayers, all of it — and the indictment rests on a specific charge: *Learn to do right; seek justice. Defend the oppressed. Take up the cause of the fatherless; plead the case of the widow* (Isaiah 1:17). Worship that is not attended by this kind of attention to the vulnerable is not acceptable to God. It is something worse than unacceptable: it is an offense.

I have read these texts in comfortable churches, including my own. We have not done well with them. We have spiritualized the widow into an image of spiritual need, the orphan into a metaphor for longing, the stranger into a category we can abstract into the distance. What we have not done, most of us, is ask who the actual widow in our actual congregation is — who the child without a father present is, whose name appears in our directory — and what we have actually done for her this year. Not planned to do. Done.

The fatherlessness piece is personal for me. I was raised without a father. I know what it means to grow up with no one to speak for you in the rooms where decisions get made about your future. I know what it means to be a stranger in those rooms. The text is not abstract to me. And the church's failure to take the orphan — including the grown orphan, the adult who carries that wound — seriously enough to actually organize around it is a failure I feel in my own history.

The New Testament does not retire this language. James defines religion that is pure and undefiled before God as this: *to care for orphans and widows in their distress* (James 1:27). Not to hold correct theology about them. To care for them. The Greek word is *episkeptomai* — to look upon, to visit, to be present with. To see.

These three figures are not a ministry category. They are not a demographic for a targeted outreach program. They are the ones God has said he watches, and the ones God has said will reveal whether we worship him in truth or merely in form.

They are watching too.
