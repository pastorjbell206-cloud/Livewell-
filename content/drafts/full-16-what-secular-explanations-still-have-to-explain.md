I want to make the strongest case for the secular worldview I can. The strongest case is stronger than most Christians admit. It is also incomplete. I want to be honest about both, because the cumulative case for Christianity does not depend on the secular case being weak. The cumulative case depends on the secular case leaving certain things unexplained.

I should say, before I begin, that I am not going to refute secular naturalism in this essay. I do not think it can be refuted in the strict sense. I am going to grant it the best version of itself, then identify the explanatory remainders, and then say what I think the remainders suggest. The skeptic who is paying attention will rightly observe that none of the remainders is, on its own, a proof of God. None of them is. The cumulative case is what is at stake, not any single one of them. Stay with me for the cumulative argument; it is the only honest version available.

## Granting the case

The strongest version of the secular case runs like this. The universe is what physics says it is. It began roughly 13.8 billion years ago, has expanded and cooled according to laws we can mostly describe, and has produced, on at least one small planet, the conditions for life. Life on this planet emerged through chemistry, and has evolved over billions of years through descent with modification and natural selection. Human beings are the current endpoint of one branch of that evolution. Our minds, our morality, our religious experiences, our sense of meaning — all of these can be accounted for, on the strongest secular view, as products of natural processes operating over long time scales. There is no need to invoke a divine designer, a cosmic moral lawgiver, or a transcendent meaning-giver. The universe explains itself, or at least, the parts of itself that can be explained admit of explanations that do not require God.

I want to grant most of this. The science is good science. The evolutionary biology is well-supported. The cosmological account is the best account we have. The neuroscience that increasingly explains religious experience in terms of brain function is real work, not propaganda. The historical-critical analysis of religious texts that traces their composition to identifiable human authors with identifiable concerns is honest scholarship. The standard secular case is not a position held by stupid people. It is a position held by some of the best minds of our time, and the case has gotten stronger over the past two centuries, not weaker.

The pop Christian apologetics that pretends otherwise is an embarrassment. The arguments against evolution that have circulated in evangelical circles for forty years are mostly bad arguments, made by people who do not understand the science they are critiquing. The claims that secular morality is impossible are empirically false; most atheists I know are good people. The dismissals of historical-critical work on the Bible are usually dismissals from people who have not read the work. The cumulative case for Christianity does not depend on dismissing the secular case. Christians who have built their case on those dismissals have built on sand, and the sand is washing away in the people they have raised on it.

I want to make this concession in full, because the rest of this essay only works if the concession is real. Yes — the secular case has real strength. Yes — the evolutionary account is well-supported. Yes — neuroscience can describe brain states correlated with religious experience. Yes — historical-critical work can identify the composition history of biblical texts. None of this disqualifies what I am about to say. What I am about to say is that the strongest secular case still leaves a set of things unexplained, and the things that remain unexplained are not minor curiosities. They are central to what it is to be a human being in this universe.

## Five remainders

The first remainder is the cosmological one. *Why is there something rather than nothing?* The question is old. Leibniz formulated it cleanly in the seventeenth century. The standard contemporary secular response — that the question may not be well-formed, that nothing as a state may be incoherent, that the universe is a brute fact — is a serious response. It is also, on close reading, an admission rather than an explanation. The brute-fact response says that the question we are asking is the question for which we have no answer; the response does not show that an answer would have been impossible in principle. The Christian theological tradition has a different response — that the existence of the contingent universe points to a necessary being whose existence is not contingent on anything else, and that the necessary being is what we mean by God. The Christian response is not a proof of God. The Christian response is an explanatory framework for the cosmological question that the secular response declares unanswerable. Neither side has a knockout. The Christian side has, in my reading, the more satisfying account of what is actually being asked.

The second remainder is the consciousness one. *What is consciousness, and why is there something it is like to be us?* The hard problem of consciousness, as David Chalmers named it in 1995, is the problem of explaining how the subjective experience of being a conscious being arises from the physical processes of the brain. The secular response — that consciousness is an emergent property of complex information processing — describes what we observe but does not explain why information processing is accompanied by subjective experience at all. Other complex systems do information processing without, so far as we can tell, having anything it is like to be them. Why are we different? The question has been a central one in philosophy of mind for forty years, and the strongest secular accounts — Daniel Dennett's, principally — are eliminativist, denying that there is a hard problem at all. The denial is honest, but it has been unpersuasive to most readers, including most secular philosophers. The Christian framework — that consciousness is an aspect of the *imago Dei*, that human persons are made in the image of a conscious God — does not solve the hard problem. The Christian framework does provide a metaphysical setting in which the hard problem is not a hard problem; it is what we would expect, if persons were what the Christian tradition says persons are.

The third remainder is the moral one. *Why does moral realism feel inescapable, even to people who deny its possibility?* When we say *the Holocaust was wrong,* we mean something stronger than *we personally dislike it* or *our culture disapproves.* We mean that it was actually, mind-independently, wrong. The secular options for grounding moral realism — evolutionary ethics, contractarian foundations, intuitionist appeals to moral facts — each do some work, none is finally compelling. The honest secular moralists I have read often grant that the strong sense of moral realism is difficult to ground secularly, even as they affirm the moral realism. The Christian framework — that morality is grounded in the character of God, and that the human capacity to recognize moral truth is a participation in the divine — provides a metaphysical setting in which the inescapable feel of moral realism is what we would expect. Again, not a proof. An explanation that fits.

The fourth remainder is the historical Jesus. The first-century Galilean rabbi who was crucified under Pilate, whose followers very quickly believed he had risen from the dead, whose movement spread rapidly through the Roman world despite enormous costs to its adherents, and whose impact on Western civilization is, as Tom Holland's *Dominion* (2019) documents, larger than the impact of any other figure in human history — this person exists in the historical record. The secular accounts of what happened — that the resurrection was hallucination, that the body was stolen, that the early Christian movement misremembered or invented its founding stories — each have to explain a great deal of historical data, and on close inspection, each has explanatory difficulties of its own. The historical Jesus essay will take this up in detail. For now, I will just say: even the strongly skeptical secular historian, working without theological commitments, generally grants that something happened in first-century Palestine that the standard secular accounts have trouble explaining cleanly. The remainder is real.

The fifth remainder is the experiential one. *Why does the personal experience of the divine persist across cultures, centuries, and contexts, including among people whose other beliefs would predict otherwise?* The phenomenology of religious experience, documented in detail in William James's *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902) and in the more recent work of Rudolf Otto and others, is consistent across an enormous range of contexts. People in cultures with very different metaphysical commitments report similar kinds of encounters with what they describe as the divine. The secular response — that all such experiences are reducible to brain states, mistaken perceptions, or cultural conditioning — accounts for the phenomenology but does not, in my reading, account for the consistent intelligibility of the experiences when reported by people from radically different traditions. The Christian framework does not require all religious experience to be authentic encounter with God; the Christian tradition has its own ways of distinguishing the genuine from the counterfeit. The Christian framework does provide a setting in which the consistent phenomenology is what we would expect, if there is a real God who is making himself, however dimly, accessible to created persons.

These are five remainders. None is a knockout. Each is an explanatory difficulty that the strongest secular case has not fully resolved. The cumulative case for Christianity is the case that the remainders, taken together, are better accounted for by the Christian metaphysical framework than by the secular alternative. The case is not a proof. The case is a comparative judgment about which framework leaves less unexplained.

## What the secular framework does explain better

I want to be fair. The secular framework explains some things better than the Christian one.

The secular framework explains the variety of religions better than the Christian framework does, on the surface. The secular account — that humans have a religious instinct, expressed differently in different cultural contexts — is a more parsimonious account of the diversity than the Christian account, which has to say that the diversity reflects partial truth in various traditions but uniquely complete truth in one of them. The Christian account can be defended; it is not the more parsimonious account at first glance.

The secular framework explains certain features of evolutionary biology more parsimoniously than the Christian framework, on the surface. The Christian framework is compatible with evolutionary biology, but it has to do more work to incorporate the apparent waste, the long timescales, and the predator-prey violence of the evolutionary process. The secular framework does not have to do this work, because the secular framework does not require the process to be morally significant.

The secular framework explains the apparent indifference of the universe to human flourishing more parsimoniously than the Christian framework does. The secular framework says: of course the universe is indifferent; the universe is not the kind of thing that could have intentions. The Christian framework says: the universe is the creation of a personal God, and the apparent indifference is part of a fallen condition that will be put right at a future consummation. The Christian framework has to do more explaining.

I want to grant these. The secular framework is not a position to be dismissed. It is a position to be engaged seriously. The engagement is the next several essays. The engagement does not conclude that the secular framework is finally adequate. The engagement concludes that the cumulative case, with all the goods on both sides, comes out — in my reading and in my experience — in favor of the Christian framework. The skeptic who reads the same evidence and concludes the other way is not making an obvious mistake. The skeptic and I are looking at the same data and reading it differently. The reading is finally a personal commitment under conditions of incomplete evidence. The commitment matters, in both directions.

## The verdict

Secular naturalism is a serious worldview. So is Christianity. The serious skeptic and the serious Christian agree on more than the lazy versions of either admit. They disagree on a few load-bearing questions that have not yet been settled by the best argument of either side.

The cumulative case I am making over this series will not, by the seventh essay, prove that the Christian explanation is true. The case will give the honest skeptic a more accurate picture of what serious Christianity actually claims, why the claims are more defensible than pop apologetics has often made them look, and what is at stake in either accepting or rejecting them. The honest skeptic, having read the case, will be in a better position to make his own decision. That is the most I can do. The decision is yours.

The next essay takes up the hardest of the remainders — the problem of suffering. I want to do this honestly. The pop apologetic answers are too clean. The biblical answers are messier and, in my experience, more livable. Sit with me through it. The essay after will be the historical Jesus, which is where the cumulative case turns from negative — what the secular framework cannot explain — to positive — what the Christian framework can explain that nothing else has been able to. We will get to the Bible, the moral question, and the cost in subsequent essays. The series will end with the honest accounting of what acceptance of this would require of you. You will know, by the end, what you are being asked to consider. The asking is real. The decision is yours.
