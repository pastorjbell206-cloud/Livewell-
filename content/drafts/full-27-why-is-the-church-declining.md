Church attendance in America is collapsing. Most of my fellow pastors blame the culture. The blame is partial truth and full lie. The decline is mostly our fault. We made the church difficult to admire, and the people who could not admire us anymore are the ones who used to fill our buildings. The repentance starts here.

I want to write this essay carefully because the conversation about church decline in American evangelical circles has been almost completely captured by self-protective explanations, and the self-protective explanations are not honest, and the dishonest explanations have prevented us from doing the actual work the decline is calling for. The work is repentance. The repentance is structural and is personal. Neither is being done at scale. I want to make the case for why both are necessary, and what they look like in practice.

## What the numbers actually show

Let me begin with the data, briefly, because the data is the necessary starting point.

Pew Research and Barna and the Public Religion Research Institute have been tracking American religious affiliation for two decades. The trends are consistent across the sources. American religious affiliation has declined by roughly thirty percentage points among adults since the early 1990s. The decline has been steepest among white Protestants, white Catholics, and historically white evangelical congregations. The category of *religiously unaffiliated* — the so-called *nones* — has grown from roughly six percent of American adults in the early 1990s to roughly thirty percent today, with the trend continuing.

The decline is not evenly distributed. It has been most severe in the mainline Protestant denominations, which have been losing members for half a century. It has accelerated in evangelical denominations over the past fifteen years, with the Southern Baptist Convention reporting its lowest membership in seven decades. It has been most severe among the youngest generations; the millennial and Gen Z cohorts are leaving at higher rates than any previous American generation, and they are not returning to religious affiliation at the rates earlier generations did when they began having children.

The decline is real. The decline is sustained. The decline shows no sign of reversing. The standard evangelical explanations of the decline have been broadly inadequate, and I want to take them up in turn.

## The standard explanations and why they fail

The first standard explanation is the *secular culture* explanation. The argument is that American culture has become aggressively secular, that the institutions of cultural authority — universities, media, corporations — have turned against Christianity, and that the decline is the predictable result of cultural hostility.

There is something to this. American cultural institutions have become less explicitly Christian than they were sixty years ago. The cultural messaging young Americans receive does not, on the whole, support orthodox Christian commitments in the way it did when those young Americans' grandparents were the same age.

But the explanation is incomplete in important ways. The countries with the most aggressively secular cultures — most of Western Europe — were already largely post-Christian by the 1970s, and yet have continued to produce serious Christian communities, including substantial revival movements. The cultural environment is not destiny. Christianity has, throughout its history, survived and grown in cultural environments far more hostile than contemporary America. Pointing to secular culture as the primary explanation lets us avoid the harder work of examining what is happening inside the church that has made the church itself less compelling to people who watched it from outside.

The second standard explanation is the *media bias* explanation. The argument is that Christian institutions have been unfairly maligned by progressive media, that the public perception of evangelical Christianity has been distorted, and that the decline reflects this distortion more than the actual realities of evangelical life.

This explanation has some truth and a great deal more falsehood. Some media coverage of evangelical institutions has been unfair. Most media coverage has been more accurate than the institutions covered would like to acknowledge. The sexual abuse scandals reported in evangelical institutions over the past fifteen years — at Sovereign Grace, at Willow Creek, at Hillsong, in the Southern Baptist Convention generally — were real scandals. The financial scandals were real scandals. The leadership-abuse stories were largely true. The complicity in racism documented across multiple historical and contemporary studies was largely true. The institutional cover-ups were largely true. The media did not invent these stories. The media reported what was happening, and what was happening was bad, and the institutions that were exposed have been only partially repentant about what was exposed. Blaming the media is a way of avoiding the substance of the reporting.

The third standard explanation is the *generational drift* explanation. The argument is that every generation has its drift away from religion in early adulthood, and that the current generation is following an old pattern that will reverse when they begin having children of their own.

This explanation was credible thirty years ago. It is no longer credible. The data on generational return to religion has been declining for several cohorts running. Millennials are not returning at the rates Gen X did, and Gen X did not return at the rates baby boomers did. The pattern has shifted. The drift is not a temporary feature of life-cycle change; the drift is, on the best evidence available, a more permanent demographic shift. The institutions banking on the return-when-they-have-children dynamic have been disappointed and will continue to be.

## The explanation I actually believe

The honest explanation, in my reading, is that American evangelicalism over the past fifty years made itself difficult to admire, and the people who could no longer admire it left.

Let me name what they watched. They watched the sexual abuse scandals, and watched the institutional responses range from inadequate to actively obstructive. They watched the financial scandals — celebrity pastors with private jets, megachurch budgets with no public accountability, prosperity preachers shaking down vulnerable Christians for money. They watched the politicization of the pulpit, the transformation of churches into wings of partisan political coalitions, the willingness to defend morally indefensible politicians as long as the politicians delivered on certain policy preferences. They watched the racial blindness — the white evangelical institutions' inability to honestly reckon with their own histories of slavery, segregation, and complicity in racial violence. They watched the anti-intellectualism, the willingness to deploy bad arguments against scientific consensus on questions where the science was not actually contested, the suspicion of education even when education was the only path to the kinds of work the people in our churches needed to do. They watched the entertainment-driven worship displacing the deeper liturgical and theological practices the historic church had relied on. They watched the marriages of celebrity pastors fail at rates indistinguishable from the surrounding culture, sometimes with the cover-ups that were worse than the failures themselves.

What they watched, taken together, was a Christianity that was difficult to admire as Christianity. The Christ of the Gospels was not visible in much of what they were being told was Christianity. The exit was a rational response to what they had seen. The exit was, in many cases, the most morally serious thing the leavers could have done.

I want to be precise about this. I am not saying every American Christian institution was bad. I am not saying every leaver was leaving for good reasons. The institutional church in America has always been a mix of faithful and unfaithful elements, and most leavers were leaving from particular congregations and traditions that had particular failures, not from some uniform monolith. But the dominant institutional witness of American evangelicalism over the past fifty years has been, in significant respects, a witness that was difficult to admire even by the standards Christianity sets for itself. The decline reflects the witness. The witness was the cause. Blaming the culture is one way of avoiding what is in front of us.

## What repentance would actually look like

The standard institutional response to the decline has been a combination of doubling down on the strategies that caused the decline and blaming the leavers for leaving. Neither response is adequate. The response that would be adequate is repentance, and the repentance is specific.

Repent of the sexual abuse cover-ups. Name names. Pay reparations. Reform the structures that allowed the abuse to happen and be hidden. Stop protecting the institutional reputation at the cost of the survivors. The work has been begun in some quarters and is incomplete almost everywhere. The work has to be done before the decline can be addressed.

Repent of the racial blindness. The white evangelical tradition's history on slavery, on segregation, on the civil rights era, on the racial dynamics of the contemporary American church — all of this has to be honestly reckoned with. The reckoning is not complete in any major evangelical denomination. The reckoning is what black brothers and sisters in our churches and outside our churches have been asking for, and the asking has been refused or deflected for too long.

Repent of the politicization of the pulpit. The conversion of churches into partisan political organizations has been one of the most catastrophic moves of the past half-century. The unwinding of the conversion is hard, slow work. The work has to happen one congregation at a time, with pastors willing to lose congregants to recover the gospel.

Repent of the anti-intellectualism. The hostility to careful biblical scholarship, to honest engagement with scientific evidence, to serious philosophical and historical work — this has been a self-inflicted wound. The wound is healing in some institutions and not in others. The healing is necessary if the church is going to produce the next generation of pastors and lay leaders who can hold the faith intellectually as well as devotionally.

Repent of the entertainment-driven worship that has displaced theological and liturgical depth. The deeper recovery of historic Christian worship is the only path to the formation of disciples who can carry the faith through the post-Christendom moment. The recovery is slow. The recovery is necessary.

Repent of the celebrity-pastor culture that has produced the recurring scandals. The accountability structures of the historic church — elder boards with actual authority, denominational oversight that actually oversees, congregational polity that limits the autonomy of charismatic leaders — exist for reasons. The reasons are being relearned at significant cost.

## The verdict

The decline is real and is mostly deserved. The decline is judgment. Judgment is not always bad news. Judgment is sometimes the love that does what nothing else has been able to do — that strips away the false expressions of faith so the true expression can become visible.

I am writing this from inside the American evangelical tradition, as a pastor who has spent twenty years inside its institutions, and as someone who has been formed by both its goods and its failures. The repentance I am calling for is the repentance I owe in my own work. The repentance is not abstract. The repentance is specific, costly, and ongoing.

The American church that is being judged can, on the historical evidence, come out the other side of the judgment. Smaller. Costlier. More honest. More faithful. The coming-out is conditional on the repentance. The institutions that refuse to repent will continue to shrink, will continue to scandal, will continue to lose the credibility that took two centuries to build and is being squandered in a single generation. The institutions that repent will be smaller and stronger. The choice is real. The choice is being made now, in particular congregations, by particular pastors and leaders, under conditions that do not allow much more delay.

I am hopeful about the church on the other side of the judgment. I am not hopeful about most of the institutions on this side. The next essay in this writing project takes up one of the specific institutional failures that the decline has exposed — the megachurch model that was always going to end this way, and the reasons the model produced what it produced. The megachurch is collapsing publicly. The collapse has not been a surprise. The next essay says why.
