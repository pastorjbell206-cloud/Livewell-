David wrote Psalm 22 from a place no one who had not been there could fully identify. "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from saving me, so far from my cries of anguish?" He was not performing grief. He was reporting it. And the first movement of that psalm is solitary in a way that only someone who has carried a weight in the dark can recognize — a man calling out and hearing nothing return, surrounded by people who do not understand what he is holding.

That is not just a spiritual portrait. It is a leadership portrait.

The loneliness of leading a congregation is structural. It is built into the role, not a symptom of doing it wrong. You hold information people gave you in confidence — the couple whose marriage is collapsing, the elder whose son is in trouble, the woman in the third row who told you something she has never told anyone. You carry that knowledge into every room. You make decisions that affect people's lives and you often cannot explain why you made them without breaching the trust that made you worth telling in the first place. You see the institution from above and below and inside simultaneously, in a way that no individual member does, and the full view is not something you can share over lunch.

This is not complaint. This is description. Leadership requires the willingness to bear weight that cannot be distributed.

But here is what I have come to understand: the loneliness that is structural is one thing. The loneliness that is self-imposed is another, and it is the more dangerous kind. I have watched men in ministry seal themselves off — not because the role demanded it, but because they decided that asking for help was a vulnerability they could not afford, that admitting struggle was inconsistent with the authority they needed to lead. They confused the necessary aloneness of decision-making with a total isolation that was not necessary at all.

The result is almost always the same. A man who seemed fine for a long time, who was performing competence successfully, and then one day was not — and the people around him had no idea it was coming because he had made absolutely sure they would not.

Moses carried the disputes of an entire nation alone until his father-in-law Jethro saw it and told him plainly: "What you are doing is not good. You and these people who come to you will only wear yourselves out. The work is too heavy for you; you cannot handle it alone" (Exodus 18:17–18). That assessment came from outside Moses. Moses was too close to see it. He needed someone positioned differently to name what the situation required.

The pastoral tradition has never held up total self-sufficiency as a virtue. The elders in Acts 20 gathered together. Paul wrote to churches and received reports from trusted people about how those churches were actually faring. The epistles are not the product of a man working alone — they are the product of a man in constant correspondence, constantly informed, constantly accompanied by a network of co-laborers and friends who told him the truth.

You are not called to carry this alone. You are called to lead well, and leading well requires being honest with at least a small number of people about what it is actually costing you. A spiritual director. A peer pastor who knows the weight of the same kind of work. A therapist who understands ministry contexts. Whoever that person is — the form matters less than the substance. The substance is this: someone who knows what you are actually carrying, not just what you perform in public.

The loneliness of leadership is real. It is also, in part, a choice.

The decision to remain alone with the weight is not heroic. It is, in the end, a form of pride — the belief that the need for accompaniment is a sign of inadequacy. The pastor who cannot be known is not protecting his congregation. He is protecting himself. And the protection eventually becomes the thing that breaks him.

Find the people who can hold what you are holding. Not to fix it. To witness it.

That is often enough.
