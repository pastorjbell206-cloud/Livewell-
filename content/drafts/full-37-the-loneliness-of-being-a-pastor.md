I am writing this from a hotel room in a city where I came to officiate a funeral. The funeral was for a member of my church who died in a way the church does not yet know about and may not, in some details, ever know. I am sitting with what I know. The sitting is the pastoral loneliness I want to name. Most of the pastors I have ever known have been carrying versions of this. None of them was warned.

This essay is for the pastor who has been doing the work for several years and has noticed, without quite being able to articulate it, that he has become more alone than he expected. The aloneness is real. The aloneness is structural. The aloneness has answers, and the answers are not what the leadership literature usually offers.

## What pastoral loneliness actually is

I want to be precise about what kind of loneliness I am describing, because the word *lonely* covers several different conditions and only one of them is the one I am addressing.

The pastor is rarely socially isolated in the simple sense. The pastor is, in most weeks, in more rooms with more people than most professionals. The pastor knows hundreds of people by name. The pastor has acquaintances across the congregation, across the community, across the denomination. The pastor's calendar is full of meetings, services, lunches, hospital visits, funerals, weddings, and the various other public functions of pastoral life. The simple definition of loneliness — being alone too much — does not apply.

The loneliness I am describing is a different category. It is the loneliness of carrying weight that cannot be put down in any of those rooms. The pastor knows things about the people in his congregation that he cannot share. The pastor carries the burden of confidential conversations from across the years of his ministry. The pastor has watched marriages collapse, watched children disown parents, watched suicides and overdoses and slow declines, watched financial collapses and concealed affairs and the slow erosion of family stability — and the pastor has not been able to talk about any of it, by professional necessity, because the people who confided in him need their confidences kept.

The loneliness is also theological. The pastor is, in most congregations, the only person whose vocation requires the sustained engagement with hard theological questions. The questions the pastor has been wrestling with — about scripture, about doctrine, about God's apparent silence in particular pastoral situations, about his own faith on the difficult days — cannot be shared with most members of his congregation, because most members are not equipped to engage them and would be alarmed by some of them. The pastor cannot share them with most other pastors he knows, because the other pastors are inside the same institutional dynamic and have learned to keep their own wrestling private.

The loneliness is also relational. The pastor is, in many congregations, treated by members as more pastor than person. The members come to him with their needs and their questions and their crises. The members do not, on the whole, come to him to ask how he is doing or to be his friend in the way friends ordinarily are. The pastor's spouse experiences this dynamic too, often more severely; the pastor's spouse is rarely treated by congregants as a person rather than as the appendage to the pastor's ministry. The relational loneliness compounds the other kinds.

## Why the structures produce it

The loneliness I am describing is not a personal failing of particular pastors. It is the structural condition of pastoral life in contemporary American Christianity, and the structure has produced it consistently across denominations, contexts, and personality types.

The structure assumes that the pastor will care for the congregation but not be cared for by the congregation. The pastor is the caregiver, the listener, the source of pastoral presence. The congregation is the recipient. The arrows of care run in one direction. The exceptions exist; some congregations care for their pastors well in some respects. But the structural assumption of the role is that the pastor gives and the congregation receives, and the structural assumption produces, over decades, the depletion that the loneliness is part of.

The structure assumes that the pastor has nowhere outside the congregation to be cared for. Many contemporary American pastors do not have ongoing relationships with denominational officials who function as pastoral support rather than as administrative oversight. Many do not have spiritual directors. Many do not have therapists. Many do not have peer pastors with whom they have built sustained relationships of mutual vulnerability. The structural assumption has been that the pastor's relationship with God should be sufficient for his own spiritual care — and while the relationship with God is necessary and is foundational, the relationship with God has, in the historic Christian tradition, been mediated through human relationships in ways that the contemporary structure has often eliminated.

The structure also assumes that the pastor's spouse is the pastor's primary support — sometimes the pastor's only support. The assumption is unfair to the spouse, who has her own pastoral needs and her own life and her own struggles, and whose own ministry is being subordinated to the structural role of supporting the pastor's. The marriages that succeed in pastoral life often succeed despite this structural assumption rather than because of it, and they succeed because the pastors and their spouses have figured out how to push back against the assumption explicitly.

## What Bonhoeffer named

Dietrich Bonhoeffer, in *Life Together* (1939), names a version of the loneliness I am describing in terms that have helped me locate what I have been carrying. Bonhoeffer wrote *Life Together* while he was the leader of the Confessing Church's underground seminary at Finkenwalde, and the book reflects his experience of pastoral and theological community under conditions much harder than mine. The book is short and is worth reading slowly.

Bonhoeffer's argument, in part, is that the Christian who lives among other Christians has to carry the burden of his own incomplete sanctification in ways that produce a particular loneliness — he is in community, but he is in community with people he cannot fully share himself with, because the sharing would either expose them to what they are not ready to receive or would burden them with what he is not yet ready to give. The loneliness is not a defect in the community; it is a feature of the Christian condition under the not-yet of the kingdom.

Bonhoeffer's answer to this loneliness is not the elimination of it. The loneliness cannot be eliminated, in his reading. The answer is the practice of confession to one Christian brother. *In confession the breakthrough to community takes place.* Bonhoeffer's claim, which has been one of the most important pastoral resources I have found, is that the practice of explicit confession to one trusted Christian who has the authority to hear it and the formation to receive it is the means by which the loneliness becomes bearable.

The historic Christian tradition has known this. The Catholic and Orthodox traditions institutionalized confession in specific structures that the Reformation, in many of its expressions, weakened or dissolved. The Reformed tradition retained the practice in some form — Calvin urges private confession to a minister when the conscience is troubled — but the practice has, in most American evangelical contexts, atrophied. The atrophy has costs. One of the costs is the unaddressed pastoral loneliness I am describing.

## What helps

I want to be concrete about what has helped me, and what has helped the pastors I have walked with through PCN over the past five years.

A spiritual director. A spiritual director outside the congregation, with whom the pastor meets regularly, under explicit confidentiality, for the purpose of pastoral care of the pastor's soul. The spiritual director is not the pastor's therapist, is not his denominational supervisor, is not his elder board chair. The spiritual director is a person specifically trained to accompany the pastor's soul. The role exists. The training exists. The accessibility is harder than it should be, but the resources are available if you look for them.

A confessor. Bonhoeffer's argument applies. The pastor needs at least one Christian to whom he can confess specifically and concretely, who has the formation to receive the confession and to extend the absolution scripture authorizes. The confessor may be the spiritual director, or may be a separate person. The practice should be regular rather than emergency-only.

A pastor's group. The PCN exists in significant part to be this for its members. The group is three to five other pastors, with whom the pastor meets regularly in conditions of mutual trust and explicit confidentiality, where the conversations can be honest about the pastoral and personal struggles each of them is carrying. The group has to be small enough for real intimacy and stable enough for sustained relationship. The group has to be willing to refuse the performance that pastoral culture often requires. The group is rare. The group is necessary.

A marriage that is honored as a marriage. The marriage cannot bear the structural weight of being the pastor's only support. The marriage can, when honored as a marriage rather than as a ministry partnership, be one substantial relationship of support among several. The pastors I know who have sustained healthy marriages across long ministries have generally been the pastors who refused to let the structural pressure collapse the marriage into the ministry, and who built other support relationships precisely to take some of the weight off the marriage.

A personal devotional practice that is not used in sermon preparation. The pastor needs scripture for himself, regularly, slowly, with attention to what God is saying to him in the reading rather than what he can extract for Sunday morning. The practice is part of how the pastoral interior is replenished rather than only depleted.

## The verdict

The pastor's loneliness is the cost of the trust the pastor has been given. The cost is not removable. The cost is, however, distributable — across a spiritual director, a confessor, a pastor's group, a wise spouse, the historic practices the church developed because the people doing this work have always known what the work costs.

Find your people. Carry the weight together. The aloneness will kill you. The carrying together will not.

I am writing this for the pastor who has been carrying versions of this loneliness without quite being able to name it. The naming is the first step. The structures I have described are available, and the building of them is part of what makes long-term pastoral work sustainable. The pastors I have known who have built these structures have generally been able to remain in the work across decades. The pastors who have not have generally been the ones who, eventually, left.

You do not have to leave. You also do not have to keep carrying this alone. The historic church has, for centuries, provided the structures the contemporary American pastor has often been doing without. Recover them. Begin with one. Find a spiritual director this month. The recovery is yours to do. The recovery is what the next twenty years of your ministry depend on.
