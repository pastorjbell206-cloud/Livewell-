Socrates said the unexamined life is not worth living. He was, of course, speaking about the examined life in philosophical terms — the life held up against questions of virtue and the good [cite — confirm]. The church inherited this instinct through Augustine, who turned it inward in a way that Socrates never quite did: not simply the life examined for virtue, but the soul examined in the presence of God, held against what is actually true about the human condition and what is actually true about the one who made it.

The *Confessions* are precisely this — not an autobiography but a sustained act of self-examination conducted in direct address to God. Augustine does not flatter himself. He does not curate his failures for dramatic effect. He looks at what was actually happening in his interior life, names it with precision, and holds it before God without flinching [cite — confirm]. The Confessions are not a genre exercise. They are the result of a man who believed that knowing himself honestly before God was not separate from knowing God.

The pastoral vocation requires this. Not as a personality preference or a temperamental gift, but as a structural necessity. The pastor who stands in a pulpit every week and speaks about sin, grace, pride, and self-deception had better have some actual experience of examining his own interior. Otherwise the sermon is not prophetic — it is merely critical. It has the form of truth-telling without the weight of someone who has actually looked at himself and been undone by what he found.

I have been in that pulpit. I know what it feels like to preach about pride from a defensive position, to speak about self-deception while practicing it with considerable skill. The congregation sensed something, even if they could not name it. What they were sensing was the absence of self-implication — a sermon that pointed outward with precision but never turned inward with honesty.

The examined life, for a pastor, requires regular rhythms of deliberate reflection. Not the passive accumulation of experience over decades, which can happen without any real examination at all — merely aging — but the intentional, repeated practice of asking: What am I actually doing here? What are my real motives? Where am I avoiding what God is showing me? Where has my leadership served my own needs more than the congregation's?

These are questions that require silence and some willingness to be surprised by your own answers. I have found them disturbing more often than reassuring. The examined life is not a comfortable practice. It is the practice of a person who believes that being known — truly known, including in the dark corners — is safer than being hidden. Psalm 139 is not primarily a comfort. It is a statement about the impossibility of successful concealment: "You have searched me and known me" (Psalm 139:1). The question is not whether God sees the interior life accurately. The question is whether the pastor himself is willing to look at what God sees.

The practical forms this takes will vary. A yearly retreat of several days alone with God and a journal. A regular meeting with a spiritual director who is not afraid to ask hard questions. A practice of reading your own sermons back to yourself and asking what they reveal about the state of your soul, not just the quality of your exegesis. What matters is not the form but the practice — the deliberate, recurrent decision to hold your interior life up to the light.

The pastor who takes this seriously will become, over time, a different kind of leader. Not wiser in the management sense. Wiser in the biblical sense — the person who knows the difference between appearance and reality, who is not easily deceived about his own motivations, who leads from a place of honesty rather than projection. The congregation needs that man. They need someone who has looked at himself and not run from what he saw.

The ministry that lasts is the ministry built by a pastor who kept examining himself with the same unflinching honesty he brought to the text.

Start there. Return there. Do not stop.
