In the ancient Near East, a king would place images of himself throughout the territories he governed. The image — the tselem — functioned as a claim of sovereignty: this territory belongs to the one whose image stands here. Genesis 1:26–27 enters this cultural world and overturns its logic entirely. The whole earth is God's territory. God's image — imago Dei — is not placed in stone in strategic locations. It is breathed into every human being, without qualification, without exception, without condition.

This is not primarily a statement about human dignity. It is primarily a statement about God's sovereignty. And its implications run farther than most preaching ever reaches.

If every human being bears the image of God, then every act of violence against a human being is an act against the image of the One whose claim extends over all creation. Genesis 9:6 makes this explicit in the context of law: "Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed, for God made man in his own image." The gravity of the crime is not merely that a life is ended. It is that an image of God has been destroyed. The prohibition against murder is grounded in theology, not merely in social utility.

Expand that logic. Not just murder. What about the slower violences — the poverty that shortens lives by decades, the incarceration that strips agency and dignity across years, the medical neglect of communities without insurance, the housing conditions that expose children to lead and mold and chronic stress? If these things diminish or destroy the people who bear God's image, what does the imago Dei doctrine demand of us in response?

I have said "every person bears the image of God" in sermons. I have not always followed that sentence to where it leads. It is easier to affirm the doctrine than to reckon with what the doctrine requires of me when I pass the man sleeping in the parking garage or vote on policies that will affect the conditions of people I will never meet. The doctrine is not comfortable if you take it seriously. It is costly. It demands that people I would rather categorize as problems be seen instead as bearers of the same image I claim for myself.

The steel-man of the objection: doctrine and policy are different things, and imago Dei does not settle contested questions about how best to structure social arrangements. True. The doctrine does not automatically generate a legislative program. But it does generate certain prohibitions. It prohibits treating any human being as less than the bearer of divine significance. It prohibits arrangements that systematically discard people — through poverty, through dehumanizing institutional treatment, through the legal stripping of legal standing — as if their image-bearing were conditional on their social usefulness.

The tradition has understood this broadly. John Calvin argued that when we meet a neighbor in need, we have no business asking whether they deserve our help, because we see on their face the image of God and that is sufficient basis for action. [cite — confirm] In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus extends the logic in a direction that should still disturb us: what you do to the least of these — the hungry, the stranger, the naked, the sick, the imprisoned — you do to him (Matthew 25:40). The image of God in the vulnerable is not a metaphor. It is identification. This is the one I am in.

Disability. Age. Poverty. Race. Incarceration. Immigration status. The imago Dei doctrine places every human being beyond the categories we use to justify dismissal. It requires us to look at the person we would rather not see and acknowledge that God's claim on them is prior to and more durable than every category we have constructed to manage our obligations toward them.

That is not comfortable theology. It was not meant to be. The text does not invite us to feel good about the dignity of people like us. It demands that we see the dignity of people we have sorted into categories of lesser concern.

What it demands next is the harder question. One the doctrine will not let us defer indefinitely.
