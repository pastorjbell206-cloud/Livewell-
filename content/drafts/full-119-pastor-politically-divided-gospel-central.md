The congregation I stood before had people who despised each other's politics. Not quietly, the way polite people manage these things — but openly, with the specific contempt that comes from years of cable news and algorithm-fed outrage. They were in the same pews. They took communion from the same table. And after the service, some of them could barely bring themselves to shake hands.

I am not describing a church that failed. I am describing almost every church in America right now.

The standard pastoral advice is to avoid politics from the pulpit, to stay in the safe center, to speak in broad enough terms that no one feels targeted. I understand the impulse. I have felt it. But that posture has a cost that pastors rarely name: it trains the congregation to believe the Gospel is compatible with any political allegiance, that following Jesus requires no specific reordering of loyalties, that the Kingdom of God is a spiritual reality with no purchase on Monday morning. The congregation that hears that for long enough will eventually organize its life around something else. And in this cultural moment, that something else is politics.

Here is the harder truth: the politically divided congregation is often a symptom of Gospel-thin preaching, not a management problem to be handled with careful neutrality.

This is not a comfortable thing to say, and I include myself in it. There have been seasons of my ministry when I softened the political edges of the Gospel to keep the peace — when I knew the text demanded more than I was willing to preach. That is not neutrality. That is cowardice dressed in pastoral language.

The Gospel is not politically neutral. The Kingdom of God claims the whole of life — not to endorse a party platform, but to relativize every earthly claim to ultimate loyalty. Jesus said to render to Caesar what is Caesar's and to God what is God's (Matthew 22:21), and the dangerous word in that sentence is *God's* — because everything ultimately belongs to God, including the political allegiances we treat as identity markers. The church that preaches this clearly will frustrate people on both sides. That is, historically speaking, a reliable sign of faithfulness.

What this requires in practice is not the avoidance of political topics but the consistent proclamation of what Scripture actually says about power, about the poor, about the neighbor, about the stranger, about the use of wealth — and the willingness to let those texts land where they land, regardless of who is sitting in the room. A congregation that has heard the Sermon on the Mount preached straight for five consecutive years will not agree on every policy question, but it will have a common framework that transcends party. That framework is the work. Building it is slower than conflict management. It is the only thing that lasts.

You will lose some people. Let me be honest about that. Some people have fused their political identity so completely with their spiritual identity that any prophetic challenge to one feels like an assault on the other. You cannot pastor that out of them in a single series. Sometimes you preach the text, they experience it as taking a side, and they leave. That is painful. It is also not a failure of your leadership. It is the normal cost of preaching the Kingdom in a nation that wants a chaplain.

What you can do is this: name the Gospel explicitly and often, call the congregation to primary loyalty to Christ above every other allegiance, and then trust the Spirit to do what only the Spirit can do. Model across-the-aisle relationships in your own friendships. Preach the texts that make conservatives uncomfortable and the texts that make progressives uncomfortable, in the same year, without flinching at either.

The congregation that emerges from that kind of preaching will still disagree about policy. But they will know what they share is more permanent than what divides them.

That is not political neutrality. That is the Gospel. They are not the same thing.
