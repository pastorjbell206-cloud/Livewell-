Charles Spurgeon preached to six thousand people at the Metropolitan Tabernacle on Sunday mornings and spent significant portions of his life unable to get out of bed [cite — confirm]. He wrote about his depression with the specificity of a man who was not performing vulnerability for an audience — he wrote about it because it was real, because it was sometimes disabling, because it returned when he had done nothing to invite it back. He called it a "fainting fit." He did not call it a faith problem. He knew better.

The tradition of preachers who have wrestled with what we now call depression is not a footnote. It is a primary document. Spurgeon. William Cowper. Martin Luther, who described periods of such darkness that he believed himself abandoned by God. The prophet Elijah, after the defeat of the prophets of Baal, under the juniper tree in the wilderness, asking God to take his life (1 Kings 19:4). The author of Psalm 88, who ends without resolution, without the turn back to praise, simply: "darkness is my closest friend." These are not spiritual failures. They are the pastoral record of people who took God seriously enough to tell the truth about what it costs.

The theological confusion that identifies depression with lack of faith is not an ancient confusion. It is largely a modern one, and it has a specific genealogy in the American prosperity theology and the broader cultural narrative that equates Christian life with emotional wellness. If God is good and you belong to him, you should feel good. Depression, in this frame, is diagnostic — evidence of some sin unrepented, some prayer insufficiently urgent, some trust inadequately formed. This is not the theology of the Psalms. It is not the theology of Paul, who wrote from prison, who had a thorn in the flesh that was not removed despite repeated petition (2 Corinthians 12:7-9). It is a theology of the Sunday morning marketing department.

Depression in ministry is not rare. The specific pressures of pastoral life — sustained exposure to grief and trauma, the isolation of visible leadership, the identity entanglement with congregational performance, the absence of genuine community — create conditions in which depression is not a surprising outcome. They are contributing conditions to a complex clinical reality that also involves biology, family history, and the kind of neurological factors that do not respond to correct doctrine. The pastor who tells his depressed congregant that "prayer changes things" without also referring them to professional care and being open to medication has confused the means of grace with the exhaustion of them.

I am not arguing that faith is irrelevant to depression. I am arguing that faith and depression are not opposites, and that the equation of depression with faithlessness is both biblically wrong and pastorally destructive. A man who is already struggling to believe that God loves him does not need a theology that makes his struggle evidence of God's absence. He needs the testimony of Spurgeon, and Elijah, and Jeremiah — men who descended into darkness while God was still present, who were sustained through the darkness rather than rescued from it.

The practical reality for pastors dealing with depression is that the professional mask makes it harder to name. You continue to function, continue to preach, continue to pray for others' healing while secretly doubting whether healing is available for you. The congregation cannot tell. The elders cannot tell. And the shame that accompanies the depression — the certainty that a man of faith should not feel this — keeps you from seeking the help that would make naming it possible.

Depression does not require a crisis of faith to produce. It requires a brain, a body, a specific set of stressors, and sometimes a family history. Treating it requires the same range of means you would bring to any other medical reality: clinical support, honest conversation, sometimes medication, always community, and the specific grace of people around you who will not interpret your struggle as disqualifying.

It is not a faith problem. It never was.

The prophets knew. The preacher knows. Now act on it.
