Try to find the solo Christian in the New Testament. Go looking. Search the letters and the gospels for the believer who follows Jesus privately, accountable to no one, gathered with no one, a faith of one. You will not find him. Not because the writers forbade him — they barely thought to forbid what they could not imagine. The category does not exist. There is no Greek word in the entire New Testament for a Christian who belongs to no church, for the same reason there is no word in an anatomy textbook for a hand that has decided it would rather not be attached to an arm.

I want to make this argument gently, because the people most likely to be reading it have usually been burned by people who made it harshly. You have been told you *must* go to church in a tone that made church sound like a debt collector. Set that tone down. I am not here to collect. I am here to describe something you may not have been shown — that the loneliness you have felt in your private faith is not a personal failing, and not a sign you're doing it wrong. It is the predictable ache of a thing that was built for two or more, running on one.

**Here is the claim, and I'll spend the rest of the essay being fair to the version of you that resists it: the New Testament knows no solitary Christian — not as a rule it enforces, but as a reality it cannot picture — because in the mind of the first believers, to belong to Christ was to be grafted into a body, and a severed member is not a smaller Christian. It is a member in trouble.**

## The metaphor that was not a metaphor

Begin with the image Paul reached for when he wanted to describe what a church is, because he reached for it more than any other, and we have softened it into a poster.

*Just as a body, though one, has many parts, but all its many parts form one body, so it is with Christ* (1 Corinthians 12:12). We read that and nod. Nice picture. Teamwork. But Paul is not handing us a motivational image about cooperation. He is making a claim about what kind of thing a Christian *is.* He goes on: the eye cannot say to the hand, *I don't need you.* The head cannot say to the feet, *I have no need of you* (1 Corinthians 12:21). And then the line we skip: *If one part suffers, every part suffers with it; if one part is honored, every part rejoices with it* (1 Corinthians 12:26).

Sit with what Paul is actually saying. He is not saying the church *functions like* a body when it cooperates well. He is saying you, the individual believer, are a body *part.* A hand. An eye. A foot. And the defining fact about a body part is that it has no independent life. A hand on a table is not a more autonomous hand. It is a dying hand. Its wholeness is not located in itself. Its wholeness is located in its attachment.

This is why the question *can I be a Christian alone* would have confused Paul the way *can I be a hand without an arm* confuses a surgeon. The hand can be detached. It just cannot stay a hand for long.

## What the first Christians actually did when they believed

The clearest answer to "can you do this alone" is to watch what the people did the very day the church was born, because they tell you what they thought belief *was* by what they immediately did with it.

Three thousand people believe in a single afternoon in Acts 2. What happens next is not three thousand individuals going home to pray privately. *They devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and to fellowship, to the breaking of bread and to prayer* (Acts 2:42). And then the description thickens: they sold property and shared it, they ate in each other's homes, they met daily, they had — and the word is startling — *all things in common* (Acts 2:44–47). Belief, for these people, did not produce a private spirituality that occasionally socialized. It produced a shared life so immediate and so total that the believing and the belonging were the same motion.

We have nearly reversed this. We treat the believing as the real thing and the belonging as the optional follow-up — first you get saved, privately, and then, if you have the energy and find a church you like, you join one. To the people in Acts 2 this would have read backward. There was no gap between trusting Jesus and joining his people, because joining his people was *how you trusted Jesus.* The fellowship was not the reward for faith. It was the shape faith took the moment it became real.

The experiment has been run, in other words. We know what conviction-plus-community does, because we watched three thousand people do it in a single chapter. We are the ones running the new experiment — conviction alone — and we are running it without a control group, on ourselves, with our own souls as the variable.

## The family you didn't choose

Here is where a New Testament scholar named Joseph Hellerman has done more to help me than almost anyone, because he recovered a word we have sentimentalized into nothing.

In *When the Church Was a Family* (2009), Hellerman shows that the earliest Christians used family language for the church not as warm decoration but as their primary social reality. They called each other brother and sister, and in the ancient Mediterranean world, the sibling bond was the strongest bond there was — stronger, in many ways, than marriage, because you could divorce a spouse but your brother was your brother for life. When the New Testament calls fellow believers your brothers and sisters, it is not offering you a friendly vibe. It is reassigning your family. It is telling you that the people in that room have a claim on you, and you on them, of the kind you do not get to walk away from when it stops being convenient.

This is the part our individualism cannot stand, and the part the wounded most need to hear honestly. A family is not a thing you consume. It is a thing you are *stuck with*, gloriously and impossibly. You cannot fire your sister for being annoying. You cannot decline your brother because his politics embarrass you. And the modern church — this is our sin, not yours — let people treat it like a service precisely by stripping out the family claim, so that when the service disappointed, leaving cost nothing. We made church a thing you attend instead of a people you belong to, and then we were shocked that people left it like a restaurant with a bad review.

You may have left a service. I understand leaving a service. But Hellerman would gently ask you whether you have ever actually been offered a family — and whether the loneliness in your faith right now is the specific loneliness of someone who has a Father and a Lord but no brothers and sisters in the room.

## Bonhoeffer, who learned this with the secret police outside

Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote the most piercing book on Christian community while training pastors in an underground seminary the Nazi state was trying to shut down. *Life Together* (1939) is not the soft book the title suggests. It is the testimony of a man who knew exactly how fragile and how necessary the body is, because his was being hunted.

Bonhoeffer says a thing that I have never been able to shake: that the Christian needs other Christians because of Jesus Christ — that I cannot reliably speak the word of God to myself. My own heart is biased toward me. It excuses what should be confessed and condemns what should be forgiven. When I tell myself the gospel alone, I am both the preacher and the only one checking whether the preaching is true, and I will, over time, preach myself a gospel shaped suspiciously like my own preferences. But when a brother or sister says it to me — *your sins are forgiven, Christ is risen, you are not alone* — it comes from outside me, with the authority of a word I did not author and cannot revise to suit myself.

This is the deepest reason the solo Christian struggles, and it has nothing to do with rules. It is that a faith with no outside voice slowly becomes a conversation you are having with yourself. The most dangerous heresy is never the dramatic one. It is the slow drift you cannot detect because there is no one beside you who would notice the drift and say, gently, *that isn't him. That's you.*

Bonhoeffer also said the opposite, which is why I trust him: that the one who cannot be alone should beware of community, and the one who cannot stand community should beware of being alone. He was not romantic about it. Community wounded him; he names the wounding. He simply refused to conclude from the wound that the body was optional.

## The objection I take most seriously

There is one response to all of this that I respect more than any other, and I will not pretend it away. It goes like this: *the church in Acts 2 sounds nothing like the church that hurt me. You are describing a family that sold its property to feed each other. I attended a building that took my money and ignored my name. Don't tell me to rejoin a body when what I experienced was a business.*

That objection is not faithless. It is accurate. And the honest answer is that the gap between the church Paul describes and the church many of you experienced is enormous, and the gap is not your fault. Much of what calls itself church in this country has drifted so far from the body in Acts 2 that the word barely connects the two. You were promised a family and sold a service, and when the service disappointed, you were blamed for leaving — which is exactly backward, because a family you cannot leave and a service you can, and they handed you the second while using the language of the first.

So I am not arguing that the buildings you left were the body. Some of them were a long way from it. What I am arguing is narrower and harder to dodge: the body is real, the New Testament insists on it, and the failures of the counterfeit do not abolish the original any more than counterfeit money proves there is no treasury. The fact that you were handed a fake does not mean there is nothing real to be handed. It means you were robbed, and being robbed is a reason for grief and caution — but it is not, by itself, evidence that the thing stolen never existed.

The solo Christian, in other words, is often not someone who rejected the body. They are someone who was shown a forgery, recognized it as a forgery, and concluded — understandably — that the whole category was fake. I am asking you to consider that you recognized the forgery *because* you know, somewhere, what the real thing is supposed to look like. The disappointment is itself a kind of evidence. You cannot be disappointed by a counterfeit unless you carry, somewhere, the image of the genuine article it failed to be.

## The building, the body, and the house

Paul has one more image, and it answers the part of you that wants to believe your private faith is structurally fine.

He tells the Ephesians they are *no longer foreigners and strangers, but fellow citizens with God's people and also members of his household* — and then he changes the metaphor mid-sentence into a building, *joined together* and *rising to become a holy temple*, *built together to become a dwelling in which God lives by his Spirit* (Ephesians 2:19–22). Read the verbs again. *Joined together. Built together.* A brick by itself is not a small temple. It is a brick. Its whole purpose waits on the other bricks, and the mortar, and the joining. A brick in a field is not expressing its brick-nature more purely by being unencumbered. It is just a brick the builder hasn't placed yet, or a brick that fell out of the wall.

I think some of you fell out of the wall. You were not thrown — you fell, in the chaos, when the wall was shaking, and no one caught you, and you have lived in the field so long that the field feels like home. I am not going to tell you the wall that dropped you was a good wall. Some of them weren't. But I will tell you what a brick is for, because no one may have told you: you were shaped to bear weight and to have weight set on you, to hold and be held, to be part of something that houses the presence of God. You cannot do that on the ground. Not because it's against the rules. Because it's against your nature as the kind of thing you are.

So — can you be a Christian on your own? I will give you the honest answer, which is neither the harsh one nor the comfortable one. You can believe alone. People do. The thief on the cross believed alone and Jesus received him without a membership card. God is not so small that he loses track of the isolated. But *believing alone* and *being a Christian alone* are not the same sentence, because being a Christian is not finally a belief you hold. It is a body you have been made part of, a family you have been adopted into, a brick set into a wall that holds the weight of God.

You can hold the faith by yourself. I just don't think it can hold you. And the day it can't — the day the wind comes and there's no one beside you saying *that's him, not you* — is the day you find out what the body was always quietly doing, by being the thing that wasn't there.
