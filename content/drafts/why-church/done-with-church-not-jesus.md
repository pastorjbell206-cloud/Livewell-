There is a sentence people say to me in coffee shops and parking lots, lowered, almost confessional, as if they are admitting to something shameful. *I still believe. I just can't do the building anymore.* They say it the way you'd admit to a debt. They expect me to argue. They have braced for the argument, because the last time they raised it someone handed them a verse about forsaking the assembly and changed the subject.

I am not going to argue with you. Not yet. First I want to tell you that you are not rare, you are not the first, and you are not, by saying that sentence, doing anything that puts you outside the reach of the God you still love.

In 2023 two researchers named Jim Davis and Michael Graham published the largest study ever done on this exact phenomenon. They called it *The Great Dechurching*. Their finding was not that America stopped believing. Their finding was stranger and harder. Around forty million American adults have stopped attending church in the last quarter-century — more people leaving in a single generation than were added in the First Great Awakening, the Second Great Awakening, and the Billy Graham crusades combined. And here is the part nobody expected: a large share of them did not deconvert. They did not become atheists. They still pray. They still believe Jesus rose. They simply stopped going, and most of them did not leave in a blaze of argument. They drifted. They moved cities. They had a baby. They got burned. Sunday came and they did not, and then it kept not happening, and one day they realized the not-going had hardened into a life.

**You are not a problem the church needs to solve. You are a person the church mostly failed to notice leaving. And the question in front of you is not whether your faith is real — it plainly is — but whether a faith this real was ever meant to be carried alone.**

That is the whole essay. Everything after this is me earning it.

## The most common spiritual position in America

Start with how ordinary you are, because the loneliness of your position is half its weight.

For most of Christian history, "I believe but I don't belong" was nearly unthinkable. Not because people were holier, but because faith was social before it was anything else. You were born into a people, baptized into a community, and your belief and your belonging arrived in the same package. The idea that you could hold the one while setting down the other would have struck a believer in the year 400, or 1400, as roughly like saying you loved your family but had decided to live as an only child.

What changed is not your sincerity. What changed is the air. Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), described the slow shift from a world where faith was assumed to a world where faith is one option among many, chosen and held by the individual, defended by the individual, and — this is the crucial turn — increasingly *located* in the individual. We did not just make belief optional. We privatized it. We moved it indoors, into the self, where it became a matter between you and God with the door closed. And once faith lives entirely indoors, the church starts to look like an accessory. A nice add-on for people who like that sort of thing. Optional, the way a gym membership is optional for someone who has decided to run outside.

So when you say *I still believe, I just can't do the building*, you are not being rebellious. You are being a perfectly formed product of the last three hundred years. The whole culture taught you that the realest part of faith is the part inside your chest, and the building is just where some people go to express it. If that is true, then leaving the building costs you nothing essential.

The trouble is that it isn't quite true. And some part of you already suspects it, or you would not lower your voice when you say the sentence.

## Why you left is not the same as why you stayed away

Be honest about two different things, because we tend to collapse them.

There is the reason you left. And there is the reason you have stayed gone. They are rarely the same, and confusing them keeps a lot of people stuck.

The reason you left might be a real wound. A pastor who lied. A community that closed ranks around an abuser and against a victim. A church that loved its politics more than its people, or its building fund more than its widows. If that is your story, I will not rush you past it, and a later essay in this set is written entirely for you. Some of you left for reasons that were not betrayal at all — a move, an illness, a season of exhaustion, a Sunday you missed that turned into a year. And some of you, if you are willing to be searched, left because the convenience of staying gone is simply enormous. Sunday morning is the last unclaimed acreage in an overcommitted life. The bed is warm. The coffee is yours. Nobody asks anything of you. That is not a sin worth shaming. It is just worth naming, because it is a different thing than a wound, and it asks for a different answer.

The reason you stay away is almost always inertia plus relief. And inertia plus relief, repeated for enough Sundays, will hollow out a faith without ever once attacking it directly. It does not argue you out of believing. It just slowly removes everything that fed the belief, until one day you notice the fire is mostly memory.

## What Jesus did when the crowds left him

Here is a scene that should comfort you and unsettle you in the same breath.

In John 6, Jesus says something so hard that the text reports the result plainly: many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him (John 6:66). This was a mass dechurching, two thousand years early. The crowd thinned. The institution of the moment — such as it was — collapsed around him. And Jesus turns to the Twelve, the ones still standing in the emptied field, and asks a question with no defensiveness in it at all: *Do you want to leave too?*

Peter's answer is not triumphant. It is almost desperate, and that is why I trust it. *Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life* (John 6:68). Notice what Peter does not say. He does not say *the community is so warm.* He does not say *I love the singing.* He says, in effect, *I have nowhere else to take this. There is no other door.* Peter stays not because the institution was working but because the Person was irreplaceable.

That is your sentence too, isn't it. *I still believe. I can't do the building.* You have kept Peter's clause — *to whom shall we go* — and set down the field he was standing in when he said it. And I think the reason your faith feels lonelier than it used to is that Peter never said that line alone. He said it standing next to eleven other people who had also just decided not to leave. The staying was personal. It was not solitary.

## The thing the building was carrying that you can't see yet

Now the gentle turn, and I mean gentle.

You experienced church as a place you went to receive something — a sermon, a song, a feeling, a service to consume. And when the service got bad, or the feeling went cold, or the people hurt you, it was entirely rational to stop consuming a product that had gone sour. I would have stopped too.

But the church was never primarily a service you attended. It was a body you belonged to, and bodies do something for their members that the members cannot do for themselves. Hebrews names it without sentimentality: *Let us consider how we may spur one another on toward love and good deeds, not giving up meeting together, as some are in the habit of doing, but encouraging one another* (Hebrews 10:24–25). Read it slowly and notice the verbs. *Spur. Encourage.* These are not things you do to yourself. You cannot spur yourself on any more than you can tickle yourself. The writer is not scolding people for skipping a meeting. He is describing a kind of strengthening that only happens between people, the way a coal stays hot in the pile and goes cold pulled out alone.

The faith you are carrying by yourself right now is a coal you pulled out of the fire. It is still glowing. I am not denying the glow. I am telling you what happens to coals over time, away from the others, and I am telling you because I would rather say it now than meet you in five years when the glow has gone gray and you can't remember why it went.

## The slow cost no one warns you about

Let me describe what I have watched happen, not to frighten you, but because no one described it to me and I wish they had.

The first year out is almost always fine. Better than fine. The relief is real and the freedom is sweet. You sleep in. You take the morning slow. Your faith, unhooked from a disappointing institution, feels purer, more yours, less cluttered by other people's noise. You read Scripture when you want to. You pray on a walk. You tell yourself, honestly, that you are closer to God now than you were in the pew, and a lot of the time you are right, because the pew had become a performance and the walk is sincere.

The second year is quieter. You notice you read less than you meant to. The walk-prayers get shorter and then occasional. Nothing dramatic happens. There is no crisis of faith, no argument that knocks you over. There is just the slow withdrawal of the thousand small pressures that used to keep faith in the foreground — the Sunday rhythm, the friend who would ask how you were really doing, the song that put words to a thing you could not say, the sermon that interrupted the story you were telling yourself about your own life. None of those were the gospel. But all of them were scaffolding, and faith, it turns out, is a thing that needs scaffolding, not because it is weak but because it is alive, and living things grow toward the things that feed them.

By the third or fourth year, for a lot of people, what is left is a warm memory of having believed, narrated in the past tense without their ever deciding to change tenses. They have not rejected anything. They could still tell you, sincerely, that they believe Jesus rose. It has simply stopped costing them anything, stopped shaping a Tuesday, stopped being the thing the rest of life bends around. The fire did not go out in a storm. It went out the way fires go out when no one tends them and no one else is keeping coals beside yours.

I am not saying this is your story. I am saying I have watched it be too many people's story for me to stay quiet about the pattern, and that the pattern is not a moral failure. It is physics. A coal alone goes cold. That is not a verdict on the coal.

## What you actually want

I think the dechurched but believing — and that is most of you reading this — are not running from God at all. I think a lot of you are running toward the God you found, and away from the people who handled him carelessly. That is not a contradiction. That is grief.

And there is one verse for you that I have watched do more work than any argument. David, hunted, exhausted, sleeping in caves, writes down the one thing he still wants when everything else has been stripped away: *One thing I ask from the Lord, this only do I seek: that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life, to gaze on the beauty of the Lord* (Psalm 27:4). The man who wrote that had every reason to be done with the institutional religion of his day. It was run, much of the time, by people trying to kill him. And the one thing he asks for is not relief from the people. It is the house. Not the building as real estate. The house as the place where the beauty of God is gazed upon in company.

That ache in you — the one that makes you lower your voice and say *I still believe* — that is not the residue of an old habit. That is Psalm 27 still running in your bones. You want to dwell in the house. You have just never been shown a house worth dwelling in, or you were shown one and it burned, and you have confused the fire with the architecture.

I am not asking you to walk back into the room that hurt you. I am asking you to consider that the thing you actually long for was never the room. It was the gazing, and David knew you cannot do it alone, because he wrote that line surrounded by the men who had refused to leave him in the cave.

You are done with church. I understand. I have been done with it too, and I am a pastor.

But you are not done with Jesus. And he has never once, in two thousand years, handed himself out one isolated soul at a time. He comes with a body attached. That is not the fine print. That is the gift. The question is not whether your lonely faith is real. It is real. The question is how long a real thing can stay alive in a field that everyone else has left — and whether the ache you keep lowering your voice to admit is, in fact, the oldest prayer in the book, asking to come home.
