> [PERSONAL STORY — James to supply: a moment you witnessed the church wound someone — a person who came to you, a situation you saw mishandled — and what it did to your understanding of what the institution is capable of. No private family scenes; pastoral observation only. Place it after "the wound is not your weakness" below.]

If you are reading this, there is a real chance you have a specific face in your mind. A pastor. An elder. A small group leader. A whole congregation that closed around something it should have exposed. The wound has a name and a date and a smell, the particular smell of the room where it happened, and you have spent years being told — directly or by implication — that the wound is your problem. That you are bitter. That you need to forgive and move on. That you left because you couldn't handle accountability, or because you wanted to sin, or because you were never really one of us.

I want to say something I am afraid the church has not said to you nearly enough, and I want to say it before I say anything else, because everything else depends on it being true.

What happened to you was real. Your anger is not your faithlessness. And the God you are afraid you have offended by leaving is, on the matter of what was done to you, angrier than you are.

**The church can be the thing that hurts you. Scripture does not flinch from this — it says it more violently than you would dare to. And the path back from church hurt does not run through pretending it didn't happen. It runs through a God who hates what was done to you, and through the hard, slow work of learning the difference between the bride and the men who betrayed her in her name.**

## The shepherds who ate the sheep

You may have been handed a Bible full of green pastures and gentle shepherds. You were not handed Ezekiel 34, and someone should have handed it to you years ago.

Listen to how God talks about religious leaders who use the people instead of feeding them. *Woe to you shepherds of Israel who only take care of yourselves. Should not shepherds take care of the flock? You eat the curds, clothe yourselves with the wool and slaughter the choice animals, but you do not take care of the flock. You have not strengthened the weak or healed the sick or bound up the injured. You have ruled them harshly and brutally* (Ezekiel 34:2–4). This is God speaking. This is not a disgruntled ex-member writing an angry post. This is the voice of God describing leaders who fattened themselves on the people they were supposed to protect, and the verb that follows is not gentle. *I am against the shepherds and will hold them accountable for my flock* (Ezekiel 34:10).

Read that and let it land. The thing you have felt — the fury that someone in God's name used God's people for their own appetite — is not your bitterness corrupting your faith. It is the echo of God's own fury running through you. You did not invent that anger. You inherited it from the heart of God, who looked at the same kind of leader and said *I am against them.* When you left a church that protected a predator, or covered a fraud, or crushed the weak to preserve its image, you were not abandoning God. In some real sense you were taking God's side against people who had stopped being on it.

## Jesus and the millstone

We have a sentimental Jesus in this country, and the sentimental Jesus is useless to the wounded, because the sentimental Jesus would never say what the real one said.

*If anyone causes one of these little ones — those who believe in me — to stumble, it would be better for them to have a large millstone hung around their neck and to be drowned in the depths of the sea* (Matthew 18:6). The "little ones" are not only children, though they are not less than children. They are the vulnerable, the new, the trusting, the ones with their guard down because they had been told this was a safe place. And Jesus — gentle Jesus, meek and mild — looks at the person who exploited that trust and says the kindest thing that could happen to them would be drowning. A millstone is the heavy stone a donkey turns to grind grain. He is describing being dragged to the bottom of the sea by a weight no swimmer could fight.

I have heard people apologize for being angry about their church hurt. I want to gently take the apology away from them. The anger is not the problem. The anger is, in many cases, the most spiritually accurate response in the room — closer to the heart of Christ than the forced smiles of the people who told you to let it go. Jesus did not tell the little ones to let it go. He picked up a millstone.

## The friend, not the stranger

There is a particular grief in church hurt that secular language cannot reach, and the Psalms reach it exactly.

*If an enemy were insulting me, I could endure it; if a foe were rising against me, I could hide. But it is you, a man like myself, my companion, my close friend, with whom I once enjoyed sweet fellowship at the house of God* (Psalm 55:12–14). This is the unique cruelty of being wounded by the church. It does not hurt like an attack from outside. It hurts like a betrayal from inside the family. The pain you feel is sharper than the pain of the world's hostility precisely because it came from the people who broke bread with you, who prayed over you, who you trusted with the softest part of yourself. *We used to walk to the house of God together.* That is the wound. The world's blows we can brace for. It is the friend's hand we never see coming.

David does not suppress this. He does not spiritualize it. He writes it down and puts it in the songbook of the people of God, which tells you something we have forgotten: lament — naming the betrayal to God, out loud, in detail — is not the opposite of faith. It is a form of it. The Psalms are full of people telling God exactly how badly God's own people behaved. If David can put it in the hymnal, you can say it at your kitchen table without fearing you have left the faith. You are speaking the native language of the wounded faithful, who have always been allowed to say the worst of it to God directly.

## What the abuse actually was — naming it precisely

Here is where two careful guides have helped me name the thing instead of waving at it, because vague language about "church hurt" lets the real mechanisms hide.

Diane Langberg, a psychologist who has spent decades with the survivors of abuse inside Christian institutions, wrote *Redeeming Power* (2020) to do one thing: to show how power, which God gives to be spent in service, gets inverted into a weapon. She names the pattern plainly. Power was meant to protect the vulnerable; in a corrupted system it protects the institution from the vulnerable instead. The victim becomes the threat — not because they did anything, but because their truth endangers the reputation of the thing. And so the machinery turns on them. They are managed, doubted, relabeled as divisive, asked to forgive in a way that means *be quiet.* Langberg's gift is to insist that this is not a relational misunderstanding. It is an abuse of power, and God, who entrusted that power, takes its abuse with deadly seriousness.

Chuck DeGroat, in *When Narcissism Comes to Church* (2020), names another piece: the way certain personalities are drawn to religious leadership precisely because it offers admiration, control, and a stage, all sanctified. He is careful, and I will be careful echoing him — not every difficult leader is a narcissist, and the word is overused. But the pattern he describes is one many of you will recognize with a sick familiarity: the charisma that pulls you in, the image that must be protected at all costs, the way criticism is reframed as a spiritual attack on God's anointed, the slow erosion of your own sense of reality until you wondered if you were the problem after all. You were not crazy. DeGroat's whole point is that the system was designed, often unconsciously, to make you feel crazy so the image could survive. The naming is the beginning of getting your mind back.

I name these two not to hand you ammunition but to hand you precision. *Church hurt* is too soft a phrase for some of what was done. What was done was an abuse of power and, sometimes, the predictable wreckage of a disordered person given a sacred platform. Naming it accurately is not bitterness. It is the first honest thing, and honesty is where healing has to start.

## The bride and the men who sold her name

Now the turn, and I am going to make it as carefully as I know how, because made carelessly it becomes another way to silence you.

There is a difference between the church and the people who hurt you in the church. I know how that can sound — like a dodge, like *not all churches*, like the thing people say to get you back in the building. That is not what I mean, and if anyone uses this distinction to rush you past your wound, they are misusing it.

What I mean is this. Scripture calls the church the bride of Christ. And what was done to you was done by people betraying that bride, trading on her name, wearing her clothes to do the opposite of what she is. When a man defrauds people while wearing a wedding ring, the institution he has dishonored is marriage, and marriage is not the villain — he is. When a man wounds the vulnerable while wearing the name of the church, the church is not the perpetrator. She is, in a real sense, another of his victims. Her name was used. Her trust was spent. Christ, who loves her, watched her name get nailed to the very thing that drove you out.

This distinction does not ask you to go back. It does not ask you to forgive on a timeline. It does not even ask you to stop being angry — you have seen who taught me about the millstone. It asks only this: do not let the men who betrayed her convince you that *they were her.* They were not. They were the shepherds God said he was against. They were the friend at the house of God whose hand David never saw coming. The bride they dishonored is still beloved, still pursued, still — somewhere, in rooms you have not yet found — being the thing they pretended to be.

## On forgiveness, since someone has probably weaponized it against you

I have to say something about forgiveness, because the odds are high that the word has already been used on you as a weapon, and I do not want to add to the pile.

Somewhere in your story, someone probably told you to forgive — and what they meant was *stop talking about it.* They used the holiest word in the Christian vocabulary to ask you to protect the reputation of the people who harmed you. They told you that your refusal to move on was a spiritual problem, that bitterness was eating you, that real Christians forgive and forget. This is one of the most common and most cruel misuses of Scripture I know, and it has driven more people out of the faith than open atheism ever has.

So let me separate some things that the weaponizers deliberately fuse together. Forgiveness is releasing your right to personal vengeance — handing the debt to God, who keeps a better ledger than you ever could, and trusting him to settle it justly. That is real, and it is, eventually, for your own freedom, because carrying the right to vengeance is its own slow poison. But forgiveness is *not* the same as trust. It is not the same as reconciliation. It is not the same as returning to the place that harmed you, or pretending it didn't happen, or staying silent so the institution can protect its image. You can forgive a man and still testify against him. You can forgive a church and never set foot in it again. You can release the debt to God and still tell the truth about what was done, loudly, so it is not done to the next person. The Bible nowhere asks the wounded to protect their abusers with their silence. That is not forgiveness. That is cover-up wearing forgiveness as a costume.

And here is the thing about timing: forgiveness that is forced is not forgiveness at all. It is performance, and God is not interested in the performance. The God who wrote David's lament into the songbook is not standing over you with a stopwatch. He let David rage at the betrayal of a friend without one word of rebuke. He can let you take however long the wound takes. Anyone who tells you the clock is running is reading a different God than the one in the Psalms.

## What I will not tell you

I will not tell you to get over it. I will not tell you the timeline. I will not tell you that the right church is out there and you just have to try again next Sunday, as if your wound were a bad date. And I will not, under any circumstances, tell you that your leaving was the sin, when in your case the leaving may have been the most honest act of your whole life.

Here is what I will tell you. The God who watched it happen was not neutral. He is against the shepherds who did it. He keeps a millstone for the ones who harmed the little ones. He wrote your lament into his own songbook so you would know that saying it out loud is allowed. And the bride whose name they stole is not the one who hurt you — she is grieving with you, because what they did to you they did in her clothes.

You do not have to walk back in tomorrow. You may not be able to for a long time, and a God who keeps millstones is not going to rush a wounded person across a threshold. But do not let the people who hurt you do the one thing they could not do while you were still in the room. Do not let them take Jesus too. They took enough. He is the one thing they were never standing between you and, even when they claimed to be — and he is, right now, more furious about what they did than you have ever let yourself be.
