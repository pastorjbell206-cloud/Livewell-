Ask most Americans, churched or not, what church is *for*, and you will get a version of the same answer. It is where you go to worship. It is where you hear teaching. It is where you get fed, encouraged, recharged for the week. Even people who love their church usually describe it this way — as a place you go to receive a thing. And if that is what church is, then leaving it is the most reasonable decision in the world the moment the thing it provides drops in quality, becomes available elsewhere, or gets ruined by the people running it. You don't keep going to a restaurant after the chef leaves and the food turns. Why would you keep attending a service that has stopped serving you?

The logic is airtight. It is also built on a definition of church that the New Testament never once uses.

**Church was never a religious service you attend. It is a body you are joined to, a family you are adopted into, and a public witness you are made part of — and not one of those three is a thing you can consume, replace, or do alone. We have spent fifty years selling church as a service, and then acting surprised that people treat it like one and leave it like one.**

I want to recover what it is actually for, because I think a lot of you left a thing that was never the real thing — and you deserve to know what the real thing was before you decide you're done with it.

## How we turned a body into a venue

Begin with how the confusion happened, because it was not your fault and it was not an accident.

For most of American history, the church was the center of a community's life because it had to be — it was the place people gathered, the source of their relief work, their schools, their funerals, their belonging. Then, slowly, the culture got richer in options. Entertainment got better. Community moved online. And the American church, watching its crowds thin, made a fateful decision, mostly unconsciously: it would compete. It would become a better product. Better music, better lighting, better preaching, better children's programming, a better experience. We professionalized the stage and we measured success in attendance, and in doing so we trained two generations of Christians to evaluate church exactly the way they evaluate every other experience they buy — by how it makes them feel, and whether the competition has a better one.

Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon saw this coming and named it in *Resident Aliens* (1989). The church, they argued, had made a quiet and disastrous deal: it agreed to become a helpful institution that supports the lives people were already living, rather than a colony of heaven that produces a different kind of life altogether. It became a service provider to the surrounding culture instead of an alternative to it. And a service provider can always be out-served. The moment church exists to meet your felt needs, it is competing with everything else that meets your felt needs, most of which require less of you on a Sunday morning.

So when you found church boring, or redundant, or worse than the podcast you could hear in bed — you were responding rationally to a thing the church had told you it was. The tragedy is that it was lying, mostly to itself, about what it actually is.

## A body, where the parts cannot quit

Paul's most common word for the church is *body*, and Ephesians shows you what the body is for in a way that demolishes the service model entirely.

Christ gave the church its leaders, Paul says — apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors and teachers — *to equip his people for works of service, so that the body of Christ may be built up until we all reach unity in the faith... attaining to the whole measure of the fullness of Christ* (Ephesians 4:11–13). Read who does the work in that sentence. The leaders do not do the ministry. The leaders *equip the people* to do the ministry. The whole point is to get every member functioning, so that the body grows itself. *From him the whole body, joined and held together by every supporting ligament, grows and builds itself up in love, as each part does its work* (Ephesians 4:16).

This is the exact opposite of a service. At a service, a few professionals perform and the audience receives. In a body, there is no audience. There are only members, each one carrying something the others need and cannot get anywhere else. Your absence from a body is not like an empty seat at a concert, which the performers barely notice. It is like a missing ligament. Something does not hold that used to hold. Someone goes uncarried who would have been carried by the part of the body that was you.

When you stopped attending, you experienced it as *I stopped receiving a service.* But from the body's side, what happened was *a ligament went missing, and the rest of us are working harder to hold what you used to hold.* You felt your own loss of supply. You could not feel the body's loss of you. That asymmetry is exactly what the service model hides, and it is why leaving feels so cheap from the inside and costs so much from the outside.

## A family, where you are stuck with each other

The second thing church is for, and the one most foreign to a consumer, is that it is a family — which means you cannot consume it, you can only belong to it.

Acts 2 describes the first church and the description is relentlessly familial. *They devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and to fellowship, to the breaking of bread and to prayer... All the believers were together and had everything in common. They sold property and possessions to give to anyone who had need. Every day they continued to meet together... They broke bread in their homes and ate together with glad and sincere hearts* (Acts 2:42–46). This is not a description of a weekly service. It is a description of a household. People eating in each other's homes. People selling property to cover each other's needs. People so entangled in each other's daily lives that the line between the meeting and the rest of the week dissolved.

You cannot consume a family. You can only be in one. A family does not exist to make you feel good on Sunday; it exists to be the people who show up when the diagnosis comes, who bury your parent with you, who feed you when you cannot get out of bed, who tell you the truth you do not want to hear because they have earned the right to. The reason this kind of church is rare is not that the model failed. It is that we replaced it with the venue, where nobody knows your name and nobody is coming when the diagnosis lands, because you were an attender, not a member of a household.

And here is the part that should make the wounded among you ache rather than scoff: the reason consumer church disappoints so reliably is that it is offering you a family's claim on your heart while delivering a venue's level of commitment. You showed up looking for the household in Acts 2 and got a concert with a sermon. Of course it left you empty. It was the wrong genre. You were starving for a family and they sold you a service, and then they blamed you for not being filled.

## A witness, which only a community can give

The third thing church is for points outward, and Jesus tied it not to our programs but to something only a community can produce.

*A new command I give you: Love one another. As I have loved you, so you must love one another. By this everyone will know that you are my disciples, if you love one another* (John 13:34–35). Notice what Jesus did *not* say. He did not say the world will know you are mine by your individual morality, or your private prayer life, or the depth of your personal convictions. He said the world will know by the love *between* you. The witness is plural. It is the thing visible in the spaces between Christians — how they treat each other, carry each other, forgive each other. A solitary Christian, however sincere, cannot produce this witness, for the same reason one note cannot produce harmony. The testimony Jesus pointed to lives in the relationship, not the individual.

Lesslie Newbigin spent forty years as a missionary in India and then came home to a Britain that had quietly stopped believing, and he gave the rest of his life to a single argument: that the way the gospel becomes credible to a watching world is not primarily through argument but through a community that visibly lives a different life. The church, Newbigin said, is the hermeneutic of the gospel — the lens through which the world reads whether the message is true. People do not first believe the gospel and then join the community. They watch the community, find it strange and compelling, and through it come to wonder if the gospel might be real. Take away the community and you have taken away the world's primary evidence.

Peter says the same thing in a single dense sentence: *You are a chosen people, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God's special possession, that you may declare the praises of him who called you out of darkness into his wonderful light* (1 Peter 2:9). Every noun is plural and corporate. A *people.* A *priesthood.* A *nation.* You cannot be a nation by yourself. You cannot be a people of one. The witness Peter describes is something a gathered, visible, peculiar community produces by existing — and no individual, however devoted, can be a nation alone.

## Why the three cannot be unbundled

You might be tempted, having heard all this, to take the parts you like and leave the building behind. Keep the body language as a metaphor for online Christian friendship. Keep the family feeling through a small group that meets in a living room and never bothers with the rest. Keep the witness by being a good and visibly kind person at work. Assemble your own church out of components, à la carte, without the institution that disappointed you.

I understand the appeal, and I want to be honest about why it does not quite work — not because the components are bad, but because the three things church is for are not three separate features you can order individually. They are three faces of one reality, and they hold each other up.

The body needs the family, because a body where the members do not actually know each other is just a crowd performing unity. The family needs the witness, because a family turned inward, existing only for its own warmth, curdles into a clique and stops being good news to anyone outside it. And the witness needs the body, because the love-between-Christians that Jesus said would convince the world cannot be produced by individuals who have assembled a private spiritual life — it can only be produced by people genuinely bound to each other, including the ones they did not choose and would not have picked. The à la carte version keeps failing for the same reason a hand kept in a jar of nutrients is not a functioning hand. You can preserve the parts. You cannot make them live apart from the whole.

This is also why the local, committed, physically gathered, frustratingly specific congregation matters and cannot be replaced by a stream or a network of like-minded friends. You can learn from a teacher online; you cannot be carried by one. You can feel inspired by a podcast; it will not sit with you in the hospital. The body, the family, and the witness all require the same thing the consumer model was built to eliminate — actual, durable, inconvenient commitment to a specific group of people who can see you, need you, fail you, and hold you. Unbundle that, and you have kept the brochure and lost the thing the brochure described.

## What you actually walked away from

So let me say, gently, what I think happened to a lot of you.

You did not walk away from the body, the family, and the witness. You probably never got offered them. You got offered a service, and the service was mediocre or wounding or simply replaceable, and you made the rational call. The error was not yours. The error was a church that forgot what it was for, dressed itself up as a venue, and trained you to evaluate it as one — and then could not understand why you left when the venue down the street, or the one on your screen, or the one called Sunday morning in bed, offered a better experience for less.

But the thing church is actually for cannot be gotten any other way. You cannot be a ligament in your living room. You cannot be a family of one. You cannot, by yourself, be the love-between-Christians that Jesus said would make the world wonder. These are not features the church offers. They are the church. And the ache you feel — the suspicion that your private faith is missing something it cannot name — is the precise shape of a body part that has forgotten it is part of a body, and a family member who has been eating alone so long they have started to call it freedom.

You are not done with church. You may have never met it. The question is not whether to go back to the service that failed you. The question is whether you have the courage to go looking for the thing the service was always a counterfeit of — and to be, for someone else, the ligament that holds when their diagnosis comes.
