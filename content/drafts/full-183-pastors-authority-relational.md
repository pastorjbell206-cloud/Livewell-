Jesus taught with authority, and the crowds were astonished — "not as the scribes" (Matthew 7:28–29). The scribes had credentials. They had lineage and training and the full institutional weight of the synagogue behind their words. What they did not have, and what Jesus had, was the kind of authority that does not require the institution to enforce it. It arrived before the argument did. It was present in the room.

We have spent a great deal of energy in the church debating the source of pastoral authority — biblical, ecclesiastical, congregational, charismatic — and the debate is not without merit. But it tends to skip the most observable fact about how authority actually functions in a congregation: people follow leaders they trust, and trust is built the same way it has always been built. Slowly. Through kept promises and honest presence and being found reliable when it costs something.

Positional authority is real. The office of pastor carries weight in the text and in the tradition. But positional authority alone can compel attendance. It cannot compel following. A congregation can sit in the pews, vote the right way, serve on the right committees, and not be following you in any meaningful sense — not actually moving in the direction you are calling them toward. They are present without being persuaded. They are compliant without being committed.

I have led that way. I know what it produces. It produces a church where the pastor is the center of everything and the congregation is the audience, where vision is issued and received rather than built and owned, where people measure the institution by whether the pastor delivers rather than whether they belong to something they believe in. It looks like a functioning church from the outside. From the inside, it is waiting to fracture along whatever fault line appears first.

The pastoral letters in the New Testament are striking for how relational they are. Paul does not write to churches as an executive issuing directives to subsidiary offices. He writes as a man who loves the people he is writing to, who names them by name, who is anxious about them and proud of them and sometimes desperate about them. His authority in those churches was real — he defended it vigorously when it was challenged — but it was not separable from relationship. The authority was the relationship, demonstrated through years of shared life and real cost and genuine care.

"I am the good shepherd," Jesus said. "The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). The sheep follow him "because they know his voice" (John 10:4). Not because he has a title. Because they know him. That knowing is built through proximity, through care that costs something, through the experience over time that this person is for me rather than for the institution.

Relational authority is more demanding than positional authority, because it cannot be granted by a vote or maintained by a document. It must be earned continuously. A pastor can have twenty years of track record and squander it in a single season of unavailability or dishonesty or decisions that communicate that the institution matters more than the people. Trust accumulates slowly and departs quickly, and the pastor who has been operating on positional authority will often not realize how thin the relational foundation has become until the moment of crisis when he needs people to follow him somewhere hard and discovers they will not.

The answer is not complicated, but it is demanding. Be present. Keep your word to people who cannot advance your career. Show up in the Tuesday-afternoon moments when no one is watching and nothing is being built. Know the names and the losses and the fears of the people in your congregation well enough that your care for them is evident rather than performed.

That kind of knowing takes time you feel like you cannot spare. It builds authority no title can replace.

The congregation that follows you into the hard seasons is the one that knew you in the ordinary ones.
