There will always be something.

That is the one thing I can promise you about pastoral ministry — the one guarantee that holds across every church size, every denomination, every city or small town or dying suburb where you have planted yourself. There will always be a crisis. Always a meeting that bled into another meeting. Always the sermon that isn't ready on Saturday night, the deacon who left a voicemail at 10 p.m., the family that fell apart on what was supposed to be your day off. There will always be a reason to keep going, to keep working, to skip the rest God commanded and call it faithfulness.

That reasoning has a name. It is not faithfulness. It is functional atheism.

The fourth commandment — "Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy" — is not a suggestion embedded in a longer list of good ideas (Exodus 20:8). It is a command carved into the same stone as the prohibition on murder, the prohibition on adultery. The rabbis did not treat it as optional. Jesus did not abolish it. He argued about how to keep it, which is entirely different. The reformers who shaped the theological tradition most of us were trained in fought long wars over the precise nature of Sabbath observance. What none of them disputed was this: the rhythm of six and one is written into creation itself, and the God who built it into creation expected his creatures to keep it.

I did not keep it for years. I told myself I was different. Pastoral work is different — you cannot walk away from a soul in crisis the way a carpenter can walk away from an unfinished cabinet. I was half-right. The crisis part is real. The conclusion I drew from it was wrong.

What I was actually saying, underneath the pastoral-care language, was that I did not trust God to hold the church while I rested. I believed, in the quiet center where actual theology lives, that my presence was indispensable. That if I stepped back, something essential would collapse. That the church was mine to carry rather than his to sustain. The theology I preached on Sunday — that God is sovereign, that the work is his, that we are instruments not owners — did not govern my Monday through Saturday. I had separated orthodoxy from practice with a wall I called "the demands of ministry."

The Exodus account is pointed about this. The manna that appeared six days a week was given in double portion on the sixth specifically because none would fall on the seventh (Exodus 16:22–26). When some of the Israelites went out on the Sabbath to gather anyway, they found nothing. The provision was structured to make the rest possible. God built the supply so the command was keepable. Which means that when you tell yourself you cannot afford a Sabbath, you are not making a resource argument. You are making a trust argument. You are saying God has not provided enough.

I am not interested in legalism about which day or what activities constitute proper rest. Those arguments are often proxies for avoiding the harder question. The harder question is this: Do you actually believe God is at work when you are not?

The pastor who cannot stop working has not, in the deepest sense, begun praying.

Your congregation will survive your day off. Your marriage may not survive the decade in which you never took one. Your body will deliver the verdict eventually, and bodies do not issue warnings — they issue diagnoses. The pastors I know who burned out were not men who rested too much. They were men who rested too little for too long and then could not rest at all, because the exhaustion had become so total it outran the capacity for recovery.

The Sabbath is mercy. It is God saying, in a language older than words: the weight is not yours to carry every hour of every day. Put it down. I have it.

You do not have to earn the day off. It was given in the beginning, before the fall, before ministry, before any of us had done enough to deserve rest. Which means rest is not the reward at the end of faithfulness. Rest is the condition in which faithfulness becomes possible.

Take the day. Not because you have finished. Because you will never finish. Take the day anyway.

What remains when you don't will tell you more than you want to know.
