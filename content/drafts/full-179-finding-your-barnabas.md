Read Acts 9 carefully. Paul has just been converted on the Damascus road. He is on fire, preaching in the synagogues, confounding the Jews who thought he was coming to arrest Christians. He arrives in Jerusalem and the church there wants nothing to do with him. They are afraid of him. The word on the street is that Saul of Tarsus destroys communities from the inside, and they are not persuaded that a vision on a road changes a man.

Then Barnabas does one specific thing. He takes Paul and brings him to the apostles. He vouches for him. He tells the story of Damascus in a room where no one wanted to believe it (Acts 9:27).

That is it. That is the whole act. Barnabas believed Paul when the church did not, and he said so out loud, in a room where the saying cost him something. Without that moment, there is no Galatian mission, no letter to the Romans, no Athens sermon. The theological architecture that has shaped Christian thought for two thousand years begins with one man willing to say: I know him. He is who he says he is.

Every pastor I know who has lasted in ministry has a Barnabas in the story. Sometimes they can name the person easily — a mentor who refused to give up on them during the early years when they were making every mistake a young pastor makes. Sometimes it is more diffuse, a small cohort of men who held each other accountable and honest across decades. But the pattern holds. The pastors who survive the long middle of ministry are not the ones who were most talented. They are the ones who were accompanied.

I did not understand this for a long time. I thought that needing someone's belief in you was a kind of weakness — that a mature pastor should have sufficient theological conviction and internal stability to move without an external witness. I was wrong, and the wrongness cost me years I would like to have back. The independent man in ministry is not strong. He is brittle. He breaks in ways that accountable men do not, because the breaks happen in the dark where no one sees them coming.

Barnabas is worth naming precisely because he understood something Paul had not yet learned to receive. His very name means "son of encouragement" (Acts 4:36). This is not a nice biographical note. It is a vocational description. There are people whose primary contribution to the body of Christ is not their preaching or their administration or their theological innovation. It is the specific gift of seeing what others cannot yet see in a person and saying so at the moment when it matters most.

You need that person. Not a yes-man — Barnabas and Paul had real conflict over John Mark (Acts 15:36–41), conflict sharp enough to end their partnership. A yes-man agrees with everything. A Barnabas believes in you while being capable of disagreement. That is an entirely different thing, and it is rarer.

Finding this person is not a passive process. Barnabas did not appear because Paul was waiting. Barnabas was already in Jerusalem, already known to the apostles, already someone who had demonstrated the character to make his vouching mean something. The relationships that produce this kind of accompaniment are built before they are needed. You do not find your Barnabas in a crisis. You find him in the ordinary Tuesday lunches and the phone calls that are about nothing in particular and the Sunday nights where you are exhausted and you call someone just to say that. The relationship that can hold you in the hard year is built in the easy years.

I have had men in my life who did this for me. They believed in my calling before my calling had produced anything worth believing in. They sat across from me in the dark seasons and refused to let me conclude that the dark was permanent. They were not naive about my failures. They knew them. They held the belief and the knowledge at the same time, and that combination — belief that survives knowledge — is precisely what makes it worth having.

Find that person. Or better: be that person for someone younger than you, and trust that the discipline of offering it will make you capable of receiving it.

The ministry that lasts is always built on something that looks, from the outside, like ordinary friendship.

It is not ordinary. Nothing about it is ordinary.
