There is an objection I have never fully answered, and I am not going to spend the next two thousand words pretending I finally have.

I can make my peace with the evil we do to each other. Give me an afternoon and I will walk you through the free-will defense, the case that a world of creatures who can genuinely love has to be a world of creatures who can genuinely wound, that forced love is a contradiction and God cannot both hand us freedom and confiscate every misuse of it. I believe that argument. I have made it elsewhere. It answers the concentration camp and the abusive father and the drunk driver, the whole ledger of what human beings choose. It answers cruelty.

It does not answer the fawn.

The philosopher William Rowe put the harder problem into a single image in 1979, in a paper called "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism," and it has not lost a step since. Picture a forest, somewhere no person has ever walked. Lightning strikes a dead tree and the fire spreads. A fawn is caught in it, badly burned, and lies in the undergrowth for several days in agony before death finally reaches it. No human being started the fire. No human being sees the fawn. No human being is ennobled by its death or learns a thing from it, or is even aware that it happened. The suffering arrives, runs its full course, and ends, and as far as anyone can tell it accomplishes exactly nothing.

That is not the problem of moral evil. No freedom is being protected in this story, because no one in it is free; there is only a fire and an animal and a length of pain with no witness and no fruit. And the fawn is not a special case. It is a single frame from something enormous. Predation is not a recent human arrangement. It is hundreds of millions of years old, written into the fossil record long before anything that could sin had evolved to sin. For most of the history of life on this planet, animals have torn other animals apart in order to live, and parasites have grown inside living hosts by consuming them slowly. Darwin, in an 1860 letter to the botanist Asa Gray, confessed he could not persuade himself that a good and omnipotent God would have deliberately designed the ichneumon wasps to feed within the living bodies of caterpillars. He was not being flippant. He was looking at the actual mechanism of the world and finding it hard to reconcile with the God he had been handed. So do I.

Bring it up out of the forest and into a hospital. A child gets leukemia. No one did it to her. There is no villain, no choice, no freedom being defended; there is a cell that copied itself wrong. A fault line that has been storing pressure since before there was a written word slips one afternoon, and a wave crosses the Indian Ocean, and a quarter of a million people are gone by nightfall. The plates were not angry. The virus has no malice. This is the suffering that no free will explains, and it is, I think, the strongest form of the case against the God I preach. Its technical name is the evidential problem of evil, and its honest version is not the claim that suffering proves there is no God. It is the quieter, heavier claim that the sheer quantity of apparently pointless suffering, the fawn multiplied by every unwitnessed agony in the long dark of natural history, makes a good and all-powerful God look, on the face of the evidence, unlikely. I felt the weight of that for years. I still feel its edge on the bad nights.

So let me say first what I will not do with it, because the church has done all of these to people in grief and I have winced from the back pew. I will not tell a mother in an oncology ward that God needed another angel, because that is not Christianity and it is not true. I will not tell her the earthquake was a punishment, because Jesus was asked that exact question about a tower that fell and killed eighteen people, and he refused the premise flatly (Luke 13:4-5). And I will not reach for the reflex that blames the whole groaning order on the sin of one couple in a garden, as though a man eating fruit rewired plate tectonics and installed the second law of thermodynamics. The Bible does not require me to say that, and the geology will not let me. Predation and decay are older than humanity by a margin no reading of Genesis can shrink. If I am going to be honest with the skeptic, I have to be honest here first, where it costs me something.

What I have instead is smaller than an answer and larger than a shrug. A few things I have come to think are true, none of which closes the case, offered to a reader I am assuming is intelligent and may be in pain.

The first is about the kind of world this is. A world in which embodied creatures can act at all is a world that runs on stable law, and stable law does not bend to spare the creature. The reason your body can heal is the reason it can break; the chemistry that knits bone is the chemistry that lets it shatter under enough force, and you cannot keep the one property and delete the other, because they are the same property described from two sides. The tectonic churn that buries carbon and renews the soil and keeps this planet habitable across deep time is the same churn that slips one afternoon and drowns a coast. Water that can bear a boat can fill a child's lungs; it is not a different substance on the two occasions. You begin to see the shape of the difficulty. A world regular enough to act in, regular enough to build a boat and set a bone and love a creature whose behavior you can predict and trust, seems to be a world whose regularities will sometimes fall on that creature and crush it. I am not claiming this proves the arrangement good. I am claiming it may be the necessary price of any world in which anyone can do anything at all, and that a world without the price might be a world without embodied life in it. That is not comfort. It is a reason to think the situation might not be the plain negligence it looks like.

The second is that the Bible itself does not call the present arrangement the intended one. This surprised me when I read it instead of assuming its contents. I had expected Scripture to defend the world as it stands, to pronounce the whole system very good and dare me to argue. It does something stranger. Paul writes: "For the creation was subjected to futility, not willingly, but because of him who subjected it, in hope that the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God. For we know that the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now" (Romans 8:20-22). Read what that does not say. It does not say the groaning is good. It does not say the futility is the last word. It calls the present order subjected, bound, in labor, straining toward a release it does not yet have. The name Scripture gives the fawn's agony is not justice and it is not design fulfilled. It is groaning. The Bible looks at the same world Rowe looked at and refuses to call it finished. Whatever this is, it files it under childbirth, not under the way things are meant to stay.

The third thing is Job, and I have to be careful, because Job is the book everyone reaches for and almost everyone uses to deliver the very lecture the book was written to demolish. Job loses everything, his children included, to raiders and a collapsing house, human evil and natural disaster braided together. His friends arrive with theodicies, tidy accounts of what he must have done to earn it, and God's verdict at the end is that the friends were wrong and Job, who protested, had spoken rightly. But when God finally answers Job out of the whirlwind, he does not explain. He asks questions for four chapters, and the strangest thing about those chapters is their content. God offers no moral defense of the universe. He gives Job a tour of the wild. He points to the lioness on the hunt and the young ravens crying for food, to the wild donkey who scorns the noise of the city, to the ostrich who deals cruelly with her own young, to the hawk and the wild ox and two untamed monsters no man can bring under a yoke (Job 38-41). It is a parade of exactly the parts of creation that do not revolve around human comfort or even human sight, a world with predation and wildness built into its frame, and God claims all of it as his, on purpose, without apology and without explanation. Job wanted the transcript of the trial. What he got was the living God and the wild otherness of what that God had made, and Job says he is satisfied, not because the ledger finally balanced but because he had been arguing about someone he had never met and now had met him. I do not fully understand that ending. I have watched it happen to people at gravesides, which is why I no longer wave it off.

And the last thing is the one that finally held me, not because it answers the argument but because it moves who is standing where. The God of Christianity does not issue the fawn a statement from outside the fire. The claim at the center of the faith is that he came into the world that groans and took its worst onto his own body. Not a symbolic body. A nervous system. He starved in a wilderness, wept at a friend's grave, and died of the ordinary brutal physics of asphyxiation and blood loss under a Roman crossbeam, crying the sufferer's own line, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46). Whatever this is, it is not a comfortable deity narrating our pain from a safe altitude. It is a God who declined the altitude. That does not tell me why the fawn burned. It tells me the maker of the fawn is not exempt from the fire, and for reasons I can defend better with my life than with a syllogism, that turned out to be the thing I could stand on when the argument could not be won.

I promised at the start that I would not pretend I had solved this, so at the end I will keep the promise where it costs the most. None of that resolves the fawn. Line the four considerations up and the animal is still in the undergrowth, still burned, still alone, and I have not produced the reason its days of pain were worth it, because I do not have that reason, and the man who tells you he does is selling you something he has never held. The evidential problem is not dissolved. It is, at most, held. What Christianity offers the skeptic here is not a proof that the suffering makes sense. It is two claims the suffering on its own terms cannot reach: that this is not the arrangement God intends to keep, and that he did not stay outside it.

I still feel the edge of this one, and I want you to know that, because the church usually hands you the man who has stopped feeling it, and you are right not to trust him. I have five sons, and I have buried other people's children, and there are nights the argument comes back at full force and I do not have the answer, only the person. The fawn's fire is real. It is also, if the gospel is true, not the last word and not the intended word, and there is a difference wide enough to build a life on between a universe where that pain is finally nothing and a universe where it is finally seen.
