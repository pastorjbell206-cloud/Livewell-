The question does not arrive in an ethics seminar. It arrives in a room with a bed in it.

Someone you love is in that bed, and a machine is doing the breathing now, or the pain has stopped answering the medicine, and a doctor with kind eyes is asking you what you want to do. Or it is not someone you love. It is you. The scan came back, and you have started doing the math on how many of the months ahead are the ones where you are still yourself. I need to say something before I say anything else, because a man in a room like that deserves it said plainly. I am a pastor. I am not a physician and I am not a lawyer, and nothing in this essay is medical or legal advice. The decisions in that room are not mine to make and not mine to grade. They belong to you, to the people who love you, to the doctors who actually know your body, and to a pastor who knows your name. What I can do is refuse to hand you a slogan on the way in.

Because the slogans are waiting on both sides of that door, and both of them are lying to you about how hard this is.

So start with the case I am supposed to be against, and refuse the cartoon of it first. The people who argue for a chosen death are not, mostly, people who hold life cheap. Many of them arrived at the argument the hard way, at a bedside, watching. They watched someone take a very long time to die and suffer nearly every hour of it. They watched a person they loved become a body that pain moved through, a mouth that could no longer form the names of the people in the room. And they came away with a conviction that deserves to be stated at full strength, not swatted down. It is this. When there is nothing left but the suffering, when the medicine has run out of mercy and the only thing modern hospitals are good at is making the dying last longer, it is not compassion to prolong it. It is cruelty wearing a lab coat. We would not do it to a dog. We call it kindness when we spare an animal this, and then we force it on our own mothers and tell them to offer it up.

Under the compassion sits a second argument, and it is harder. It is the argument from your own hands. If anything in this world is yours, surely your own death is yours. You did not choose to be born; the least the universe owes you is a say in how you leave. A man who has run his own life, made his own bed, paid his own way, should not have to spend his final chapter as a problem to be managed, kept alive past the point he recognizes himself because a law or a religion he never signed decided his suffering was necessary for reasons that are not his. The word we reach for is dignity, and behind it is a real fear, one I will not pretend is unreasonable. It is the fear of a medicalized dying. Of ending not in a bed at home with the people who know you, but in a fluorescent room among strangers who are efficient and gentle and cannot stop, wired to machines that beep your heart back from every exit it tries to take, your death postponed one intervention at a time until it happens anyway, later, worse, and further from anyone you love. The Greek word euthanasia means, at its root, a good death. The people asking for it are not asking for death. They have that already. They are asking that it be good.

I want you to feel the weight of that before I say anything else, because if you cannot feel it you are not ready to disagree with it. I have felt it. I have stood at enough bedsides to know that the suffering behind this argument is not theoretical, and that the people making it are often the ones who loved most and watched longest. Anyone who answers them with a bumper sticker about the sanctity of life has not been in the room.

Now let me stand somewhere else, and try to earn the standing.

The Christian account does not begin with a rule about dying. It begins with a claim about owning, and the claim is that you do not. Your life is not a possession you hold; it is a gift you were handed. Paul says it to a church that thought its bodies were its own business: "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price" (1 Corinthians 6:19-20). The psalmist says it looking backward at his own beginning: "Your eyes saw my unformed substance; in your book were written, every one of them, the days that were formed for me, when as yet there was none of them" (Psalm 139:16). And Job, who had more reason than any of us to curse the arrangement, says it having lost everything: "Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD" (Job 1:21). This is not the tidy answer it can sound like from a pulpit. It is the whole quarrel, stated. If my life is mine, then the autonomy argument is close to unanswerable, and the only real question is whether I am choosing well. If my life was given, then even my death is not a thing I own outright, and I hold it the way I hold everything else that was given, with open hands and not a clenched fist. The two arguments are not really about pain. They are about whose life it is. That is the whole disagreement, and it is worth naming honestly instead of pretending the compassion case is the only thing on the table.

But here is where the church has to be more careful than it usually is, and where a lot of frightened people have been handed a cruelty in God's name. Saying life is a gift is not the same as saying we must cling to every hour of it at any cost. It is not. Scripture itself will not let me say it. "For everything there is a season, and a time for every matter under heaven: a time to be born, and a time to die" (Ecclesiastes 3:1-2). There is a time to die. Christianity has never taught that death is the ultimate defeat to be resisted with every machine we own; that is closer to the creed of a culture that has stopped believing in resurrection and so has nothing left but the fight. The faith that says a man rose from a grave is not the faith most desperate to keep everyone out of one.

Which is why the tradition drew a line a long time ago that the current debate keeps blurring, and the line matters more than almost anything else I can tell you. It is the difference between allowing a death and causing one. For centuries Christian moral thought distinguished between what it called ordinary and extraordinary means, between the care you owe a dying person and the interventions that only prolong a dying already underway. Declining a fourth round of chemotherapy that will buy weeks of misery and no cure. Turning off a ventilator that is breathing for a body that will never breathe on its own again. Choosing comfort over one more surgery. The tradition has generally permitted all of these, and called none of them killing, because in each of them the disease is what takes the life. You are not reaching for death. You are declining to wage a war against a death that has already won, and letting a person go home. That is not the same act as putting the death in the syringe. One removes an obstacle and lets nature finish; the other makes the ending happen by your own hand. A skeptic will tell you the line is a fiction, that a dead patient is a dead patient and the rest is Catholic hairsplitting to salve the conscience. I understand why it looks that way from outside. But every parent knows the difference between failing to save a child and harming one, and every court knows the difference between letting a man die and killing him, and the difference does not evaporate because the case is sad. What this means for you, in the room, is freedom you may not know you have. You are not required to torture someone you love into a few more days. Letting go is not the sin the machines want you to think it is.

So if the tradition already lets us stop, what is left to argue about? Only the last step. Only the hand on the syringe. And here the Christian objection is not mainly about the sufferer, who has my whole sympathy. It is about everyone standing behind him. Because the moment a society makes killing a medical option, it changes what it means to be old, to be disabled, to be expensive, to be a burden. The offer, once available, becomes a question, and the question does not stay unasked. A frail woman who would never have thought of it starts to wonder whether she is costing her children too much, whether the decent thing, the loving thing, would be to stop being in the way. Nobody has to coerce her. The mere existence of the door does the work; her sense that she ought to consider walking through it is the coercion. Wherever assisted dying has been made law, the criteria that began narrow, for the terminal and the imminently dying, have tended over years to widen, toward the chronically ill, the disabled, the mentally anguished, the merely tired of life. I am not going to hand you invented numbers, and I am not going to pretend every expansion is a scandal. I am going to say the plain thing the disability rights community has been saying, from the left, for a long time, and being ignored: a right to die, in a society that treats some lives as less worth living, has a way of becoming a duty to die for the people that society was already tired of carrying. The most vulnerable person in that hospital is not the one with the strong family and the good insurance who wants control. It is the one with neither, who is about to learn what his society thinks he is worth.

There is a better thing than the argument, and the church half-forgot it and had to be reminded by a woman most of us have never heard of. In 1967, a British physician named Cicely Saunders, a Christian, opened St Christopher's Hospice in London and more or less founded the modern hospice movement out of one conviction: that a dying person is a living person, that the pain of dying is not only in the body but in the mind and the spirit and the fear, what she called total pain, and that all of it could be met. Not cured. Met. She proved something the whole assisted-dying debate is starving for. That the choice is not really between agony and the needle. That a death can be attended, its pain actually managed, its terror actually company-kept, the person actually accompanied to the door instead of hurried through it. Most of the horror driving the case for a chosen death is the horror of dying abandoned, dying as a medical problem in a room full of strangers. The answer to being abandoned is not to be killed. It is to not be abandoned. Hospice at its best is the church remembering an older word for this than dignity. The word is unabandoned. There is such a thing as dying well, surrounded and unafraid, and we let it become exotic.

Now I have to be careful about a different kind of person, because I have watched this get bungled too. There is a distance the width of a life between the debates I have been describing and the private darkness of someone who wants to die because the pain of being alive has become unbearable. If that is where you are as you read this, I am not handing you a verdict. That is not a bioethics question and I will not treat it as one. It is a wound, and it wants company and care, not an argument, and it wants them now. Please tell someone today. A doctor, a pastor, a crisis line, a person in your house. In the United States you can reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline by calling or texting 988. The despair is lying to you about how permanent it is and about how little you would be missed. Do not believe it alone in a room. The church's word to you is not a position paper. It is: stay, and let us stay with you.

I have been writing as if I were only the pastor at the bedside. I am also going to be in the bed. I have five sons, and one of the quiet arithmetic problems of loving them is knowing that one day I will most likely be their burden, that the strong father who carried them will become the old man they have to lift. And I have felt, already, in preview, the exact fear the autonomy argument is built on: the fear of being too much, of costing them their years, of the loving thing being to get out of the way. I understand the man who wants to spare his children that. I am that man on the bad afternoons. Which is exactly why I do not trust the fear to write my ending. It is not asking to die. It is asking not to be a burden, and a faith worth having tells me that being carried is not beneath my dignity, because I was never the source of my dignity in the first place. "For if we live, we live to the Lord, and if we die, we die to the Lord. So then, whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord's" (Romans 14:8). I do not get to be useful all the way to the end. I get to be his. That will have to be enough, because it is what is true.

Let me leave you with the man who I think understood this best, because he wanted to die and did not. Paul, in a prison cell, tired, with the executioner somewhere down the hall, wrote: "I am hard pressed between the two. My desire is to depart and be with Christ, for that is far better. But to remain in the flesh is more necessary on your account" (Philippians 1:23-24). Read what that is. It is a man who genuinely wanted to go, who was not clinging, who had made his peace with death and honestly preferred it. And who stayed. Not because dying was forbidden to want, but because his life was not his own to spend, and there were still people it was for. He held his own death with open hands and then handed even that back.

I cannot tell you what to do in the room. I told you that at the start and I meant it, and anyone who would tell you is a stranger to how heavy that room is. What I can tell you is that you do not have to fight death like an enemy at every cost, and you do not have to reach for it like a friend to escape the cost, and there is a whole territory between those two panics that the church knew once and is trying to remember. It is the territory of dying well. Attended, not abandoned. Unafraid, because carried. Given back the way it was given. If you are in that room tonight, I am not going to hand you a rule. I am going to tell you that you are not the first to stand there, that the people who love you are the ones to stand there with you, and that whatever you decide, you were never as alone in it as the room makes you feel. Do not decide it by yourself. That is the only thing I am sure of. Not by yourself.
