The suspicion is older than any of us, and it does not come dressed as an argument. It comes as a feeling in the room when a frightened person prays.

You have seen it. A woman in a hospital corridor asking a God she has not spoken to in years to spare her mother, because she has run out of everything else. A man at a graveside telling himself he will see his father again, because the alternative is a door closing forever on the only face he ever wanted. And something in you, watching, thinks the honest thing, the thing no one says out loud at a funeral: of course they believe it. Look at what they need it to be true. The need came first. The belief arrived to meet it. Faith, on this reading, is not a discovery about the world. It is a wish the frightened animal makes and then mistakes for news.

I want to be honest about where I am standing when I write this, because I did not come to this objection as a believer swatting away a bug. For most of my life I held it, and I held it as the whole case. I did not think faith was probably false. I thought I could see the machinery that produced it, the fear underneath and the comfort on top, and once you can see the machinery you do not need to argue about the output. I thought belief was a coping mechanism that had learned to talk about truth. So when I take the objection seriously here, I am not being generous. I am remembering a version of myself I still respect.

## The objection at full strength

Two men gave this suspicion its spine, and neither was a fool, and I will state them the way their defenders would want them stated, because a version you can knock over is not worth the paper.

The first is Ludwig Feuerbach, writing in *The Essence of Christianity* in 1841. His claim is elegant and it does not require you to sneer at anyone. Feuerbach said that when human beings talk about God, they are talking about themselves without knowing it. Everything we ascribe to the divine, the perfect love, the perfect knowledge, the perfect justice, is the best of human nature abstracted from the actual weak and mortal people who carry it in pieces, purified of its limits, and then projected outward onto the sky as a being who stands over against us. Theology, he said, is anthropology. God is not the Father in heaven who made us in his image. God is the image, the ideal of our own species held up at a distance and mistaken for a person. On this account religion is not a lie exactly. It is a portrait of humanity that forgot it was a mirror.

The second is Sigmund Freud, and he took Feuerbach's mirror and found the specific wound it reflects. In *The Future of an Illusion*, published in 1927, Freud argued that religious ideas are not conclusions about reality at all but illusions in a technical sense, beliefs whose real engine is desire rather than evidence. And the desire he named is the one I cannot pretend I did not feel. The child, small and helpless before a world that can crush him, longs for the protection of a powerful father who will make it right. He grows up, and the world does not stop being able to crush him, and the longing does not die. It relocates. It reaches past the human father, who is finite and disappoints and eventually dies, to an infinite Father who cannot fail and cannot leave. God, for Freud, is that longing given a throne. The exalted cosmic father the frightened child never stopped wanting.

Now I have to do the thing that most defenses of faith refuse to do at this point, which is to grant how much of this is simply true. Religion can be a crutch. It is a crutch, constantly, for people who have made it one, and I have watched them, and I have been one of them. A great deal of what gets called faith is transparently projection, a god assembled to order out of a person's fears and preferences, warm where they want warmth and silent where they want silence. And the church, God forgive it, has sold exactly that god by the millions. It has offered the comfortable deity people already wanted, the one who blesses the plan they had made anyway and asks for nothing they were not already prepared to give. If your whole argument is that some faith is wish-fulfillment, you are not wrong. You are describing my inbox.

## Why the how cannot settle the whether

But watch what the argument is quietly doing while you nod along, because it is doing something it is not allowed to do.

Feuerbach and Freud both explain how a belief might arise. They give you a mechanism, fear plus longing plus projection, that would produce belief in God whether or not God exists. And then they slide, without earning the step, from how the belief arose to whether the belief is true. That step has a name. Philosophers call it the genetic fallacy, and it is a fallacy for a reason that survives inspection: the origin of a belief is simply not evidence about its accuracy. A frightened man on a sinking ship who believes the shore is close because he desperately needs it to be close may be believing for a bad reason. The shore may also be close. His fear does not move the coastline. Explaining why someone believes a thing tells you exactly nothing about whether the thing is so.

And here is where the tool turns in the hand of the one holding it. If a wish can produce a belief in God, a wish can produce the denial just as easily, and the same argument that dissolves faith dissolves the atheism that wielded it. Freud believed there was no God. By his own method, we are entitled to ask what he wanted. A man who spent his life arguing that the father is a fiction we invent, who had his own famously complicated account of what sons feel toward fathers, is not obviously a neutral instrument on the question of a heavenly Father. I am not saying his atheism was merely a wish. That would be the same cheap move, run in reverse, and it would prove nothing. That is the point. It proves nothing in either direction. You cannot psychoanalyze a proposition into being false. The couch is not a laboratory.

## The wish nobody audits

So grant that projection is real, and then ask the question the objection never turns on itself. Which wish are we talking about.

Because the assumption underneath the whole crutch theory is that all the wishing runs one way, toward God, and that unbelief is the wish-free default, the cold sober ground you stand on once you stop wanting things. That is not true, and some of the most honest atheists have said so. Thomas Nagel, who is one of the sharpest philosophers of the last half century and no friend of religion, wrote something in *The Last Word* in 1997 that I could not have written when I was where he is, and that I have never forgotten. He admitted that his resistance to God was not a cool matter of evidence. "I don't want there to be a God," he wrote. "I don't want the universe to be like that." That is not a crack in his argument. It is the most honest sentence in the room, and it detonates the crutch objection from the inside, because it means the wanting is on both sides of the table.

Of course it is. Consider what a God would cost. A God means I am not finally my own. It means there is someone I did not choose whom I will answer to, a judgment I cannot appeal, a floor of moral demand under my life that I did not set and cannot lower. It means my worst hours are not private. The wish that there be no God, no reckoning, no witness, no one at the center with a claim on how I live, is not the absence of a wish. It is one of the most powerful wishes a human being can have, and it is precisely the wish that a certain kind of man is least able to see, because it feels like nothing. It feels like just being reasonable.

I know it feels like that because it felt like that to me. I told myself for years that I had followed the evidence into unbelief, and there was real evidence and real reasoning, and I do not disown it. But underneath it, where I did not look, was a boy raised without a father who had decided very early that no father was coming, and who found in a godless universe not a grief but a relief, because a sky with no Father in it could not disappoint him the way one man already had. I wanted there to be no God. And that wanting was its own wish, as fevered and interested as anything I mocked in the believers, and I never once put it on the table and examined it. I ran the projection test on everyone but myself.

## The God nobody would order

There is one more thing the objection cannot account for, and it is the one that finally moved me, so I will end there.

If faith were wish-fulfillment, if we were building the father we longed for out of our own fears, then the god we built would be recognizable. He would be endlessly affirming. He would want what we already want. He would be, above all, nice, a cosmic parent who says the thing the frightened child aches to hear, that you are fine as you are and nothing more is required. That god exists. He is the best-selling god of the therapeutic age, and I have preached against him, and he is exactly what Feuerbach predicted, a mirror with a halo.

He is not the God of the Bible.

The God at the center of this book is not the one a frightened person would invent, because he begins by making a demand no frightened person could meet. "You therefore must be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect" (Matthew 5:48, ESV). That is not comfort. That is a bar set at the ceiling of the universe. When a man in Scripture actually comes into the presence of this God, he does not feel affirmed. Isaiah, a good man by every human measure, sees the Lord and comes apart: "Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the Lord of hosts!" (Isaiah 6:5, ESV). No one invents that experience to soothe himself. You do not build a comfort object that leaves you undone on the floor.

And then this holy God, who could have stayed safely in the sky as our projected ideal, does the one thing no projection would ever script. He comes down and is executed. "But God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, ESV). A wish-god does not bleed. A wish-god does not require a cross, because a wish costs the wisher nothing. The God of the cross confronts me with a holiness I cannot survive and a love I did not earn and a bill I could never have paid, settled by him at a price I would never have set. That is not the deity a child dreams up to stop being afraid. It is a God who takes the fear seriously enough to walk into it and die of it.

So I will give you back your own tool, honestly, because it is a good one. Ask what you want to be true. Ask it of the believer, and you will find plenty of faith that is nothing but a wish with a steeple. But then have the nerve to ask it of yourself, in the quiet, where no one is grading. Ask whether the God you have ruled out is the God of Scripture, holy and demanding and crucified, or a softer thing you assembled to be easy to refuse. And ask whether the freedom you feel on the far side of belief, the relief of a universe with no one at the center, is really the sound of a mind that wants nothing.

I thought I had followed the truth into the cold. I had followed a wish, and mistaken it for clarity, and called it maturity for twenty years. The crutch was real. It was just in my other hand.
