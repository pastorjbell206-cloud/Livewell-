Two sentences get said whenever this subject comes up, and both of them are ways of not having the conversation. The first is that Christianity and Islam are basically the same thing, two roads up one mountain, differing only in the accent of their prayers. The second is the opposite, that they have nothing in common at all, two armed camps who worship different gods and always have. I have said both, at different times in my life, for the same reason: I did not want to look closely. When I was an atheist the first one was especially convenient. If all religions were interchangeable, I did not have to take any single one of them seriously enough to be troubled by it. Lumping them together was not tolerance. It was a way of keeping every one of them at arm's length.

They are not the same. And they are not strangers. Both of those are true at once, and the honest thing is harder than either slogan, because it asks you to hold the real common ground and the real dispute in the same hand without letting either one cancel the other.

Start with the common ground, because it is not small and it is not nothing. Both faiths confess one God, not many. That alone divides them from most of the religious history of our species, and it is not a coincidence; they are cousins, and they know it. Islam traces itself, as Judaism and Christianity do, back to Abraham, and honors him as a man who submitted to the one God before there was a law or a church. Both take the moral life with a seriousness the modern West has mostly misplaced. A devout Muslim praying five times a day, fasting through Ramadan, giving alms as an obligation and not a mood, holds a discipline that ought to rebuke the average Christian rather than reassure him. And Islam honors Jesus. This surprises people who assume the two faiths meet only as enemies. The Qur'an holds Jesus, Isa, as a prophet and a messiah, born of the virgin Mary, a worker of miracles by God's permission. No Christian should wave that away. It is more honor than the secular world gives him.

So the disagreement is not the disagreement of people who share nothing. It is a family argument, which is the bitterest kind, because it runs straight through the things both sides hold most holy.

I want to state the Muslim objection to the Christian claim in its strongest form, the form a devout Muslim would recognize and sign, because a version he would not sign is not worth answering. At the center of Islam is tawhid, the absolute oneness of God. God is one, without partner, without division, without equal. The gravest sin in Islam, shirk, is the sin of associating anything with God, of setting any created thing beside the Creator. From inside that conviction, the Christian doctrine of the Trinity does not look like a richer account of God's unity. It looks like an assault on it. Three who are one sounds like arithmetic done to hide a compromise. And the claim that God has a son sounds worse. It sounds like dragging the Most High down into biology, into fathering and generation, into the very created order he stands infinitely above. The Qur'an rejects the idea that God begets or is begotten. A Muslim hears the Christian call God Father and Son and hears something close to blasphemy, not because he thinks little of Jesus but because he thinks so highly of God. And the cross compounds all of it. Would God abandon his prophet into the hands of his enemies, to be stripped and beaten and executed like a criminal on a Roman post? The God of Islam is too great, too victorious, to be defeated in public. It would be unfitting. It would be beneath him.

Grant the reverence in every line of that. This is the thing I most want the skeptic to see, because the skeptic assumes the Muslim objection is ignorance, and it is not. It is worship. Each of those objections is defending the size of God. A Muslim will not compromise the oneness of God, will not tolerate a rival beside him, will not picture him weak enough to be killed, precisely because he is guarding God against everything small that human beings are forever tempting God down into. If you do not feel the dignity in that, you are not ready to disagree with it.

And then, having granted it, I have to be honest about where the granting stops, because a respect that will not name a real disagreement is not respect. It is condescension wearing good manners.

## The nature of God

Both faiths say God is one. They mean genuinely different things by it, and pretending otherwise helps no one. Islam means a oneness that is also a simplicity: God is one the way a point is one, indivisible, without interior life, related to his creation but not, within himself, a relationship. Christianity means a oneness that is a unity: one God who exists eternally as Father, Son, and Spirit, not three gods and not three masks worn by one actor, but one being whose life is, from before creation, a communion of love. The Christian is not doing bad math. He is refusing to choose between "God is one" and "God is love," because a God who was alone before he made anything would have had no one to love until he made something, and love would then be a thing God learned rather than a thing God is. This is the deepest divide, and it does not dissolve. To the Muslim it endangers the oneness. To the Christian it is the only way the oneness could ever have been love.

## The identity of Jesus

Here is common ground, and here is the cliff at the end of it. Both honor the virgin birth. Both call him messiah. Both deny he was merely an ordinary man. But Islam honors Jesus by making him the greatest of the prophets, and Christianity confesses him as the one thing a prophet can never be. A prophet points away from himself, toward God. The scandal of Jesus, the thing that finally got him killed, is that he did not point away from himself. He forgave sins that had not been committed against him, accepted worship a faithful Jew would have died rather than accept, and let a doubting friend fall in front of him and say the unsayable. "Thomas answered him, 'My Lord and my God!'" (John 20:28). The Christian claim is not that Jesus was a good man who taught about God. It is that in this one man the God who made the galaxies had come near enough to touch. "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth" (John 1:14). Islam reveres Jesus and stops at the prophet. Christianity worships him and cannot stop there. There is no middle shelf to set the disagreement on.

## The cross

This is the sharpest one, and the most concrete, because it is not only about the meaning of an event. It is about whether the event happened. The dominant reading of the Qur'an, drawn from a verse in the fourth sura, an-Nisa, holds that Jesus was not crucified, that it was only made to appear so, that God did not permit his messiah to be killed but took him up to himself instead. Notice how coherent that is from inside Islam. Of course God would not surrender his prophet to a mob. Of course the victory of God could not run through a public execution. The denial of the cross is not a small historical footnote in Islam. It follows from everything Islam believes about how God treats the ones he sends.

And it lands on the single fact Christianity cannot give away. Because for the Christian the cross is not the tragic interruption of the story. It is the center of it. Remove it and there is nothing left standing. "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3-4). Paul knew exactly what he was staking. "And if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14). The two faiths do not merely interpret the cross differently. One says it did not happen, and the other says nothing else finally matters if it did not. That is not a difference of emphasis. It is a fork in the road with no path between the branches.

## How a person is made right with God

Then the question all the others were leading toward. How is the distance between a holy God and a failing human being ever closed. Islam's answer is coherent and demanding: a person submits. The word islam means submission. You surrender to the one God, you keep his commands, you pray and fast and give and confess his oneness and his prophet, and you throw yourself on the mercy of a God who is merciful, and your deeds are weighed. There is no atoning death in it, because none is thought to be needed; God can simply forgive, and the notion that God would require a death before he forgives can look, from inside Islam, like a limit placed on a mercy that ought to be limitless.

Christianity says the distance is too wide for submission to close, and that God knew it, and so God closed it himself, from his own side, at his own cost. "For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9). Here the two faiths are not describing one salvation in two vocabularies. One says a person climbs, helped by mercy, toward a God who commands the climb. The other says a person cannot climb, and God came down. It is grace as reward and grace as rescue, and they do not meet in the middle.

I will not pretend those four disputes are misunderstandings that a patient afternoon could clear away. They are not. They are the load-bearing walls of two different houses, and the people living inside them are not fools on either side. But naming the disputes honestly is the opposite of the contempt that usually stands in for this conversation. The pluralist who says the faiths are all the same has not honored Islam. He has erased it. A Muslim does not believe God is a Trinity, does not believe Jesus died on a cross, does not believe he was God in the flesh, and telling him he secretly agrees with the Christian is not respect. It is refusing to hear him. Taking a person seriously means letting him disagree with you all the way down.

When I was an atheist I kept every religion in one bin marked "things people believe to feel better," and the bin let me avoid all of them at once. Then I started reading them as if they might be making claims about what is actually real, and the bin came apart in my hands, because they were not saying the same thing in different keys. They were saying different things, and the differences could be examined, and at least some of them could in principle be true or false. Whether a crucified man walked out of a tomb outside Jerusalem is not a matter of preference. It happened or it did not.

The Christian faith stakes everything on the claim that it did. Not that Jesus was a great teacher, which Islam grants. Not that he was a prophet, which Islam honors. But that the one God, without ceasing to be one, entered his own creation as a man, was handed over to be killed, and was not defeated by it; and that this is not God brought low but God showing at last what his greatness was always for. I hold that as confession, not as a point scored against a neighbor I respect. I could be wrong, and if I am, I have built my life on a rumor. But I cannot make it smaller than it is to keep the peace at the table, and neither can the devout Muslim across from me make his smaller, and the honesty we owe each other is not the pretense that the gap between us is narrow.

It is to look at it plainly, name what stands on each side of it, and refuse to lie about its width.
