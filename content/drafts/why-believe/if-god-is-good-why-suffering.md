I will not start this one by being clever, because the subject does not allow it. The strongest argument against the existence of God is not the one made in philosophy seminars. It is the one made by a hospital ward full of children who did nothing to deserve what is happening to them. If there is a good and all-powerful God, that ward should not exist. It does exist. For a great many thoughtful people, that single fact has closed the question, and I do not think they are stupid or shallow for letting it. The problem of suffering is the best reason not to believe, and any apologetics that treats it as a debating point to be scored has already failed the people it claims to be helping.

So I am going to take it at full strength before I say anything in reply, and I am not going to pretend my reply dissolves it.

**My claim is deliberately modest, because anything less than modest here is a lie: the problem of suffering does not disprove God, but it does not need to be solved to be survived — and the alternative, a universe in which the suffering means nothing at all, is harder to live in than skeptics let on.** I am not going to tie a bow on this. There is no bow.

## Let the strongest version stand

The cheap Christian move is to soften the objection so it is easier to answer. I refuse to do that, because the strongest version is the one I actually believe, and the only honest way through it is straight in.

Dostoevsky put it more devastatingly than any modern atheist, and he put it in the mouth of a man named Ivan, in *The Brothers Karamazov* (1880). Ivan does not deny God exists. He does something worse for the believer — he says that even if God exists, even if there is some grand harmony at the end of all things in which every tear is wiped away and every wrong made right, he wants no part of it, because no future harmony can be worth the torture of one innocent child. He piles up real cases, atrocities done to children, and then he refuses the ticket. *It is not God I do not accept,* Ivan says in effect, *it is the world he made; I most respectfully return him the ticket.* That is the argument at full power. It is not a logic puzzle. It is a moral refusal, and it is more honest than most defenses of God I have heard.

The philosophical version is tidier and almost as hard. If God is all-powerful, he could prevent suffering. If God is all-good, he would want to. Suffering exists in staggering quantities. So either he cannot, and is not all-powerful, or will not, and is not all-good — and either way the God of traditional theism seems ruled out. For most of my life I thought that argument was airtight, and I thought anyone who believed had simply not let themselves feel its force.

I no longer think it is airtight. I do still think it should be felt with full force, and the believer who has not felt it is not believing in the real God. He is believing in a smaller one who never had to answer for the ward.

## Why the logical version weakened

The philosophers have actually moved on this, and it is worth a skeptic knowing where the conversation stands, because the version of the argument most people carry is a generation out of date.

For a long time the claim was that the existence of any evil at all was logically inconsistent with the existence of an all-powerful, all-good God — that you could not coherently hold both, the way you cannot hold a married bachelor. Alvin Plantinga is generally credited with ending that strong version. His free-will defense does not claim to explain why God permits any particular evil. It makes a narrower, more durable point: it is at least possible that a world containing free creatures, capable of genuine love and genuine evil, is more valuable than a world of puppets who cannot do wrong because they cannot do anything, and that even an all-powerful God cannot create free creatures and also guarantee they never misuse the freedom, because forced love is a contradiction. If that scenario is even possible — and almost no one now argues it is not — then the bare logical contradiction collapses. The coexistence of God and evil is not a contradiction in terms.

That settles less than it sounds like. It addresses the evil we do to each other. It does not touch the earthquake, the cancer in a child, the suffering no free human chose. And it does not make the suffering hurt less. Plantinga himself never claimed it did. It moves the argument from disproof to something murkier and more human: not *God cannot exist given this,* but *how could a good God allow this* — which is not a proof at all. It is a cry, and it deserves to be treated as one.

## The honest limit of every answer

Here is where I have to disappoint anyone hoping for the knockout, because the knockout does not exist and pretending it does is the cruelty I most want to avoid.

There are partial answers, and they carry some weight. The free-will defense covers moral evil. The argument that real virtues — courage, compassion, sacrifice — require a world with real stakes covers some of the rest. The observation that a creature cannot see the whole from inside the story covers the rest in principle. C.S. Lewis worked these through in *The Problem of Pain* (1940) with rigor and some real insight, and then, years later, after his wife died of cancer, he wrote *A Grief Observed* and discovered that none of his own arguments touched the wound. Both books are true. That is the thing about this subject. The intellectual answers can be sound and the wound can stay open, and a faith worth having has to hold both without letting either cancel the other.

Tim Keller, in *Walking with God through Pain and Suffering* (2013), made the observation that finally reframed it for me, and it is not a solution but a redistribution of the difficulty. Every worldview faces the problem of suffering. The secular one does not escape it — it relocates it. If there is no God, the suffering of the child is not a problem to be explained; it is simply a fact with no meaning, no redemption, no final accounting, a burst of pain in an indifferent universe that will forget it ever happened. The atheist does not get to keep the moral outrage that powered Ivan's argument and also keep the universe that makes outrage incoherent. If there is no God, on what ground is the child's suffering an *outrage* rather than merely an event, the way a landslide is an event? Ivan's refusal borrows a moral law to indict the universe, and a universe with no God supplies no such law to borrow.

That is not a proof of God. It is an observation that the problem of evil cuts both ways, and the side that thinks it has escaped the problem has only stopped naming it.

Press the point one turn further, because it is the turn that did the most to unsettle my own atheism. The problem of evil is not just an emotional difficulty for the unbeliever; it is a logical one, and it runs in the opposite direction from where I had aimed it. To call the suffering of a child *evil* — not unfortunate, not statistically regrettable, but evil, a violation of the way things ought to be — is to appeal to a standard of how things ought to be. But where does a purely material universe get an "ought"? Atoms do not have oughts. A process of blind selection produces creatures that survive; it does not produce a cosmic standard of justice that the universe is failing to meet. The atheist who is genuinely outraged at suffering is standing on a moral floor his own worldview cannot pour. He is using the reality of evil to argue against God, while the very category of evil — real, objective, binding wrong — is far easier to account for if there is a God whose character defines the ought than if there is not. I had thought the problem of evil was my strongest weapon against belief. It turned out to be borrowing its ammunition from the thing it was firing at.

## What Scripture does not do

The thing that disarmed me, when I finally read it rather than assuming its contents, was what the Bible does not do with suffering. I had expected it to explain. It refuses.

When Job — who has lost his children, his health, everything — finally gets the audience with God he has demanded, God does not answer the question. God does not explain the wager in the prologue, does not justify the loss, does not produce the theodicy Job's friends kept manufacturing. Instead, out of the whirlwind, God asks Job questions for four chapters (Job 38–42): where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth, can you bind the stars, do you give the horse his strength. It reads at first like evasion. It is not. It is the refusal of a glib answer from the only source entitled to give one. God will not reduce Job's agony to a lesson. He offers, instead, himself — his presence, his reality, the sheer scale of a wisdom Job cannot audit. And Job, strangely, is satisfied, not because he got the explanation but because he got God.

Habakkuk does the same thing from the other side. The prophet opens by shouting the problem straight at God — how long shall I cry for help and you will not hear, why do you make me look at injustice (Habakkuk 1) — and the book never resolves the complaint into a clean answer. It resolves into trust held in the dark. The Bible authorizes the protest. It puts the strongest version of the objection into the mouths of its own heroes and does not rebuke them for it.

And then there is the line of Jesus that should stop the believer cold. When people brought him the news of a massacre and a fatal accident — clearly fishing for the explanation, *what did those people do to deserve it* — he flatly denied the premise that suffering maps to guilt (Luke 13:1–5). Those who died were not worse sinners. The clean accounting we crave, where suffering is the receipt for sin, he tore up. He left the why unanswered and turned it back on the askers as a question about themselves.

## The strange location of the Christian answer

If Christianity had an explanation for suffering, it would be one more theodicy among many, and I would not have found it more persuasive than the others. It has something stranger, and the strangeness is the point.

The center of the faith is not a God who explains suffering from a safe distance. It is a God who entered it. The claim is that the One who could have answered Ivan from the throne instead came down into the ward — was betrayed, tortured, and executed, and cried from the cross the exact question every sufferer cries, *why have you forsaken me.* Romans says the whole creation groans as in childbirth, and we groan with it, waiting (Romans 8:18–25) — not denying the groan, naming it, locating it inside a labor that is going somewhere. The Christian answer to suffering is not a syllogism. It is a wounded God and a promised end, offered to people in the middle who cannot yet see either clearly.

I do not say this resolves the problem. It does not. The child in the ward is still in the ward. But it changes what kind of universe the ward is in. Not a universe where suffering is meaningless noise, and not a universe where a distant deity watches unmoved, but one where the suffering is real, and named, and entered, and not the last word.

## Where I leave you, because I cannot do better

I am not going to end this with a resolution, because to resolve it would be to betray everyone who is actually suffering while they read it. The problem of evil is not solved in this essay or any other. It is the hardest thing, and the people who tell you they have it figured out have usually not been near the worst of it.

Here is the most I can honestly say. The suffering does not disprove God; the logical version of that argument did not hold, and the moral version turns out to need a moral law that a Godless universe cannot supply. But the suffering does not get explained away either, and the faith that pretends otherwise is not worth having. What is left is a choice between two unprovable things: that the agony of the world means nothing and will be forgotten, or that it is real and seen and will somehow be answered by a God who did not stay out of it. Neither can be proven from inside the story. But one of them I have watched people die holding, with a peace the other never gave anyone.

The question was never going to get a clean answer. The God of Job never gave one. What he gave was himself, in the whirlwind and finally on a cross, and the people who have met him in their worst hour do not come back saying they understand. They come back saying they are not alone in it. That is less than you wanted. It may be more than you expected.
