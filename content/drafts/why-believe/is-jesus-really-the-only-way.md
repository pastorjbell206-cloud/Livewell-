The question almost never arrives as a question. It arrives as a face.

Someone is sitting across from you, and they have just buried a grandmother who prayed five times a day toward Mecca for sixty years, or lit lamps to a god whose name you cannot pronounce, and prayed with a devotion that shames your own. And the person across from you says the thing that is really being asked whenever anyone asks whether Jesus is the only way. They say: are you telling me she is in hell. They are not asking for a doctrine. They are asking whether the God you believe in is the kind of God who would do that, and whether you have the nerve to say it to their face.

I want to be honest about where I am standing when I write this. I did not come to this question as a believer defending home turf. I came to it as the objection itself. For most of my life I was an atheist, and of all the things the church claimed, this was the one I found not merely improbable but obscene. The idea that the accident of where a person was born, the family that raised them, the book that happened to be on the nightstand, would decide their eternity, struck me as the clearest evidence that the whole system was invented by men who wanted to believe they alone had the map. I did not think the exclusive claim was hard to believe. I thought it was easy to see through.

So when I say the objection is serious, I am not being generous. I am remembering.

Here is the objection in its strongest form, and I will not soften it, because a version you can knock over is not worth answering. The philosopher John Hick spent a career pressing it, and he pressed it well. Hick called for what he named a Copernican revolution in the way Christians think about other religions. For centuries, he said, we placed our own faith at the center and made every other tradition orbit around it, counted as true only insofar as it reflected ours. But what if the center is not Christianity at all. What if the center is God, or what Hick carefully called the Real, and Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, and the rest are all planets in orbit around that one sun, each catching the light at a different angle, each a genuine human response to the same infinite reality no one of us can see whole. On this account the sincerity of that grandmother is not a problem to be explained away. It is evidence. A God worth the name would not hide himself so that only the people handed the right pamphlet could find him.

And underneath the philosophy sits an intuition even harder to shake, because it does not require a philosophy degree to feel. If you had been born in Riyadh instead of a small town in Michigan, you would almost certainly be a Muslim, and you would hold it with exactly the confidence you now hold this. Your deepest convictions track your zip code. That should unsettle anyone. It unsettled me for years.

Grant all of it. Grant that the church has often held this doctrine like a weapon, has used the word only to bless conquest and baptize contempt, has said to the grieving what should never be said to the grieving. Grant that a great deal of what passes for defending the exclusivity of Christ has been the ugliest thing in the room. If you do not feel the force of that, you are not ready to say the next sentence.

Now watch what the argument does when you turn it over.

Hick loved to tell the old parable of the blind men and the elephant. One grasps the trunk and says the elephant is a snake, one the leg and says a tree, one the ear and says a fan. Each has part of the truth, none has the whole, and how foolish for any of them to insist his piece is the animal. It is a devastating little story. It has been used against Christian confidence for a century. But notice the one thing the parable cannot survive being asked. Who is telling it. The man narrating the story is not one of the blind men. He is standing above the whole scene with his eyes open, describing the entire elephant that all the others are too limited to see. To tell the parable at all, he has to claim the exact thing he is denying to everyone else: a view of the whole. Lesslie Newbigin, who spent forty years as a missionary in India and thought harder about this than almost anyone, put his finger on it. The person who says all religions are partial glimpses of a reality none can grasp is not standing nowhere. He is standing somewhere, and it is a very particular somewhere, and from it he is making the largest and most exclusive claim in the room. He is claiming to know the real shape of God well enough to correct every prophet and every scripture that ever said otherwise.

This is the thing I did not see when I was on the other side. I thought pluralism was the humble position and exclusivity the arrogant one. It is the reverse. Pluralism does not refuse the total claim. It makes a total claim and calls the refusal humility. Everyone at the table is making a truth claim about the whole. The Buddhist who says the self is an illusion and the Christian who says the self is loved by name are not describing the same elephant from two angles. They are saying incompatible things about what is finally real, and the pluralist who says they are both partial is not standing outside that disagreement. He has joined it, on a third side, and pretended he floated above it.

So the question was never whether we can avoid an exclusive claim. No one avoids one. The question is which exclusive claim is true. And that is a different kind of question, a harder and more honest one, and it deserves better than the charge of arrogance, because the charge applies to the one making it just as much.

Then there is the history, which almost everyone gets backwards. When people hear the church say there is no other name, they hear Christendom talking. They hear cathedrals and crusades, the strong telling the weak that the weak must convert or be damned. But go back to where the sentence was first spoken. Peter says it in the fourth chapter of Acts, and the scene is not a throne room. Peter is a fisherman standing trial. He and John have been arrested, hauled before the very council that arranged the execution of Jesus a few weeks earlier, and asked by what power they had healed a lame man. It is into that, powerless before the people who could kill him as easily as they killed his teacher, that Peter says it: "And there is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved" (Acts 4:12). That is not the language of empire. That is a condemned minority refusing to add the emperor's name, or the council's, to the name of the crucified man they will not stop preaching. The exclusivity did not begin as a boast of the powerful. It began as the defiance of the powerless, and it cost most of them their lives.

The scandal of the claim was never that it grabbed too much power. The scandal was where it located salvation. Not in a philosophy the clever could master, not in a law the disciplined could keep, not in a birthplace or a bloodline, but in an executed provincial nobody from an occupied backwater. That is the offense. Christianity does not say the way is narrow because God is stingy with the map. It says the way is a person, and a person is the one thing you cannot earn your way to or reason your way to or be born into. You can only receive him. Which means the door the doctrine seems to close on merit and pedigree and geography is the same door it throws open to the grandmother who never had the right book, because if salvation is a gift and not a grade, then the one thing that cannot disqualify her is that she was born in the wrong place.

I need to be careful here, because two questions get fused and they are not the same. One is whether Jesus is the only Savior, whether the cross is the sole ground on which any human being is ever reconciled to God. The historic church answers yes, and it is bound to, because the whole faith stands on it. The other question is whether every person must consciously hear and name him in this life to be reached by what he did. That question is genuinely harder, the church has held more than one faithful answer to it, and it is not the one I am settling today. Hold it open. What the exclusivity of Christ settles is the mechanism, that all grace runs through one cross. It does not by itself hand you the roster of who is saved, and anyone who tells you it does has said more than the text does.

Which brings the whole thing back to the face across the table, and to the verse people quote at that face without ever reading where it sits. "I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:6). We hear it as a bouncer at a door. Read the sentence before it. Thomas, frightened, says, "Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?" The line about the only way is not spoken to an outsider to keep him out. It is spoken to a terrified friend who asked how to find God, and it is the tenderest possible answer: you do not have to know the way, because I am the way, and I am not leaving you to find it alone. The exclusivity and the comfort are the same sentence. They were never meant to be pried apart.

I do not know the eternal standing of your grandmother, and I will not pretend to, and anyone who says it easily has not understood either the doctrine or the grief. What I know is that the claim I once thought was Christianity's cruelty is closer to its whole hope. A God you reach by being born right, or thinking right, or behaving right, is a God who belongs to the lucky and the strong. The God who comes down to one cross, and offers himself as the way rather than posting the way on a wall, is the only kind of God who could ever be good news for the person who got everything wrong.

That is not a smaller God than the pluralist's. It is a more dangerous one. He will not stay a symbol you assemble from all traditions at your convenience. He has a name, and he was killed under it, and he asks to be received rather than admired. You can refuse him. People did, to his face, and he let them walk. But do not refuse him for being arrogant. Refuse him, if you must, for being too particular, too specific, too unwilling to be one glimpse among many. And then sit with the possibility that the particularity you resent is the only shape love could take if it meant to reach the ones who could never have found it themselves.
