The water is the same. That is the strange part.

Walk into a Baptist church on a Sunday and you may see a grown man led down into a tank at the front, dropped all the way under, and brought up streaming and shaking and alive. Walk two streets over into a Presbyterian or a Lutheran or an Anglican or a Catholic church and you may see a woman hand her eight-day-old son to a minister who wets three fingers and touches the child's head with water he will never remember. Same God named over both. Same words, nearly. Same conviction in the room that something real is being done. And a wall between the two buildings that has held for five hundred years and shows no sign of coming down. Two Christians can love the same Lord, read the same book, and stand on opposite banks of the same font, each certain the other has gotten the water wrong.

I want to tell you at the front what kind of question this is, because naming the order changes how hard you are allowed to hit. This is a second-order question. It is the classic one, in fact. First-order matters are the ones the ancient creeds bind, the things you cannot deny and still be confessing the faith: the Trinity, the deity and humanity of Christ, the bodily resurrection, salvation by grace through the cross. Deny those and you have left the room. Baptism is not that. How and to whom the water goes divides faithful churches without dividing the faith itself, and the men who argued it hardest knew it. The Reformers who would not budge an inch on justification wrote to each other across this line as brothers. So I am not going to adjudicate it for you. I am going to hand you both cases at full strength, name what neither can escape, and leave you to carry the weight of deciding, because the church has carried it unresolved for a very long time and has not been ashamed to.

First, what the two sides are not fighting about, because it is more than you would guess. Both confess that baptism is not a naming ceremony with a religious accent. Both confess it is the sign and seal of union with Christ, a death and a resurrection worked on the person and not merely a decision performed by them. Both read Paul and mean it: "Do you not know that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? We were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:3-4). Buried and raised. The Baptist and the Presbyterian will preach that verse the same way. What they cannot agree on is who belongs in the grave, and how far under they have to go. The meaning is shared. The recipients and the mode are the fight.

Take the believer's case first, and take it at full height, because a caricature of it is easy and worthless. The credobaptist, the one who baptizes only those old enough to profess faith, is not being stingy with grace. He is reading the New Testament with a plain eye and asking you to explain what you see. Every single baptism recorded in the book of Acts follows a personal, spoken turning toward Christ. Peter, on the first day, does not say be baptized and then believe. He says the other order: "Repent and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit" (Acts 2:38). Repentance first. Repentance is not something an infant does. The credobaptist looks across the whole New Testament and finds the baptized always described as those who have heard, believed, received the word, and then gone down into the water, in that sequence, never once reversed with a commentary explaining the exception for children.

And the shape of the act itself, he says, is preaching a sermon that sprinkling mutes. Look again at Romans. Buried with him. You do not bury a body by dampening its forehead. You put it all the way under and it disappears and then it comes up. The Greek word the New Testament reaches for, baptizo, carries in its ordinary use the sense of dipping, plunging, submerging. When the picture is dying and rising with Christ, immersion is not a preference. It is the drama made visible, the four seconds in which a person is a corpse in the water and then is not. This is the free-church conviction, the one the Anabaptists paid for in blood in the 1520s when Zurich drowned some of them for the crime of being baptized twice, and the one the Baptists confessed in London in 1689: the church is a community of the regenerate, of those who can say for themselves that they have died and been raised, and the water is for them. To baptize a baby who cannot yet believe, the credobaptist says gently, is to put the sign on a faith that is not yet there, and to risk teaching a child for the rest of his life that he was made a Christian by something that happened before he could say yes.

Now turn, and give the other side the identical refusal to caricature, because the paedobaptist is not baptizing babies out of sentiment or a wish to get them into heaven early. He is standing on the one unbroken storyline of the whole Bible, and he is asking the Baptist a question the Baptist finds harder than he lets on. God has always put the sign of belonging on the children of believers before those children could choose. He did it for two thousand years. When God made his covenant with Abraham, he commanded that the sign be laid on every son at eight days old, long before that son could repent or profess or understand a thing. The children of believers were marked as belonging to the covenant people from the beginning, by grace, before response. The paedobaptist asks: did God change his mind about that in the new covenant, and narrow the promise to exclude the very children he had always included? He reads Peter's own next sentence at Pentecost, "the promise is for you and for your children," and he hears the ancient pattern held open, not closed.

And he has a text that ties the two signs together with a knot the credobaptist has to work at. Writing to the Colossians, Paul says the Christians there were "circumcised with a circumcision made without hands, by putting off the body of the flesh, by the circumcision of Christ, having been buried with him in baptism, in which you were also raised with him through faith in the powerful working of God, who raised him from the dead" (Colossians 2:11-12). Circumcision and baptism, laid side by side, the old sign and the new one, both burial, both belonging. If baptism now stands where circumcision stood, the paedobaptist reasons, then it goes where circumcision went, onto the children of the faithful. This is the case Calvin argued and the Westminster divines confessed and Rome and the Lutherans and the Anglicans hold in their own keys: that grace precedes our grasping of it, that the covenant has always gathered up the households of believers, and that a baby at the font is the gospel preached in its purest form, a person receiving a love he did nothing to earn and could not yet even want.

The households press the same point. When Lydia comes to faith by the river, Luke writes that "after she was baptized, and her household as well, she urged us" (Acts 16:15). No mention of who in that household believed. When the Philippian jailer is converted in the middle of the night, "he was baptized at once, he and all his family" (Acts 16:33). In the ancient world a household was not a room of individual deciders. It was a man, his wife, his children, his servants, gathered under him, and when the head turned to Christ the sign came down over the whole house. The paedobaptist sees his practice sitting right there in the text. The credobaptist answers, honestly and not weakly, that the same chapter says the jailer "rejoiced along with his entire household that he had believed in God" (Acts 16:34), and that a believing household is exactly what he would expect. Both men read the same three verses. Both men are telling you the truth about what they see. That is what a second-order question looks like when it is handled without contempt.

The mode, sprinkle or immerse, is the lighter half of this, and I will treat it lightly on purpose, because the church itself has. The word leans toward immersion and the picture of burial leans harder, and the credobaptist is right that we lost something real when the tank became a bowl. But the earliest church did not make it a wall. The Didache, the little manual of church practice from the first or early second century, tells believers to baptize in running water if they have it, in still water if they do not, in cold or warm as they can, and then adds that if they have not enough water for either, they may pour water on the head three times. Pour. That instruction is old, older than most of the creeds, and it tells you the ancient church cared that the water was applied in the name of the triune God more than it cared about the depth. Reasonable Christians have plunged and poured and sprinkled for twenty centuries. I am not going to pretend the depth of the water is the hill the resurrection stands or falls on. It is not.

Here is where I owe you my own hand, because it would be a coward's move to hold this open while hiding that I am standing somewhere in it. I came to Christ out of atheism as a grown man, and I went down into the water as an adult, professing, weeping, knowing exactly what was happening to me. My own baptism was a believer's baptism, and it is one of the fixed stars of my life. I cannot pretend I have no history here. And I am telling you, with that history, that I will still not settle it for you, because I have stood at the fonts of men I know to be more godly than I am, watched them wet the head of a baby, and been unable to call it anything but faith. My experience is a witness. It is not a verdict. If I made my Tuesday morning the rule for the whole church, I would be doing the very thing this kind of question forbids.

And both sides have a temptation crouching at the door, the same instrument turned on each, because the point is not to spare one and gut the other. The credobaptist, so careful to require a real profession, is tempted to make the water a monument to the strength of his own decision, to preach a baptism that is finally about the believer's yes and not about God's prior grace, until the sacrament quietly becomes a work and the man is trusting his choice instead of his Christ. The paedobaptist, so sure the sign belongs on the child, is tempted to presumption, to let the water carry the weight faith was meant to carry, to raise a churchful of the baptized who were never converted and were told all their lives that the font had already handled it. Both temptations are real. I have watched both do their damage. And notice that each side's medicine is the other side's warning, which is one more reason to think the church needs both voices in the room and not one of them exiled.

So I leave you where the New Testament leaves us, with the water and without the rule. A grown man comes up out of a river with his whole professing life in his eyes, and the church says: faithful. A baby is carried to a font he will never remember, marked with a grace he did not ask for, and the church says: faithful. Both of them soaked in the same claim, that they have been buried and raised with Christ and made to belong to a people not their own. You can read this and take your child to the font and remain my brother. You can read this and wait to bring your child down under the water on the day he can speak for himself, and remain my brother. What you cannot do, what none of us gets to do, is treat the water as though it were the cross, or treat the brother on the other bank as though he had missed it. He did not miss it. He is standing in the same river you are, arguing about the depth, both of you already wet.
