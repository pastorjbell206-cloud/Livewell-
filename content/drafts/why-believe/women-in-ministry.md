The question does not usually arrive in a debate. It arrives after the service, in the emptying room.

A woman has just done a thing. She stood up in front of your people and opened the text, and the room went quiet the way it only goes when someone is actually being fed, and old men who have heard a thousand sermons leaned in, and something true got said better than most of the men you have watched try. And then the room clears, and she finds you by the door, and she asks the question she has carried up the aisle. Was I allowed to do that. Not was it good. She knows it was good. The people told her so on the way out. She wants to know whether the thing that just happened, the thing God appeared to use, was obedience or trespass. I have stood in that emptying room. I did not have a clean answer for her then, and I am not going to pretend I have grown one since.

So I am not going to tell you where I land, and I want to say why before you suspect a dodge. This is a second-order question. It divides complementarian churches from egalitarian ones, and it divides them hard, but it divides people who confess the same Christ, the same cross, the same empty tomb, the same grace none of us earned. The creeds do not bind it. Chalcedon says nothing about who may stand behind the pulpit. A church can teach either answer and still preach a gospel you could stake your life on. That is the category we are in. Not the deity of Christ, where the church has drawn a line and had to. This is the other kind of hard, where two faithful traditions read the whole Bible, love the same Lord, and come out in different rooms, and the honest thing is to hand you both at full strength and refuse to pretend one of them is stupid.

Start with the complementarian case, and refuse the cartoon of it first. The caricature says these are men protecting their turf, who think women are less intelligent, less spiritual, a rung down the ladder of being. No serious complementarian believes anything like that, and if that is the version in your head you have never actually heard the case.

Here is the case. It does not begin with the fall. It begins before it, in a garden with no sin in it yet. When Paul tells Timothy how the assembly is to be ordered, he does not reach for the curse and he does not reach for the culture of Ephesus. He reaches all the way back to the sixth day. "I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man; rather, she is to remain quiet" (1 Timothy 2:12). And then the ground he stands it on: "For Adam was formed first, then Eve" (1 Timothy 2:13). Read what he did there. He could have said the women in Ephesus were causing a specific problem, and he did not. He anchored the instruction in the order of creation itself, before there was any sin to correct, which means the pattern is not a wound the gospel came to heal. It is a design the gospel came to restore. The complementarian takes that seriously because Paul took it seriously, and grounding a command in Eden rather than in a local mess is Paul telling you the reach of the thing.

And the pattern holds when Paul lists the men who will lead. The overseer, the elder, is described in terms that assume a man: "the husband of one wife" (1 Timothy 3:2). Not as an insult to women but as a shape, a particular calling given a particular form, the way a father is a particular thing a mother is not, and no one insults a mother by saying she is not the father. The complementarian vision, at its best, is not a ladder at all. It is equal worth in distinct roles, and its holders insist on that word equal with everything they have. They will point you to the life inside God himself, where the Son submits to the Father and is in no way less than the Father, fully God, eternally beloved, and yet ordered under him, so that submission and equality are revealed to be not enemies but the deepest grain of reality. The men who wrote the Danvers Statement in 1987, who gathered under the banner of what they called biblical manhood and womanhood, were not trying to shrink women. They believed they were receiving an order they did not invent and had no authority to revise, and that the loving thing, the true thing, was to keep it and not to congratulate themselves for improving on God.

Now turn, and give the other side the identical refusal to caricature. Because the egalitarian is not the person who reads the Bible with an eraser, cutting out what the age finds embarrassing. That is the cartoon, and it is just as false.

The egalitarian begins somewhere else in the same Bible, at a wall that came down. "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28). And then they do the thing that is harder to answer than any single verse. They walk you through the actual women in the actual pages. Paul commends "our sister Phoebe, a servant of the church at Cenchreae" (Romans 16:1), and the word under servant is diakonos, deacon, the same office he uses of himself. When a gifted preacher named Apollos got the gospel not quite right, it was a married couple who corrected him, and the wife is named first: "Priscilla and Aquila heard him, they took him aside and explained to him the way of God more accurately" (Acts 18:26). Philip "had four unmarried daughters, who prophesied" (Acts 21:9), and to prophesy in the assembly is to speak God's word to God's gathered people, which is most of what preaching is. And Paul sends greetings to a woman named Junia: "they are well known to the apostles" (Romans 16:7). For the first thousand years of the church the fathers read that name as a woman, and John Chrysostom, no soft liberal, marveled that a woman had been counted worthy of the name apostle. It was only later that translators, sure the text could not mean what it said, changed her into a man named Junias, a name otherwise unknown in the ancient world.

Then the egalitarian turns the complementarian's own strongest text back toward him. If women are simply to be silent, why does Paul, in a letter he wrote himself, regulate how "every wife who prays or prophesies" (1 Corinthians 11:5) should do it in the gathered church, assuming as settled that she does. And what was actually happening in Ephesus when Paul wrote to Timothy, a city ruled by the temple of Artemis, a cult led by women, drowning in exactly the false teaching Paul spends the whole letter fighting. Read the sentence before the prohibition and you find something a first-century man was not supposed to say: "Let a woman learn" (1 Timothy 2:11). The egalitarian, the scholar like Gordon Fee or Craig Keener, does not delete the restriction. They read it as Paul silencing untaught women spreading a specific error in a specific church, the way you would tell any untaught person to sit down and learn before they teach, and they hold that local correction next to Galatians and Phoebe and Junia and refuse to let one verse in Ephesus overrule the pattern of the whole.

Now let me put the two of them in the same room, because that is where they actually live, and refuse to let either off the hook.

To the egalitarian, the complementarian says: you keep telling me the ban was local, tied to Ephesus and Artemis and a specific mess. But read what Paul actually reached for. He did not say the women there were causing trouble. He reached past Ephesus, past the whole first century, back to the order of creation, to Adam formed first in a garden with no false teaching in it. You have given me a brilliant reading of the context. Paul gave you Eden. Whose context is that. If the reason is creation and not culture, then your local explanation has to answer why the apostle grounded a passing correction in the permanent shape of the world.

To the complementarian, the egalitarian says: if the silence is universal and permanent, then explain your own apostle to me. In the same breath he tells women to keep quiet, he greets a woman deacon, salutes a woman known to the apostles, corrects his theology by way of a woman named first, and regulates the women who prophesy out loud in the assembly. You have told me which verse is the rule and which is the exception. Are you sure you have them the right way round. Are you sure the thing you are defending as the order of creation is not, at least in part, the order of the world you happened to be handed.

Neither of them has a clean answer. That is not my failure to find one. That is the shape of the thing.

And here is where each is tempted to overreach, and I have seen both up close, so I will not point from outside. A complementarianism held wrong does not stay a doctrine of equal worth. It curdles. It becomes a room where a man never has to listen to a woman and calls the not-listening faithfulness, where a woman who can plainly teach is felt as a threat to be managed, where distinct roles quietly shrinks to a smaller and smaller room until the equal has bled out of it and only the ranking is left. I have sat in those rooms. I have nodded in them. And an egalitarianism held wrong does the opposite thing and it is no better. It starts treating the hard text as an embarrassment to be handled, a relic of a darker age Paul would surely renounce if he could see us now, until Scripture stops being the thing that judges the culture and becomes a mirror that only ever reflects what the culture already decided, and the word of God is quietly demoted to the amen of the age. One tribe makes a footstool of women. The other makes a footstool of the text. Both call it obedience. I have been near enough to each to know I could have been at home in either.

I told you I would not tell you where I stand on this, and I will keep that, because it is one of the questions the church has held open with its eyes open, where a woman who submitted to a conviction and served faithfully in the room she was given and a woman who believed God had called her to preach and answered him are both, the church has dared to say, faithful daughters. If your position requires you to make a fool of the woman on the other side, or a fool of Paul, your position is smaller than the Bible it claims.

So I owe that woman by the door an answer, and the truest one I have is not the one she wanted. The church that has read this book longer than either of us has been alive has not agreed. It has produced her, and produced the men who would tell her to sit down, and called them all brothers and sisters, and gone on breaking the same bread. What I can tell her is that the thing that happened in that room, the quiet, the leaning in, the true word landing, was not nothing, and any doctrine that makes it nothing should frighten the one who holds it. And what I cannot tell her, what I will not manufacture to end the ache, is a verse that closes the case. Paul reached back to Eden. Paul greeted Junia. Both are on the page. Both are Scripture. And on this side of the kingdom they have not, for two thousand years, resolved into a single rule you can hand a woman in an emptying room and settle what God was doing when he used her.
