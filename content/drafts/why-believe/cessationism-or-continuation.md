The first time I prayed for someone to be healed, part of me was waiting to see if the machines would change their numbers.

I had my hand on a dying man's shoulder, and I said the words the Bible tells me to say, and the oldest part of me, the part that spent a whole young life certain no one was listening, watched the monitor. Nothing moved. I have also sat across from a woman who told me, without theater, that she had been prayed over and a thing her doctors could not account for had simply lifted, and I could not prove she was lying and did not think she was. I came to faith out of atheism. That means the miraculous is not a doctrine I inherited and quietly stopped examining. It is the exact seam where my old life and my new one still rub against each other, and the rubbing has never fully stopped.

So when a Christian asks me whether the gifts of the Spirit still happen, the tongues and the prophecy and the healing that run all through the book of Acts, I know they are not asking me a trivia question. They are asking whether the God who acted then acts now, or whether he has gone quiet on purpose. And I have to tell them the same thing I am about to tell you. The church has not agreed. It has divided into serious, Bible-loving traditions that read the same New Testament and walk out into different rooms, and it has never once resolved the division into a rule you could carry into a hospital hallway and lean on.

Let me name what kind of question this is before I say anything else, because the category decides how hard you are allowed to hold it. This is a third-order question. It divides Pentecostal churches from charismatic ones from cessationist ones, and it divides them in earnest, but it divides people who confess the same Christ, the same cross, the same grace none of us earned. No creed binds it. A church can teach that the gifts stopped, or teach that they continue, and still preach a gospel you could build your life on. That is the ground we are standing on. Not the deity of Christ, where the church has drawn a line and had to. This is the other kind of hard, where two faithful families love the same Lord, read the whole Book, and land in different places. The honest thing is to hand you both at full strength and refuse to pretend either one is foolish.

Start with the cessationist, and refuse the cartoon first. The caricature says the cessationist is a dried-up rationalist who has explained the Holy Spirit into a footnote and would be embarrassed if God actually did something. No serious cessationist believes anything close to that. If that is the version in your head, you have never heard the case.

Here is the case. The cessationist begins with a question about what the miraculous gifts were for. In the New Testament, signs and wonders travel with revelation. They accredit the messenger. When the writer of Hebrews describes how the gospel reached the second generation, he says it "was declared at first by the Lord, and it was attested to us by those who heard, while God also bore witness by signs and wonders and various miracles and by gifts of the Holy Spirit distributed according to his will" (Hebrews 2:3-4). Notice the logic. The signs bore witness. Paul says the church is "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone" (Ephesians 2:20), and a foundation, by its nature, is laid once. The cessationist reasons from there. If the miraculous and revelatory gifts existed to accredit new revelation, then when the revelation was complete, when the last apostle died and the canon of Scripture closed, the accrediting work was done, and the gifts that served it were done too. B. B. Warfield laid this out at length in Counterfeit Miracles in 1918, arguing that the age of miracles belonged to the age of the apostles and the founding of the church, and that later claims of miracle-working would not survive honest scrutiny.

And underneath the argument is a pastoral fear worth honoring, not sneering at. The cessationist has watched what happens when people hunger for a fresh word more than they trust the finished one. He has seen the manipulation, the healer who blames the sick man's faith when the healing does not come, the prophecy that flatters the crowd, the Bible left closed on the lap while someone waits for a whisper. He has concluded that the sufficiency of Scripture is a wall worth defending, because the alternative is a church tossed around by whoever claims the hottest line to heaven. When Warfield wrote, and when careful Reformed voices after him, like Richard Gaffin in Perspectives on Pentecost, pressed the same case, the point underneath was not a small God. It was a settled God, who has spoken finally in his Son and in the Scripture that testifies to him, and who does not need a stream of new signs to prop up a word that is already complete. That is not unbelief. To the people who hold it, it is reverence.

Now turn, and give the other side the identical refusal to caricature. Because the continuationist is not a gullible enthusiast chasing goosebumps. That is the cartoon, and it is just as false.

The continuationist begins with a simple observation about the text. Nowhere does the New Testament put an expiration date on the gifts. The chapters that deal with the gifts most directly, 1 Corinthians 12 through 14, do not read like a description of a temporary phase. They read like instructions for an ongoing life. Paul does not say the Corinthians once had these gifts. He tells them how to use them without wrecking the church, and then he says, plainly, "Pursue love, and earnestly desire the spiritual gifts, especially that you may prophesy" (1 Corinthians 14:1). That is a command in the present tense, aimed at a church and, the continuationist argues, at every church after it. A few lines later Paul writes, "earnestly desire to prophesy, and do not forbid speaking in tongues" (1 Corinthians 14:39). It is difficult to obey a command to earnestly desire something you have been told God withdrew.

And there is the famous passage the cessationist leans on, which the continuationist reads the other way. Paul writes that "as for prophecies, they will pass away; as for tongues, they will cease; as for knowledge, it will pass away" (1 Corinthians 13:8), and then gives the timing: "but when the perfect comes, the partial will pass away" (1 Corinthians 13:10). The whole question is what the perfect is. The cessationist has sometimes read it as the completed canon. But look at what Paul says next, in the same breath: "For now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known" (1 Corinthians 13:12). Face to face. Known as fully as God knows me. That is not the arrival of a book, the continuationist says. That is the return of Christ. And if the gifts pass away when we see him face to face, then they were meant to run until that day, not until the last page of Revelation was copied out.

Serious Reformed scholars have made a careful case for it. D. A. Carson, in Showing the Spirit, worked through 1 Corinthians 12 through 14 and concluded that the burden of proof sits on the one who says the gifts have ceased, because the text nowhere says so. Wayne Grudem has argued at length for the continuation of prophecy, understood not as new Scripture but as a lesser, fallible gift that must always be tested against the Book that cannot fail. And then there is the plain fact of the last hundred years. Much of the explosive growth of the church across the global south came through Christians who prayed expecting God to act and testified that he did. The movement that traces back to the Azusa Street revival of 1906 is now counted in the hundreds of millions. The continuationist offers that not as proof but as a fact the cessationist has to explain, because it is hard to say the Spirit stopped giving gifts while looking at where the church is actually growing.

There is a middle, and it deserves to be named honestly rather than used as a hiding place. Many thoughtful Christians land in a posture often called open but cautious. They cannot find the verse that shuts the gifts off, so they will not say God does not still give them. But they have seen too much abuse to go looking for signs, and they read the New Testament's own commands to weigh and to test as the reason for their caution. They hold the door open and stand back from it, unwilling to deny what Scripture never denied and unwilling to chase what it never told them to chase. It is not a dodge. It may be the most exact reading of a Bible that both refuses to end the gifts and refuses to let them run wild.

Now let me put them in the same room, because that is where they actually live, and refuse to let either off the hook. And I have to start with myself, because I have leaned both ways in the same week.

To the continuationist, the cessationist says: you say you are earnestly desiring the gifts, but look at what your movement has produced when it stops testing. You have people manufacturing a spiritual experience and calling it the Spirit, prophets who are never wrong because no one is allowed to check them, a hunger for the next sign that has quietly grown louder than your hunger for the God who already spoke. You call it faith. Are you sure it is not appetite. This is the continuationist's besetting temptation, and it has a name he does not like to say: credulity, the spiritual theater that mistakes intensity for the presence of God, and every honest continuationist has watched it happen and winced.

To the cessationist, the continuationist says: you say you are guarding the sufficiency of Scripture, but look at what your reasoning does to your Tuesday. You have built a theology in which the God of the Bible acted powerfully for one generation and then, functionally, went silent, and you pray without expecting an answer you would recognize, and you call your low expectations maturity. You have a doctrine of the Spirit and a life that could run without him. That is the cessationist's besetting temptation, and it also has a name: a functional deism that has boxed God into the first century, that believes in principle he could act and in practice would be startled if he did. And I know that temptation from the inside, because the old atheist in me finds it the most comfortable room in the house. It asks nothing of me. It lets the machine stay silent and calls the silence theology.

Neither side has a clean answer to the other. That is not my failure to find one.

And here is what the honest reader has to sit with. The New Testament that will not put an expiration date on the gifts is the same New Testament that will not let you swallow every claim uncritically. To one church Paul writes both halves in a single breath: "Do not quench the Spirit. Do not despise prophecies, but test everything; hold fast what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:19-21). Do not quench. Do not despise. And also, test everything. The text hands the eager one a warning and the cautious one a rebuke, in the same sentence, and walks away without telling you which clause is load-bearing. The traditions that go to war over the gifts are both quoting a Book that refused to take their side.

I told you the miraculous is the seam where my two lives still rub, and I am not going to pretend I have sewn it shut. I will not tell you the gifts have ceased. I cannot find the verse, and I have met the woman I could not prove wrong. I will not tell you to go chase them either, because I have watched what the chasing does to people who stopped testing, and I have felt in my own chest how easily a man can want a sign more than he wants God. You are trusted here. You can close this page a cessationist and remain my brother. You can close it a continuationist and remain my brother. What neither of us gets to do is treat the other one as a fool for standing where the church has let faithful people stand.

So I leave you where the hospital room leaves me. My hand is still on the shoulder. I do not know, going in, whether God will act the way Acts describes or hold his peace, and I have stopped pretending I know. I only know I am not allowed to close the door in either direction, because the God who could has not told me to, and the God who does has not asked my permission. He is quieter than the continuationist sometimes promises and louder than the cessationist sometimes allows, and he has never once let me settle which. I have decided to keep praying with my hands open and my eyes open, which is the only honest posture left to a man who used to be sure the room was empty and is now only sure that it is not.
