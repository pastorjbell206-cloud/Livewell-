Nobody talks themselves out of God the way the debates pretend. There is no evening when you weigh the cosmological argument against the problem of evil, tally the columns, and conclude that the universe is unattended. That is not how unbelief actually happens, and I say that as someone who lived inside it for most of my life. Unbelief happens the way weather happens. You grow up breathing an air in which God is simply not a live presence, in which the burden of proof sits permanently on the believer and never on the doubter, and one day you notice you stopped expecting anyone to be there. The philosopher Charles Taylor gave this the best name it has. He called it the immanent frame, the sense, so deep we no longer feel it as a choice, that the world is a closed system of natural causes with no window cut in the ceiling. Taylor's point, in his history of how the West got secular, is that five hundred years ago belief was the water everyone swam in and unbelief was nearly unthinkable, and now the position has exactly reversed, and the reversal happened underneath argument, not through it. I did not reason my way out. I was raised in the frame. It felt like clear sight. It was a set of walls I had mistaken for the horizon.

So I am not going to try to reason you into anything. You cannot be argued into the kingdom, and anyone selling a proof that closes the case like a math problem is selling something that has never once worked on a living person. What I can do is tell you the thing I did not expect, which is that the frame started to cost more than I could afford. The atheism I held was not a neutral absence of belief. It was a full account of everything, and that account kept running up debts it quietly refused to pay.

Let me name the strongest version of the case I was defending, because a version you can knock over is not worth leaving. The most serious atheism is not the sneering internet kind. It is the one that says: the world looks exactly as we would expect it to look if no one were in charge. The innocent suffer at random. The universe is vast beyond meaning and indifferent beyond appeal. If there were a God who wanted to be known, he has done a strange job of it, hiding behind a silence that looks identical to absence. The philosopher J. L. Schellenberg built a rigorous argument out of that last point alone, that a perfectly loving God would not permit the honest seeker to find only silence, and the silence is real, and so perhaps the God is not. I felt that argument in my body long before I could name it. Do not let anyone tell you it is weak. The hiddenness of God is the best card the other side holds, and pretending otherwise is how believers lose the honest people in the room.

Grant it. Grant the silence and the suffering and the sheer indifferent scale of things. Now watch what my old account had to spend to keep the ledger closed.

It started with the simplest question anyone can ask, the one Leibniz put three centuries ago and no one has retired since: why is there something rather than nothing. Not why is there this particular something, but why is there anything at all, why is there a universe here to be indifferent instead of the clean silence of nothing whatever. The honest atheist answer is that there is no answer, that the universe is just there, a brute fact with no account of itself. And maybe that is where it ends. But notice that this is not a discovery. It is a refusal to keep asking, dressed as maturity. I had called my refusal courage. It was closer to a man who stops opening the mail.

Then there was the fine-tuning, which I first heard and dismissed and could not make stay dismissed. The physical constants that govern the universe, the strength of gravity, the balance of the forces that let matter clump into stars instead of flying apart or collapsing, sit in a range so narrow that a small nudge in almost any of them yields a cosmos with no stars, no chemistry, no observers, nothing. The naturalist has a reply, and it is a real one: perhaps there are countless other universes with other settings, and we necessarily find ourselves in one of the rare ones that works, because we could not find ourselves anywhere else. A multiverse. But sit with what that costs. To avoid the conclusion that the one universe we can observe looks set up, we posit an infinity of universes we cannot observe, in principle, ever. That is not the humble empirical move. That is an enormous act of faith wearing a lab coat, and I had been making it without noticing that it was faith.

The bill I could least afford came due over something smaller and closer than cosmology. It came due over the fact that I could not actually live as though cruelty were merely something I disliked. When I read of a child tortured for someone's pleasure, everything in me said not I disapprove of this but this is wrong, wrong in a way that would still be wrong if every person on earth applauded it, wrong the way a false equation is false regardless of the vote. C. S. Lewis pressed exactly here, and I resented it before I conceded it. If there is no God, then that overwhelming sense of a real moral law standing over me and over the torturer alike is a chemical event in a primate brain, useful for keeping the tribe together, and nothing more. It does not report a truth. It only feels like it does. I could say that sentence. I could not live it. I went on treating some things as really evil and some things as really owed, and every time I did, I was writing a check on an account my worldview swore was empty.

That is the pattern I did not see until I was most of the way out. The atheism I held could be stated, but it could not be inhabited. It told me the universe was meaningless, and then could not stop me grieving a death as an outrage rather than a rearrangement of molecules. My reason, on that account, was the accidental byproduct of blind selection, and I was asked to trust it anyway to deliver the truth about everything, including the truth of atheism, which is a little like trusting a random number generator because it assured you it was reliable. And the person across the table was a bag of chemicals I was nonetheless expected to treat as though she had a dignity no chemistry confers. At every one of those points the account said one thing and I lived the other, and I had called the gap between them realism. It was not realism. It was a man refusing to notice that he already believed the world was the kind of place that only makes sense if someone made it.

None of this is a proof, and I will not insult you by calling it one. The arguments do not march anyone across the line at gunpoint, and they are not meant to. Blaise Pascal saw this better than the debaters do. He said God has given enough light for those who desire to see, and enough darkness for those who do not, that the evidence is calibrated not to overwhelm a mind but to meet a heart that is actually looking. That used to sound to me like an excuse for a God who would not show up. It sounds different now. It sounds like the difference between a proof, which compels a mind and changes nothing else, and a person, who can only be known by those willing to be changed by the knowing.

Scripture does not argue for God's existence. It is worth noticing that. It never once treats the matter as an open question to be settled by evidence, and for years I read that as evasion. Now I read it as a diagnosis. Paul, writing to Rome, says that what can be known of God has been plain, that "his invisible attributes, namely, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world, in the things that have been made. So they are without excuse" (Romans 1:20). The claim there is not that the argument is airtight. The claim is that the problem was never mainly the evidence. The problem is that we are practiced, all of us, believer and atheist alike, at not seeing what we would rather not owe. I know that practice from the inside. I was very good at it.

So I am not going to hand you a syllogism and tell you the debt is settled. I am going to tell you what I found on the other side of the frame I mistook for the sky. The question is not really whether you can prove God, because you cannot prove, in that hard sense, most of the things you would die for. The question is which account can carry the weight of what you already know and cannot stop knowing: that some things are truly wrong, that your mind can reach real truth, that the person beside you is more than meat, that the universe's being here at all is stranger than your silence about it admits. Atheism can say those things are illusions. It cannot get you to live as though they were. I tried, longer than most. In the end the unbelief was not too rational to keep. It was too expensive, and I had been paying, all along, in a currency it told me did not exist.
