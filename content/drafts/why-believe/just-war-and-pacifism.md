The question does not usually arrive in a seminar. It arrives at a door.

Someone is on the other side of it, and they mean to come through, and behind you in the house are the people you would die for. You have seconds. And in those seconds a man who spent his life reading the Sermon on the Mount has to decide whether the words he has underlined a hundred times mean what they seem to mean, or whether there is an exception written in ink he cannot see. I have five sons. I know exactly which way my body wants to move. What I do not know, and what I want to be honest that I do not know, is whether the wanting is obedience or the oldest sin dressed as love.

So I am not going to tell you where I land. Not because I am hedging. Because the church has held this both ways for two thousand years, has canonized saints on both sides, and has never once resolved it into a rule you can carry into that hallway. The most honest thing I can do is hand you both cases at full strength and refuse to spare you the weight of choosing.

Start with the case for peace, and refuse the cartoon of it first. Pacifism is not the belief that violence is unpleasant. Everyone believes that. It is the belief that Jesus meant it.

Read the words without the escape hatch already installed. "But I say to you, Do not resist the one who is evil. But if anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also" (Matthew 5:39). And a few sentences later: "But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). The pacifist reads these and asks a question the rest of us work very hard not to ask. What did he mean by enemy. Not the annoying neighbor. The Greek word carries the enemy who wants you dead, the occupying soldier, the one with the sword. Jesus is not describing an unpleasant coworker. He is describing Rome. And into a crowd that had every earthly reason to take up arms against an empire, an armed rebellion any of them would have called just, he said love them, and he did not add a footnote for the hard cases.

Then he did the thing that is harder to argue with than any verse. In the garden, when the soldiers came for him, one of his own men drew a sword and used it, and struck, and drew blood in the one situation every one of us would call justified. This was not aggression. This was the defense of an innocent man, the most innocent who ever lived, against an unjust arrest by a mob in the dark. If self-defense is ever righteous, it was righteous there. And Jesus stopped him. "Put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword" (Matthew 26:52). He healed the wound his defender had inflicted. Then he let them take him. The pacifist stands on that ground and asks the rest of us a question we cannot easily answer: if the sword was wrong there, in the clearest case of just defense in human history, where exactly does it become right.

And this is not a modern softness invented by people who never bled. For its first centuries the church leaned hard against the sword. Tertullian, writing around the year 200, argued that when Christ disarmed Peter he disarmed every soldier, that the Christian could not serve in an army whose trade was killing. Origen, answering the pagan Celsus a generation later, said plainly that Christians do not take up arms against anyone, that they fight instead by prayer. The historical record is not perfectly clean, and honest scholars argue over how many Christians were in the legions. But the moral center of gravity in the first three centuries pulled away from the sword, and it pulled that way while the church was being fed to lions, which is to say it was not the pacifism of the comfortable. It was the conviction of people who were themselves the ones being killed and still would not kill back.

The modern voice that pressed this hardest was John Howard Yoder, whose 1972 book The Politics of Jesus argued that the church misread its whole vocation the day it decided its job was to run the empire responsibly rather than to be a different kind of people inside it. Jesus, Yoder said, was not a spiritual teacher indifferent to politics. He was intensely political, and his politics was the cross, a refusal to secure the good by the world's means. Stanley Hauerwas carried the same fire into a thousand lecture halls: the first task of the church is not to make the world's history come out right, because that was never something God asked us to control. The first task of the church is to be the church, a community that can afford to lose, because it has already been told how the story ends. The pacifist is not naive about evil. He simply refuses to believe that the answer to a cross is a sword, when the whole faith turns on a man who took the first and would not take the second.

That is the case for peace, and it should cost you something to read it. Now turn, and give the other side the same refusal to caricature. Because the just war tradition is not the church baptizing what it wanted to do anyway. It began as an attempt to say no.

Augustine did not develop it to unleash violence. He developed it to leash it, in a world where the empire that had finally stopped feeding Christians to lions was now the only thing standing between ordinary people and the men who would burn their villages to the ground. And Augustine asked a question the pacifist has to answer and often does not: what do you owe the person being murdered in front of you. Love your enemy, yes. But your neighbor is also being killed by that enemy, and love has a second object in the room. To stand by with clean hands while the defenseless are slaughtered is not obviously innocence. It may be a way of loving your own purity more than your neighbor's life. The just war tradition was born from taking that question seriously.

Thomas Aquinas gave it the shape the church has argued over ever since, in the Summa Theologiae, laying down the tests: a just cause, a legitimate authority rather than a private grudge, and a right intention aimed at peace rather than plunder. Later hands added the rest, the criteria a war must pass before it can be called just: it must be a last resort, its violence proportional to the wrong it answers, waged with reasonable hope and never with the deliberate targeting of the innocent. Read that list honestly and notice what it is. It is not permission. It is a wall of conditions so high that almost no war humans have actually fought could climb it. The just war tradition, done right, disqualifies far more than it blesses. It is the church's way of saying that if force is ever used, it is used the way a surgeon uses a knife, under strict limit, to save a body, and never for the joy of the cut.

And there is a text the tradition will not let the pacifist read past. Paul, writing to Christians under the very empire that would eventually kill him, says of the governing authority that "he does not bear the sword in vain. For he is the servant of God, an avenger who carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer" (Romans 13:4). Paul is not describing a spiritual sword. He is describing the coercive power of the state, and he is calling that power, restrained and just, a servant of God. The just war Christian stands there and asks the pacifist: if all sword-bearing is sin, why does Paul call the sword-bearer God's servant. There is a place, the tradition says, for a soldier who steps between a killer and a child, and calling that soldier a murderer is a moral confusion that the Sermon on the Mount, rightly read, never asked for.

Now let me put the two questions in the same room, because that is where they actually live, and refuse to let either off the hook.

To the pacifist, the just war Christian says: your hands are clean, but are they clean because you are righteous or because someone else did the bleeding you were unwilling to do. When the killers come and you will not lift a hand, it is not only your life you are laying down. It may be your daughter's. You have called that faithfulness. Are you sure it is not just the luxury of never having been the only thing standing between evil and the people it wants.

To the just war Christian, the pacifist says: every empire that ever marched believed its cause was just. Every soldier was told he was the one protecting the child. You have given the sword a doctrine, and the doctrine has never once, in all of history, stopped a nation from finding its war on the approved list. You say you use force like a surgeon. The graveyards say you use it like everyone else and quote Aquinas afterward. Where is the war your tradition actually stopped.

Neither of them has a clean answer. That is the point I will not spare you.

And both have paid. Do not imagine this is a debate between people who have risked nothing. The martyr paid, the Christian who would not raise a hand and was killed for it, and the church counts that blood holy. The soldier paid too, the believer who went to the border and stood between his town and the men who meant to erase it and did not come home, and the church has buried him with honor and meant it. Two graves. Both of them Christian. Both of them, the church has dared to say, faithful. If your position requires you to spit on one of those graves, your position is too small for the people who are actually in them.

I told you I would not tell you where I stand, and I will keep that, because this is one of the questions the church has held open with its eyes open, not out of cowardice but out of the honesty to admit that the Sermon on the Mount and the letter to the Romans are both Scripture and both true and do not, on this side of the kingdom, resolve into a single command you can hand a soldier or a martyr and settle the matter. You are trusted here. You can read this and pick up the rifle and remain my brother. You can read this and lay it down and remain my brother. What you cannot do, what none of us can do, is pretend the other one made the easy choice.

So I leave you where the garden leaves us. A sword, drawn in the one case we would all call just. A voice telling the man to put it away. And a cross that the man on it could have called ten thousand angels down to prevent, and did not. I do not fully understand what that scene demands of a man standing between an intruder and his children. I only know that whichever way I move, I will not get to move as though it were obvious. He did not leave it obvious. He left it costly, on purpose, and told us to follow him into a garden where the right thing to do with a sword is the hardest question a Christian ever has to hold.
