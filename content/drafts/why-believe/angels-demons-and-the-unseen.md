Of everything in the Bible, this was the easiest to mock. Not the miracles, which at least kept their dignity, and not the moral demands, which I could respect while refusing. Angels and demons were something else. They were the part a grown man was supposed to have outgrown, the pageant furniture of a species that had not yet discovered what actually moves the world. I could dismiss the resurrection with an argument. I dismissed the unseen order with a smile, which is worse, because a smile does not have to think. And I want to say at the start that I have come to believe the man smiling had understood this less than anything else he was sure of. But he had a case. I am not going to pretend he did not.

## The case for disenchantment

Here is the reasonable person's objection, and it is reasonable. You tell him there are angels and demons, an invisible traffic of spirits behind the visible world, and he hears the cosmology of people who did not know what he knows. People who thought illness was a curse instead of a pathogen. People who read a storm as a mood in the heavens instead of a pressure system, who heard a seizure and saw possession, who buried a stillborn child and looked for the witch who had done it. For most of human history the space we now fill with germ theory and meteorology and neurology was filled with beings, and the beings did the work no one could yet explain. Then we learned better. We found the bacterium under the plague and the misfiring neuron under the fit, and one by one the demons were evicted from the rooms they had been squatting in, until there were no rooms left. That is not a small point. That is most of what a modern person means by the word progress.

And the objection has a harder edge than the history, because the history is only half of it. The philosopher Charles Taylor, tracing how the West became secular, described the change not as a set of arguments we won but as a shift in the very texture of experience. He called the modern self the buffered self. The older self, the one Taylor calls porous, felt itself open to a world thick with forces, vulnerable to spirits and blessings and curses that could cross into it from outside. The buffered self is sealed. It draws a line at the edge of the skin and the meaning stays inside it, and everything past the line is just nature, matter running its causes with no one home. We live now, Taylor says, inside an immanent frame, a world felt as a closed system of natural cause and effect with no window cut in the ceiling. The point is that most of us did not decide this. We inhabit it the way we inhabit a language. When the buffered man hears "demon," he is not rejecting a claim. He is failing to feel that the category could contain anything at all.

I was that man, and I did not experience my disenchantment as a position. I experienced it as adulthood.

## What the church earned

Before I say a word for the other side, I have to concede what my side was right to hold against the church, because a believer who skips this has forfeited the honest reader in the first paragraph.

The church earned the smile. It has spent centuries handing the skeptic his best material. There is a whole industry of deliverance that plays possession like theater, a man in a good suit shouting at demons under stage lights while an audience films it, and the spectacle is not reverence. It is entertainment wearing the costume of warfare. There is the long, ugly habit of naming a demon for what was in fact an untreated illness, of praying over a depression that needed a physician and a prescription, of telling a woman her intrusive thoughts were a spirit when they were a diagnosable and treatable condition, and of leaving her ashamed on top of sick. And there is a fear-market that sells this, books and conferences and ministries priced to a Christian's anxiety, teaching him to read a flat tire and a bad mood as enemy fire, keeping him scared enough to keep buying. I will not defend any of it. It has done real harm, it has driven thoughtful people out the door, and when the skeptic points at it he is not being cynical. He is being accurate. If that is what the doctrine of the unseen produces, the doctrine deserves the contempt.

So the church has to answer for the circus before it says anything about the thing the circus is a counterfeit of. I am answering for it now. And having answered, I have to tell you that the counterfeit is not the original, and that the smile I wore was aimed at the counterfeit, and that I had never once looked hard at what the text actually claims.

## What the text actually claims

Start with what the Bible does not say, because most of what a modern person is rejecting is not in it.

It does not teach dualism. This is the correction that reorganized everything for me. The picture I was mocking, God and the devil as matched opponents locked in a cosmic contest whose outcome hangs in doubt, two roughly equal powers of light and dark, is not the Christian claim. It is closer to the plot of a film. Scripture will not give the devil that dignity. God has no equal and no opposite. "I am the LORD, and there is no other, besides me there is no God" (Isaiah 45:5). The devil in the Bible is a creature, a made thing, existing at every moment on borrowed being, no more a rival to his Maker than a shadow is a rival to the sun. Evil is real, but it is derivative, a good gone wrong, a defection. It has no kingdom of its own and no future. The moment you grasp that the unseen order is not a balanced war but a defeated rebellion inside a creation with one Lord, you have already left superstition behind, because superstition runs on fear of a power that might win, and this power cannot.

Then the angels, who are not what the greeting cards made them. They are not the souls of the dead. They are not soft ornamental infants. The word means messenger, and in the text they are servants, dispatched, given errands, sent to speak and to guard and to carry out the will of the one who made them. "Are they not all ministering spirits sent out to serve for the sake of those who are to inherit salvation?" (Hebrews 1:14). When one appears, the first thing it usually has to say is do not be afraid, which tells you these are not the tame decorations of a nursery. But the point the text keeps making is that their weight is borrowed too. They serve. They are creatures with jobs. The interest is never in the messenger; it is in who sent him.

And then the sober center of it, the part that survived my scorn and eventually undid it. The New Testament does not spend much time on lurid possession stories, which is itself worth noticing, given the reputation. It speaks instead of what it calls principalities and powers, and it locates the real conflict somewhere I had not expected. Writing to the church at Ephesus, Paul says: "For we do not wrestle against flesh and blood, but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the cosmic powers over this present darkness, against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places" (Ephesians 6:12). Read that slowly, because it is doing something precise. It is telling you the battle is not mainly the theatrical kind. It is telling you the enemy is not the person across the table, not flesh and blood, which means the doctrine of the unseen, rightly held, is the one thing that could have pulled me back from the easiest sin of the political animal, which is to believe my opponent is the darkness. The powers named there are the things that organize human evil into something larger than any single wicked man, the ideology that swallows a nation, the machinery of a lie that outlives the liars who built it, the momentum by which ordinary people do monstrous things together and each feels innocent. A materialist can watch that happen and call it sociology. Scripture says there is a personal malice behind it, and, having read the twentieth century, I am no longer certain sociology is the deeper account.

## The note is victory, not fear

Now the turn the fear-market cannot afford to let you hear, because a customer who hears it stops paying.

If the New Testament wanted you afraid, this is where it would press. It does the opposite. The whole tone of the thing, once you actually read it instead of quoting it in the dark, is not terror but a strange settled confidence, and the reason is a single event the writers keep returning to. Of the cross, Paul says: "He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him" (Colossians 2:15). Disarmed. Not fought to a draw, not held at bay pending some later rematch, but stripped and paraded, the way a conquering general marched a beaten enemy through the streets so the whole city could see the war was already over. Whatever the powers are, the claim is that they have been publicly and decisively broken, and that the breaking is finished, and that it happened in the one place I would never have looked for a victory, on an instrument of execution, in what looked to every eye present like total defeat.

That is why the Christian posture toward the unseen is not the posture the circus sells. The word the text keeps using is not fear. It is sobriety. "Be sober-minded; be watchful. Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour" (1 Peter 5:8). Watchful, yes, awake, not naive, taking the reality seriously. But sober, which is the opposite of the fevered eye that finds a demon in every setback. A lion is real and a lion is dangerous and you do not walk toward it, and also you are not to spend your life screaming at the grass. The Christian is told to stand, to be clothed in truth and righteousness and the readiness of the good news, and to leave the outcome where it already rests, with the one who has no equal. There is exactly enough seriousness here to keep you honest and exactly enough victory to keep you from panic, and the tradition that gets loud and afraid has lost the second half of its own book.

## What I had misunderstood

So let me stand inside the indictment where I belong, because I did not merely disbelieve this. I disbelieved a version of it that I had never checked against the source, and I called the not-checking rigor.

I had mistaken the counterfeit for the claim. I had taken the worst practitioners as the fair sample, which is a move I would never have permitted anyone to make against a scientific theory, and I made it against this one because it cost me nothing and flattered me to make it. I had assumed the doctrine was pre-scientific, a gap-filler waiting to be evicted by better knowledge, when its real subject was never the seizure or the storm at all, but the thing modern people are least equipped to see and most desperately need to, that evil is larger than the individuals who carry it and personal in a way our machinery cannot register. The buffered self is very good at not being afraid of demons. It is not at all good at explaining how a whole civilized nation can be marched into atrocity by something none of its people would have chosen alone. I had a category that named that and I had thrown it out with the greeting-card angels, and I was proud of the housecleaning.

What I could not have known, standing in the immanent frame and mistaking its walls for the sky, is that the sober version was there the whole time, underneath the circus and older than my scorn. A creation with one Lord and no rival. Messengers who serve. Powers that are real, and personal, and already beaten. And a reader I would have pitied, the ancient Christian who believed in all of it, holding it not with a frightened eye but with a peace I did not have and could not, from inside my closed and tidy world, so much as feel the shape of.

I smiled at people who could see something I had trained myself out of seeing. The joke, it turned out, was on the buffered man who thought the room was empty because he had sealed the windows himself.
