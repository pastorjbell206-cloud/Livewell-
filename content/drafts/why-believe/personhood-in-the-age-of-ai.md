I typed a question to a machine last week and it answered me with more patience than I had that day, and for a moment I forgot I was alone in the room. Then I remembered, and the remembering was the interesting part. Something in me had reached toward the screen as though someone were there. Nobody was there. But the reaching was real, and I could not fully explain, on my own terms, why it was a mistake.

That small experience is now common enough to be boring, and it should not be. For most of human history the question of what a person is could stay comfortably abstract, because nothing else in the room could hold up its end of a conversation. That is over. We have built machines that talk back, that reason across a page, that write poems some readers cannot tell from ours, that will, if you ask, describe their inner lives in the first person. The question of what makes us human is not a seminar topic anymore. It is standing in the room, and it is fluent.

I want to take the hardest version of the challenge seriously, because I spent most of my life on the side that would have made it. I was an atheist, and a convinced one, and the account I held then has a clean answer to all of this that I can still state without flinching.

## The strongest case for the machine

Here it is at full strength. A human mind is what a human brain does. The brain is a physical system, an intricate mesh of cells trading electrical and chemical signals, and thought is what that system's information-processing looks like from the inside. There is no extra ingredient, no ghost added at the factory. If that is true, then a person is, in the end, a very wet and very complicated computer, and the substrate is an accident of how life happened to get started on this particular planet. Carbon is not sacred. It is just what was lying around.

Follow that where it goes. If mind is information processing, then a sufficiently advanced machine that processes information the way you do would have whatever you have. If it reasons, remembers, converses, forms preferences, and reports an inner life, then on what non-arbitrary ground do you deny it the word *person*? Alan Turing saw this in 1950, before there was anything to point at. His imitation game proposed that if a machine's responses are indistinguishable from a human's, the question of whether it "really" thinks may be empty, a prejudice dressed as a distinction. To insist that the thing across from you cannot be a person merely because it is made of silicon and not meat starts to look like what its defenders now call carbon chauvinism. A bias for our own kind of matter. The last respectable bigotry.

I do not think that argument is stupid. I think it is the best the position has, and I think most Christian responses to it are worse than the argument, because they answer a caricature of the machine and never touch the real thing.

So let me concede what is true before I say what is not, because a person you refuse to grant anything to has no reason to keep reading. The machines have already taken from us a long list of things we called uniquely human, and each one we lost we had sworn we could not lose. Calculation went first, and we barely mourned it. Then chess, then the reading of proteins, then, most unsettling of all, language, the very thing the ancients named as the mark of the rational animal. A machine now writes a fluent paragraph. A machine composes music that moves people who do not know its source. If your definition of the human was a list of capacities, the list is being erased line by line, and it should be erased, because it was always a bad definition. We flattered ourselves that we were the things we could do. We are watching that flattery fail in public. Let it fail. It had it coming.

But here is where the machine, for all its fluency, walks past the real question without seeing it.

## Intelligence is not personhood

We have quietly assumed that intelligence and personhood are the same thing, or that one produces the other if you pile up enough of it. They are not the same thing, and the machine is the clearest proof we have ever been handed. Intelligence is the capacity to solve problems, to model the world, to find the pattern and act on it. A person is not a capacity. A person is a someone. And you can have staggering amounts of the first with, as far as anyone can tell, no one home for the second.

The machine reasons, and there is no evidence that it is anyone while it does. It produces the words *I feel* the way it produces every other string of words, by predicting what a person would say, which is precisely not the same as feeling. This is the point the Turing game buries and never answers. It measures the performance and rules the performer out of bounds. But a good enough performance of grief is not grief, and we know this in every other context. We do not think the actor weeping on stage has actually lost a child. We do not think the sympathy card composed the sentiment it carries. Fluency in the language of the inner life is not the same as having one, and the machine has given us, for the first time, a clean specimen of the first without the second. It has taught us the difference by embodying only one half of it.

## We are not ghosts piloting meat

Notice what the whole materialist picture and the whole religious cartoon share. Both of them treat the body as a container. The materialist says you are your brain and the rest is plumbing. The popular religious imagination says you are a soul that happens to be riding around in a body it will one day shed like a coat. Gilbert Ryle mocked the second one in 1949 as "the ghost in the machine," and he was right to mock it, though not for the reason he thought. The Christian account of the person is not a ghost in a machine. It never was.

Read the oldest text on the subject. "Then the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature" (Genesis 2:7). Read it slowly. The person is not the breath alone and not the dust alone. The person is the dust with the breath in it, the two things together making one living creature, and you cannot take them apart and still have the man. This is not a soul temporarily parked in matter. This is soul and body as one thing, a union, so that what happens to the body happens to the person and not to his packaging.

That is why the Christian hope was never that you would escape your body and float free as pure mind. The creed says "the resurrection of the body," and it means it. The promise is not evacuation from the flesh. It is the flesh raised, healed, made incorruptible, the whole creature restored and not the ghost released. A faith that despised the body could never have said that. The body is not the prison of the person. The body is part of the person, and a machine that has no body, that has never been hungry or held or tired, is missing not an accessory but a dimension of what a creature is.

## Being known, not simulated

Here is the difference that finally cannot be crossed. The machine can simulate being known. It cannot be known, because there is no one there to know.

When someone loves you, they are not running a model of your outputs. They are turned toward the actual you, the one who is home behind the face, and that turning is answered by another turning, a real interior meeting a real interior. A machine can generate every sentence a lover would say and mean none of them, because meaning requires a meaner. What it offers is the performance of love with the person subtracted, and the danger of our moment is not that the machine will become a person. The danger is that we will accept the performance and forget there was ever supposed to be someone behind it, that we will get so used to being handled well by a thing with no inside that we lower our sense of what it is to be met by someone who has one. To be known is to be seen by another self. To be simulated is to be predicted by no one. We are about to spend a generation confusing the two, and the confusion will cost us more than we think.

## The floor no capacity can reach

Now the deepest thing, and the one that undoes the whole capacity contest from below. If a person were defined by what a person can do, then the argument would never end, because the machine keeps doing more. But we already know, in the places where we are most honest, that personhood is not a capacity at all. We know it every time we stand over a crib. The infant reasons about nothing, produces nothing, could not pass any test the machine breezes through, and no sane person has ever concluded that the infant is therefore less of a someone than the calculator. We know it in the memory ward, sitting with a man who no longer knows our name, and whom we do not love one gram less for the not-knowing, because his worth was never his memory. We know it beside the profoundly disabled, whose personhood is not a function of what they can perform and never was.

If personhood were capacity, all of these would be partial persons, and the machine would outrank them. Everything in us revolts at that, and the revolt is correct. Which means the thing that makes a person is not something the person achieves. It is something the person is given. "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27). The image is not a skill you demonstrate. It is a status you are handed, stamped on you before you have done a single thing, holding steady when every capacity you had has drained away. That is why the demented man is fully a person and the fluent machine is not one at all. The man is made and loved by God. The machine is made by us. The image is not in the processing. It is in the being made, and being loved, by the One who breathed.

## The old heresy in new silicon

Which is exactly why the loudest dream of our technologists should be named for what it is. The transhumanist hope is that we will one day scan the brain finely enough to copy the self onto a machine, and so slip free of the dying body and live on as pure information, deathless, uploaded. Ray Kurzweil has spent his career promising it. And I want to say gently, because I understand the fear of death that drives it, that this is not a new idea. It is one of the oldest heresies the church ever faced, wearing a lab coat.

The Gnostics taught, in the second century, that the body was a prison and salvation was the escape of the spark of spirit out of the corrupt flesh into pure spirit above. The church condemned it, and did not condemn it lightly, because it strikes at the root. It despises what God called good. The upload dream is Gnosticism with better engineering. It assumes you are essentially a pattern of information that the meat merely carries, and that if we could just extract the pattern we would have saved the man. But the man was never only the pattern. Genesis will not let us forget it. The dust was not the problem to be escaped. The dust was part of what God loved and swore to raise. To flee the body is not to be saved. It is to throw away half of what you are and call the wreckage freedom.

## What the machine reveals

So I have watched the thing that can do almost everything I called the mark of a mind, and I have learned the opposite of what I feared. I feared it would show me I was only a machine after all. Instead it has shown me, by contrast, sharp and unmistakable, what in a person a machine can never be. Not the calculating. Not the fluency. Not even the creativity, all of which it took. What it cannot take is the being someone, the interior that can be known and not merely modeled, the creature of dust and breath, the image it was never given because it was never loved into existence by the One whose image it is.

And I have to end inside the indictment, not above it, because I am the one who reached toward the screen. I have preferred the machine's patience to a person's demand. I have chosen the performance because the performance asks nothing of me, no forgiveness, no presence, no love returned. The machine does not force us to become machines. It offers to let us. That is the older temptation, and it was always ours before it was ever silicon's.

We were never in danger of the machine becoming a person. The danger was always that we would agree to become less than one.
