The most common objection I hear to the Bible is not really an objection to the Bible. It is an objection to a story about the Bible — a story most people absorbed without ever checking, the way you absorb the rules of a game you have never read. The Bible was copied by hand for centuries, the story goes, so it must be like the telephone game, garbled past recovery. It was assembled by a council of bishops with political motives who voted on which books would make Jesus divine and burned the rest. It contradicts itself constantly. By the time the story is finished, you would be a fool to take it seriously, and most people I talk to have never gotten past the story to the thing itself.

I lived inside that story for years. It was load-bearing for my unbelief. When it collapsed, it did not make me a Christian — but it took away an excuse I had been leaning on hard.

**Here is what I will argue, and I will try to give the objections their full weight before I answer them: the case against trusting the Bible is built almost entirely on claims that the relevant scholarship — much of it produced by non-Christians and skeptics — does not support.** That does not settle whether the Bible is true. It clears away the underbrush so the real question can be asked.

## The telephone game is the wrong picture

Start with the most popular objection, because it is the most quickly answered and the most stubbornly repeated.

The telephone game works because each player hears the message once, from one source, and passes it to exactly one other person, with no way to check. That is the opposite of how the New Testament was transmitted. The texts were copied many times, in many places, by many hands, and the copies spread outward in a branching tree, not a single fragile line. F.F. Bruce, the Manchester scholar whose *The New Testament Documents: Are They Reliable?* (1943) is still a sober starting point, made the basic point that the sheer volume of manuscripts is itself the safeguard. We have thousands of Greek manuscripts and many thousands more in other ancient languages — vastly more than for any other document of the ancient world. Caesar's *Gallic Wars* survives in a handful of late copies, and no one writes books questioning whether Caesar really wrote it.

Abundance is not a weakness. It is how scholars catch errors. When you have one copy, a mistake is invisible. When you have thousands branching from different regions, a copyist's slip in Egypt shows up against a clean reading in Syria, and the original is recoverable by comparison. The vast majority of the variations among manuscripts are spelling differences and obvious slips of the pen. The places where a genuinely meaningful, disputed variant affects anything are few, well known, and printed right in the footnotes of any decent modern Bible. The text is not hidden from you. The uncertainty is disclosed on the page.

This is not the same as proving the contents true. A perfectly transmitted false claim is still false. But the specific charge — that the words were garbled in transit until we cannot know what was originally written — does not survive contact with the manuscript evidence. We know what they wrote with a confidence that embarrasses the comparison to every other ancient text.

## The council did not invent the canon

The second story is more dramatic and more wrong: that a council of bishops sat down centuries later and voted Jesus into godhood, choosing four gospels and suppressing dozens of others to consolidate power. It is a good plot. It is not history.

The canon was not decided by decree. It emerged by recognition, slowly, from below. The four gospels were functioning as authoritative across scattered, often persecuted churches long before any council ratified them — the church recognized the books it had already been reading and dying for, rather than conferring authority on them by vote. The criteria were not political: a book had to trace to the apostolic circle, be in wide use across the churches, and cohere with the faith already received. The famous alternative gospels usually invoked here were written considerably later, mostly in the second century and after, often carrying a theology the apostolic churches had already rejected on its face. They were not suppressed by a conspiracy. They lost because they arrived late and said things the eyewitness communities knew to be foreign.

Luke states the method out loud at the start of his gospel. He says many have undertaken to compile accounts, that he has carefully investigated everything from the beginning, drawing on those who were eyewitnesses from the first, so that his reader may know the certainty of what he was taught (Luke 1:1–4). That is not the preface of a man inventing a religion. It is the preface of a man doing research and showing his work.

It is worth pausing on why the conspiracy version is so attractive despite being so weak, because the attraction tells you something. The story of a power-hungry council voting Jesus into godhood gives the skeptic a villain, a motive, and a clean exit — if the whole thing was a power grab, you can dismiss it without engaging it. That is a great deal more comfortable than the actual history, which is messy and bottom-up and full of ordinary people in scattered towns recognizing books they were already reading aloud and dying over. The conspiracy is the easier story to disbelieve, which is exactly why it gets believed. I preferred it for the same reason most people do. It let me close the file without reading it. The trouble is that the real history of the canon refuses to supply the villain the story needs, and a dismissal aimed at a villain who was never there is a punch thrown at a shadow.

## The eyewitness question is where it gets interesting

For a long time the scholarly fashion held that the gospels were anonymous folk traditions, shaped and reshaped by communities over generations until whatever historical core they had was buried under invention. That model dominated, and it is the assumption sitting underneath most educated dismissals today.

Richard Bauckham took it apart in *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* (2006), and the argument is worth a skeptic's attention precisely because it cuts against the academic grain. Bauckham marshals the evidence that the gospels are not anonymous community products but testimony tied to named individuals who were there. He points to the strange specificity of named minor characters who serve no literary purpose — why name the man who carried the cross, and his sons, unless those sons were known figures in the church who could be asked? He shows that the patterns of names in the gospels match the actual statistical frequency of Jewish names in first-century Palestine, a fingerprint almost impossible to fake from a distance or a later generation. He argues that the period between the events and the writing was not the silent gap legend requires but a span in which the eyewitnesses were living, teaching, and available to correct the record.

You can disagree with Bauckham — scholars do. But you cannot any longer treat the eyewitness question as settled in favor of the legend model, because a major work of mainstream scholarship reopened it and put the weight back on the side of testimony. That is the honest state of the field, and it is not the state of the field most skeptics think they are standing on.

## The texts touch the ground

There is a quieter line of evidence that rarely makes the popular debates, and it mattered to me precisely because it was checkable against things outside the Bible. The documents are not free-floating tales set in a vague never-land. They are studded with the names of real rulers, real places, real political arrangements that we can verify from sources that had no stake in the Christian claim.

Luke in particular reads like a man who expected his readers to be able to look things up. He anchors his account in datable reigns and named officials — governors, tetrarchs, high priests — many of whom turn up in Roman records, in the Jewish historian Josephus, and in inscriptions pulled out of the ground centuries later. An inscription confirming Pontius Pilate as the Roman prefect of Judea was recovered in the twentieth century, long after anyone could have planted it to help the gospels. The titles Luke gives to local officials in various cities, which earlier critics flagged as errors, turned out to be precisely correct for the specific places and periods he was describing — a kind of accuracy a late forger writing from a distance would have been unlikely to get right. None of this proves the resurrection, and I will keep saying that, because the line between corroboration and proof is exactly the line careless apologists erase. But it does undercut the picture of the gospels as floating myth. A myth does not bother to get the provincial bureaucracy right. A myth has no governor to name.

## What about the contradictions

This is the objection I respect most, because it is the one with real texture, and the glib Christian answers to it are genuinely bad.

The gospels do not agree on every detail. How many angels at the tomb, the order of events on Easter morning, the exact wording of sayings reported differently — these differences are real, and pretending they are not is a kind of lying that does the Bible no favors. But here is what serious historians notice, and what should give a skeptic pause: ancient testimony that agrees on every detail is the suspicious kind. It signals collusion, a single source dressed up as many. Independent witnesses to the same event always differ at the edges and converge at the center, and that is exactly the pattern the gospels show. They differ on the number of angels. They do not differ on the crucifixion, the burial, the empty tomb, the appearances. The variation at the periphery is evidence of independence, not evidence of fraud.

N.T. Wright has made this point across his historical work: the differences in the resurrection narratives are precisely what you would expect from separate witnesses describing a real, confusing, overwhelming event, and precisely not what you would expect from a committee inventing a story to agree on. The flat, harmonized account would be the forgery. The textured, partly-divergent account is what testimony looks like.

There is a further detail in those narratives that a committee bent on persuasion would have edited out. In a culture where a woman's testimony carried little legal weight, all four gospels make women the first witnesses to the empty tomb. If you were fabricating a story to win converts in the first century, you would not anchor your central claim in the testimony of the witnesses your own society discounted. You would put the men there first. The gospels do the opposite, and the most economical explanation for an embarrassing detail that works against the storyteller's interest is that it happened that way and the writers were not free to change it. Historians call this the criterion of embarrassment, and the gospels are full of it — the disciples are dull, cowardly, and slow; their leader is baptized by a man preaching repentance; he dies the death reserved for the lowest criminals. These are not the flattering details a movement invents about its origins. They are the details a movement reports because it is stuck with them.

## The claim the Bible makes about itself

There is a move I have to make carefully here, because it can sound circular, and a skeptic is right to flag circular reasoning. I am not arguing the Bible is true because the Bible says so. I am noting what kind of claim its authors thought they were making, because it bears on how to read them.

Peter, writing about what he had seen, insists he was not passing on clever myths — that he had been an eyewitness of Jesus' majesty (2 Peter 1:16). John ends his gospel saying plainly that Jesus did many other things he did not record, and that the ones he did record were written so the reader might believe (John 20:30–31). The selection is admitted. The purpose is admitted. And Paul tells Timothy that all Scripture is God-breathed and useful for teaching, correcting, training (2 Timothy 3:16) — a claim of inspiration, not of magical transmission, made by men who also told you they did research and selected their material.

These are not the moves of people who think they are writing fiction. Whether they were right is the question that remains. But the texts present themselves as testimony and selection and investigation, and they should be examined as that — not as the garbled rumor of the telephone-game story, which the documents themselves refuse.

## Where this leaves the question

Clearing the underbrush does not get you to faith, and I will not pretend it does. You can grant every point I have made — that the transmission is reliable, that the canon emerged by recognition, that the eyewitness case is live, that the contradictions are the signature of independence — and still conclude the witnesses were sincerely mistaken about what they saw. That is a coherent position. It is a far more honest one than the position I held, which was to reject the whole thing on the strength of stories about it I had never checked.

That is the part I have to own. My unbelief had felt like the brave, clear-eyed option, the refusal to be fooled. It turned out to be the credulous option — I had simply transferred my faith from the Bible to the secular folklore about the Bible, and never noticed I had believed something on worse evidence than the thing I was rejecting.

So the question I would leave you with is not whether the Bible is convenient or comfortable or flattering. It is older and simpler than that. Have you actually read it, with the attention you would give a hostile witness you suspected of telling the truth? Or have you, like me, been quoting the trailer and calling it the film?

The book is not asking to be trusted blindly. It is asking to be opened. Most of the people most confident it cannot be trusted have done everything with it except that.
