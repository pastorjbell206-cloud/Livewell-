The question does not usually announce itself. It waits for a good day.

You have done the work well, the people you love are asleep in the next rooms, the bills are covered this month, and you are standing in a dark kitchen with a glass of water when the thought arrives without being invited. None of this finally matters. Not that it feels bad in the moment. It feels like nothing, which is worse, a sudden weightlessness where you expected the floor. The children you would die for will die too, and their children, and one day the last human being will close his eyes on a cooling planet under a sun that is itself running down, and no one will be left to remember that you loved anyone or built anything or stood in this kitchen afraid. Then the moment passes and you go to bed. But you have seen it now, and you cannot un-see it.

I know the thought from the inside, and I need to say where I am standing before I say anything else. I did not first meet that 2 a.m. weightlessness as a pastor with an answer ready. I met it as an atheist who thought he had already made his peace with it. For most of my life I believed the universe owed me no meaning and I did not resent it for the silence. So when the church tells you that people without God are secretly miserable, that they lie awake in despair and only pretend to be happy, I want you to know that a man who lived on the other side is telling you the church is wrong. That story is a lie we tell to comfort ourselves, and it is an insult besides.

## The strongest form of the other answer

Let me put the secular account in the form its best defenders actually gave it, because a version you can knock over is not worth the paper.

Albert Camus opened *The Myth of Sisyphus* in 1942 with a sentence that has never stopped being true: "There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide." He was not being morbid. He was being honest in a way most people never are. If the universe is silent, if there is no given purpose written into things, then the first question is not what to have for dinner or how to get promoted. It is whether a life with no external meaning is worth continuing at all. Camus refused the two easy exits. He would not lie to himself and pretend the cosmos cared, and he would not step off the ledge either. He chose a third thing he called revolt: to live fully and defiantly inside the absurd, to squeeze the day for everything it holds precisely because nothing guarantees it, to keep pushing the rock without the consolation of a summit. His book ends where every honest reader remembers it ending. "One must imagine Sisyphus happy."

Jean-Paul Sartre pressed the same ground from a different angle. For a manufactured object, he argued, essence comes first: the knife is designed for cutting before it exists. For a human being it runs the other way. Existence precedes essence. You arrive with no blueprint, no assigned purpose, no maker's intent, and that terrifying vacancy is also the only real freedom there has ever been. You are, in his phrase, condemned to be free. No god hands you a meaning, which means no god can take it from you or shrink you to fit it. You author it yourself, and you are wholly responsible for what you write.

And here is what I will not let the believer skip. This is not a recipe for despair, and the people who live by it are not frauds. I have known atheists who loved their spouses with a fidelity that shamed the elders of my church, who gave to strangers with no heaven watching the ledger, who faced a terminal diagnosis with a steadiness I would want at my own bedside. They built meaning the way Sartre said you could, out of love and craft and loyalty and the refusal to look away, and it was real meaning, not a stage set. Any Christian who has watched that up close and still says it is secretly empty is not defending the faith. He is protecting his own comfort by slandering better men. Concede this fully, or do not bother turning the page.

## The seam

Now turn it over, gently, and look at the seam.

There is a difference between a meaning you invent and a meaning you discover, and the whole question lives in that difference. What you invent, you can also un-invent. What you author, you can revise, and on the worst night you can delete. A purpose I assign to my own life is answerable to me, which sounds like power until the hour comes when I am the very thing that has broken, and then the author and the sinking ship are the same person, and there is no one on the shore. Meaning you make cannot save you, because it is downstream of you. It has exactly as much authority over you as you are willing to grant it, and the granting can be withdrawn.

And under even that, the second problem, the one Camus was too honest to hide. The heat death does not care what you authored. If matter and time are all there is, then every cathedral and every symphony and every child taught to be kind is a pattern that the running-down of the universe will eventually smooth flat, and the smoothing is not a tragedy the cosmos will mourn, because there will be no one to mourn and nothing that was ever owed. A meaning that is finally erased and never even registered as a loss is in a strange position. It is real to you now and null to the universe always, and you are asked to hold both of those at once and call it enough.

I am not scoring a debating point. I lived here. I want to show you two men who lived here harder than I did.

## Tolstoy in the study

Leo Tolstoy had, by any accounting the world keeps, won. By the time he wrote *A Confession* in 1882 he had produced *War and Peace* and *Anna Karenina*, he had land and wealth and international fame, a wife and children, health, and the full use of a mind that ranks among the strongest his century produced. He had built meaning by every method Sartre would later prescribe, and more of it than almost anyone alive. And in the middle of that life he was ambushed by a question he could not answer and could not silence: was there any meaning in his life that the death waiting for all of it would not destroy. He could not find one. He described hiding a rope so that he would not hang himself from the beam of his own room, and giving up hunting with his gun for fear of what he might do with it alone in the woods. This is not the confession of a failure drowning his regrets. It is the confession of the most successful man in Russia discovering that success was never the thing that answered the question, and that he had no idea what was.

That is the seam I mean. The invented meaning worked beautifully right up until the moment he looked past its edge, and then it held nothing, because it had never been anchored to anything outside the man holding it.

## The values they could not fund

Here is the tell I find most telling, and I offer it as diagnosis, not as a gotcha. The great atheist thinkers kept reaching past their own systems for something their systems could not pay for.

Camus could not stay inside pure absurdity, and he knew it. In *The Rebel*, in 1951, he argued that the man who rebels against oppression is not merely expressing a preference. In saying no to the tyrant he appeals, whether he admits it or not, to a value he believes holds for every human being and not only for himself. But a value that binds everyone, that would still condemn the torturer even if the torturer felt fulfilled, is exactly the kind of objective moral fact a silent and indifferent universe does not stock. Camus wanted justice to be more than his taste in the matter. His cosmos could not make it so.

Sartre did the same with his body as well as his pen. In 1966 he sat as executive president of the tribunal Bertrand Russell convened to judge war crimes in Vietnam, and he judged them, as crimes, as things that were wrong and not merely disliked. A philosophy in which each of us authors his own values from nothing has no floor under the word *crime*. Yet Sartre stood on that floor as though it were solid, because at the level below argument he knew some things are wrong for everyone whether or not anyone authored the rule. He borrowed the plank. He could not have milled it in his own shop.

I do not say this to sneer at two serious men. I say it because I did the identical thing for years and called it maturity. I insisted the universe was meaningless and then lived, minute to minute, as though my son's life had a worth no atom of me had voted on. The account I defended in argument was not the account I trusted with my child.

## The nihilist inside the canon

Here is what took me longest to believe about the Bible, once I finally opened it instead of dismissing it. It is not afraid of any of this. It got there first.

There is a book in it that stares straight into the void Camus described and does not blink. "Vanity of vanities, says the Preacher, vanity of vanities! All is vanity" (Ecclesiastes 1:2). The Hebrew word behind *vanity* means vapor, breath, the mist your mouth makes on a cold morning that is gone before you finish looking at it. The Preacher has money, pleasure, projects, wisdom, work, and he weighs each one and files the same verdict. "Then I considered all that my hands had done and the toil I had expended in doing it, and behold, all was vanity and a striving after wind, and there was nothing to be gained under the sun" (Ecclesiastes 2:11). That is Sisyphus and his rock, written two thousand years before the Frenchman named it. The scandal is not that the skeptic has a hard question the Bible cannot handle. The scandal is that the Bible put the hardest form of the question in its own canon and canonized it, and no council ever voted it out.

But the Preacher leaves one seam of his own, and it is the whole difference. God, he says, "has put eternity into man's heart" (Ecclesiastes 3:11). The reason the mist grieves us, the reason a temporary meaning cannot finally satisfy a creature who keeps demanding a permanent one, is that we were built to want something the running-down world was never able to supply. The ache is not a malfunction to be medicated. It is a piece of evidence about what kind of creature is doing the aching.

## What I would tell the man I was

So I will not tell you that you cannot make meaning without God. You obviously can, and you may be making finer meaning than half the people in my pews. That was never the real question, and pretending it was is how Christians lose the argument they should win.

The real question is quieter and it does not go away when you succeed. It is whether the meaning you are making can carry your weight on the night it has to. Whether it is a thing you hold up, which fails exactly when your arms do, or a thing that holds you, which is only good news if it came from somewhere other than you. Invented meaning has one fatal property: on the day you can no longer believe in it, it is gone, because it was only ever your believing. Discovered meaning has the opposite property, and it is the only property that finally helps. It is still there when you stop feeling it. It was true while you slept.

The Christian claim is not that we escaped the vapor by refusing to look at it. Paul looked straight at it and wrote that if the dead are not raised, then "let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die" (1 Corinthians 15:32), which is to say that if the grave is the end he would concede the whole nihilist case and pass the wine. The claim is that a man walked out of a tomb on the far side of the vapor, and that this makes love and labor and a kindness no one will remember into something the heat death cannot reach, not because we authored them but because they are held by someone the cooling universe does not get to erase.

I made my own meaning for years, and it was good, and it worked, right up until it did not, on a night when I was the thing that had broken and there was no one on the shore because I had spent my whole life proving the shore was empty. I am not going to tell you what to conclude from that. I will only tell you not to trust the peace you have made with the silence more than you trust the silence itself. Test it. Wait for the good day, the dark kitchen, the glass of water, the weightlessness. Ask whether what you have built is holding you up, or whether you are holding it.

Then notice which one of you gets tired first.
