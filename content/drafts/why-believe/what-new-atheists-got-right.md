I owned the books. Not borrowed, not skimmed at a table in a store: bought, underlined, argued from at dinner tables where I already knew I would win. When Richard Dawkins published *The God Delusion* in 2006, I read it the way a soldier reads dispatches from a front where his side is advancing. Christopher Hitchens came the next year with *God Is Not Great: How Religion Poisons Everything*, and I read that one twice. Sam Harris had fired the opening shot in 2004 with *The End of Faith*, written in the smoke still rising off September 11th. Daniel Dennett rounded out the four in 2006 with *Breaking the Spell*. Someone nicknamed them the Four Horsemen, and I would have followed all four off a cliff.

I have to say that before I say anything else, because the easiest thing a pastor can do with these men is build a version of them out of straw and knock it down in front of an audience that was never going to read them anyway. I refuse. I stood where they stood. I made their arguments better than most believers I now know could answer them. And here is the sentence it took me years to be able to write without flinching: they were right about a great deal, and a believer who cannot say so has understood neither the men nor the faith he imagines he is defending.

## The four, at full strength

Take them at their most serious, because a version you can knock over is not worth the paper.

Harris wrote first and wrote angriest, and his anger had a specific occasion. Nineteen men had flown planes into buildings because they believed something about the next life, and Harris argued that the moderate believer who calls their faith a perversion has no principled ground to stand on, because he too holds beliefs he cannot test and will not surrender to evidence. Religious moderation, he said, is not a solution. It is the cover that keeps the whole enterprise beyond criticism. Faith is belief without sufficient reason, and in a century of engineered viruses and portable warheads, belief without reason is not a private comfort. It is a public danger.

Dawkins pressed a cleaner claim. The existence of God, he insisted, is not a separate kind of question fenced off from investigation. It is a hypothesis about what is really out there, and a hypothesis can be weighed. Weigh it, he argued, and it fails: the universe looks exactly as we would expect if there were no one behind it, and the God most people actually worship is a bronze-age tribal figure whose recorded conduct, laid out plainly, would disgrace a human being. He catalogued that conduct at length, the jealousy and the slaughter and the sanctioned cruelty, and dared the believer to read his own scriptures with the flinch removed.

Dennett was the quietest and, in a way, the most subversive. He did not mainly argue that religion is false. He argued that we have made it rude to ask. His book proposed to break a spell: the unspoken agreement that faith is the one human phenomenon we do not study the way we study everything else. Treat religion as a natural thing, he said, a product of human minds and human history with causes we can trace, and watch how quickly its claim to have descended from heaven begins to look like something that grew up from the ground.

And Hitchens. Hitchens did not build a system. He indicted. Religion, he wrote, is man-made, and its title tells you where he landed: it poisons everything. It corrupts our view of our own origins, sets us at the center of a cosmos that plainly does not revolve around us, promises eternity to buy obedience now, and reserves its special energies for the policing of sex and the shaming of the body. He had watched clerics of every flag bless armies and cover for the men who harmed children, and he had the memory and the malice to name every instance. I laughed out loud reading him. I also, in the honest hours, could not answer him.

## What they got right

So let me stop performing balance and simply concede the ground, because it is real ground and it is soaked.

Religion has blood on it. Not metaphorically, not in the soft sense a sermon uses before the reassuring turn. The church blessed the conquest of peoples and called it mission. It burned dissenters and lit the fires with Scripture. In my own lifetime it moved men who abused children from one parish to the next and spent more energy protecting the institution than the children, and when the accounting came it reached for lawyers before it reached for repentance. Hitchens did not invent any of that. He read it off the record, and the record is public, and the reflex of the church's defenders to change the subject the moment it comes up is very nearly as damning as the thing itself.

They were right about the anti-intellectualism too, and this one I feel personally, because I was on the receiving end of it before I ever left. I asked hard questions as a young man and I was handed slogans. I raised the problem of suffering and got a bumper sticker. I noticed the contradictions honest readers have always noticed, and I was told that noticing was the sin, that a good heart does not ask. Faith was offered to me not as a conviction that could survive scrutiny but as a fortress built to avoid it, and Harris is exactly right that a faith which forbids the question has already conceded it cannot answer it. The apologetics I was given were thin, and I could feel their thinness the way you feel a floor that will not hold your weight, and the men who gave them to me mistook my leaving for rebellion when it was closer to disappointment.

And they were right about the poison in the specific place it does its quietest work: the shutting down of the mind in the name of God. This is the hit I most want to dodge and will not. Every time the church has answered a real question with a demand for silence, every time it has treated doubt as disloyalty and curiosity as pride, it has taught its own children that the faith cannot bear examination. It taught me that. I did not walk away from Jesus of Nazareth. I walked away from a version of him that came with the instruction to stop thinking, and I was right to walk away from that, and it took me a long time to learn that the instruction had never come from him.

Grant all of it. If you cannot grant it, you are not ready for the next paragraph, and neither was I.

## Where the argument overreaches

Now turn the argument over and look at its underside, slowly, the way I finally learned to.

Watch first what each man does with the evidence when it runs the other way. The worst of religion is presented as its essence, the thing it truly is once the pleasantries are stripped off. The worst of secularism is presented as an aberration, a betrayal of principles that remain clean. But the twentieth century will not sit still for that division. The regimes that killed on the largest scale the world has ever recorded were not priest-ridden. They were built by men who had read their Feuerbach and their Marx, who abolished God by decree and put the state in the empty chair. Stalin's cellars, Mao's famine, the killing fields of Cambodia: these were not the church's crimes. They were carried out by movements that shared the horsemen's diagnosis and drew the practical conclusion. When Hitchens was pressed on this, he answered that those regimes were religious in structure, cults of personality with their own liturgies. It is a clever answer. It is also the whole argument collapsing, because if every organized cruelty counts as religion, then religion is just a name for the human capacity to organize cruelty, and the word has stopped meaning what his book needed it to mean.

That is the deeper flaw under all four, and it has a name older than any of them: the genetic fallacy, the mistake of thinking you have shown a belief false by exposing where it came from. Dennett is right that religion has natural causes, that it grew in human minds and human tribes and can be studied like anything else. But so did mathematics. So did his own confidence in reason. Show me the evolutionary account of why a hominid came to trust its own reasoning, and you have explained the origin of trust without touching the question of whether the reasoning is any good. A belief is true or false on the merits, not on its pedigree. Trace the faith all the way back to the anxious ape at the fire, and you have still not said one word about whether, at the end of that long chain, there is anyone there.

And Dawkins made the cleanest category mistake of the four, the one I repeated for years without noticing. He treated God as a hypothesis inside the universe, a large invisible object whose existence should leave fingerprints his instruments could detect. But the God the historic creeds confess was never advanced as an item within the world, one more thing alongside the galaxies to be found or ruled out. He is offered as the reason there is a world at all, the ground the whole experiment stands on. You do not test the foundation by searching the house for it. To demand that God show up as data within the system is to have decided in advance that nothing could count as him, and then to announce, with the air of discovery, that nothing does.

There is a fair objection here, and I will not hide from it. The believer who says God is beyond all testing can sound like a man moving the goalposts to a field where he can never lose. That is a real danger and the church has abused it. But there is a difference between a claim that explains nothing and a claim that is simply the wrong shape for a microscope, and the honest atheist knows the difference, because he applies it to his own foundations without blinking every day.

## What I finally saw

I have saved the hardest concession for the end, and it is a concession I make against my old self.

Jesus said harder things about religion than Hitchens ever managed. "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you are like whitewashed tombs, which outwardly appear beautiful, but within are full of dead people's bones and all uncleanness" (Matthew 23:27, ESV). That is not a skeptic writing from outside. That is the center of the faith turning on the religious professionals with a fury the horsemen would have envied, and it means the critique the four thought they were leveling at Christianity from without had been sitting inside it, in red letters, the whole time. The book they were attacking already contained the indictment. It also contained the standard by which the church stands condemned: "Religion that is pure and undefiled before God the Father is this: to visit orphans and widows in their affliction, and to keep oneself unstained from the world" (James 1:27, ESV). By that measure most of what the four attacked deserved attacking, and the New Testament said so first.

What I could not see while I held their books, because I was too busy winning, was this. They were right about the church I was leaving. They were wrong about the God I had never actually met. Every crime they catalogued was real, and not one of them was Jesus of Nazareth. They had assembled a brilliant, furious, documented case against religion as a human machine for control, and I cheered because I thought that machine was the whole of it, and I had never once been shown the man at the center who was executed by exactly that machine for exactly the reasons the horsemen despised it. I left over the poison. Nobody told me the poison was the thing he came to name, and that they had crucified him for naming it.

So I do not stand over these men now. I owe them too much. Harris taught me that a faith which forbids the question is already dead. Dawkins taught me to read my own scriptures with the flinch removed, and when I finally did, they were harder and stranger and more honest than the sanitized version I had been sold. Hitchens taught me that the church must carry its crimes as facts and not misunderstandings, and I have carried them into hospital rooms and gravesides ever since. They cleared a great deal of rotten ground, and the God I found was standing on the far side of it, where I would never have looked as long as I mistook the rot for the thing itself.

The test they never let me apply to my own certainty is the one their own hero recommended. "Thus you will recognize them by their fruits" (Matthew 7:20, ESV). Apply it to the church and it draws blood, and it should. Apply it to the confident secular century that promised to do better once God was gone, and it draws blood too. Neither of us gets to walk away clean. That is not a victory for my side. It is the end of the idea that either of us had a side that was clean, and it is exactly where an honest man, believer or not, should have to stand before he says one more word about who poisons everything.
