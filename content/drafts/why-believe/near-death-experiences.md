A bestselling book about a boy who came back from heaven sold by the millions, and then the boy grew up and said he had made the whole thing up. I remember the satisfaction that news gave me, back when I held that the brain was a machine and death was the machine going dark. I kept a whole shelf of contempt for these accounts. The tunnel, the light at the end of it, the dead relatives waiting there, the warm voice saying it was not yet time. I filed all of it under wishful thinking, the last chemical panic of an organ shutting down, and I was not entirely wrong. I am no longer sure I was entirely right. That uncertainty is the only honest place to start.

Set the thing on the table before either side reaches for it. A near-death experience is what some people report after being pulled back from the edge: the cardiac arrest, the operating table, the crash that nearly finished them. Raymond Moody gave the phenomenon its name in 1975, in a book called *Life After Life*, and since then the reports have been gathered by the thousands. They are strange, and they are consistent, and both of those facts have to be accounted for by anyone who wants to be taken seriously here.

## The case a believer can honestly make

Start with the case for taking these accounts seriously, because it is stronger than my old contempt allowed. The reports rhyme across people who have nothing in common. A retired machinist in Ohio and a woman in a village that never heard his language describe the same architecture. The passage. The enveloping light. The sense of a love so total it reorders everything the person thought they wanted. Many describe a review of their own life, every unkind thing felt again from the other side, from inside the person they wounded. And a striking number come back angry to be back, grieving the return to their bodies the way you would grieve a country you were deported from. A hallucination, you would think, would be as various as the people having it. This one keeps its shape.

Then there are the accounts that are harder to wave off. The ones where a patient with no heartbeat and a flat reading later describes something they should not have been able to describe: the instrument the surgeon called for, a conversation out in the corridor, an object on a ledge no one in the room had reason to mention. I hold these loosely, because such stories are hard to verify after the fact and easy to round up in the retelling, and I will not build anything on them. But I no longer pretend they are not there. The honest skeptic ought to be at least unsettled that they exist at all.

What moved me most, when I finally looked instead of sneering, was not any single story. It was that some of the people studying this are not credulous at all. A handful of cardiologists and neurologists have spent careers on the phenomenon, working in ordinary hospitals and publishing in ordinary medical literature, precisely because it would not sit still under the easy explanations they started with. You can decide they are chasing a mirage. You cannot decide they never looked. I had assumed the only people interested were the ones already sure of the answer, and that assumption was the tell of a man who had not done any looking himself.

## The case a skeptic can honestly make

Now give the skeptic his full say, because he has earned it, and because for most of my life I was him. The dying brain is a known factory of vivid experience. Starve it of oxygen and it produces light, tunnels, euphoria, the felt presence of others. This is documented in situations that have nothing to do with dying, in fighter pilots blacking out under G-force, in certain seizures, under certain drugs. The tunnel and the light may be the retina and the visual cortex failing in a particular order, not a corridor to anywhere.

Expectation shapes the rest. A person raised on a certain picture of the afterlife tends to meet that picture at the threshold. The details track the culture of the one reporting them, which is exactly what you would expect if the mind is furnishing the room out of its own stock. Memory finishes the job. What the patient reports is not a recording played back but a story assembled afterward, smoothed and revised each time it is told, sincerely, with no intent to deceive. We do this with ordinary memories every day. There is no reason to think we stop doing it with the most overwhelming experience a person ever has.

And then the point that stands even if you grant every report, the one that finally matters most. A near-death experience is, by definition, not death. The person came back. Whatever they touched, they touched with a brain that was injured but not gone, and then they returned to a body and a bed to tell it. It may be the mind's last flare and not the far shore at all. To call it a report from the other side is to assume the very thing in question, which is whether there is another side and whether anyone in that bed actually reached it. The most that can honestly be said is that something happened near the border. Near is not across.

## What these accounts cannot carry

So let me say plainly what these stories cannot bear, because the people who love them tend to load them past their strength, and I have done it myself in a hospital room when I wanted something to be true. They are not proof of heaven. They conflict in their particulars, and the conflicts matter, the same event narrated through Hindu furniture and Baptist furniture and no furniture at all. They are shaped, visibly, by the expectations of the one reporting, which is precisely what an invention would also look like. A faith built on them is a house built on sand, and the first hard question washes it out.

I have watched a grieving person hold one of these accounts the way you hold a plank in deep water, and I would not pry it from their hands. But I would not tell them it was the rock, either. It is not the rock. Anyone who sells it as the rock is setting them up for the day the plank fails, and planks fail.

## Where the hope actually rests

Here is what I did not understand as a materialist mocking these accounts, and did not understand for a while as a new believer half hoping they were true. The Christian hope never rested on any of this. It was never built on private tunnels of light, or on what a man glimpsed while his heart was stopped and his eyes were closed. It rests on a public event. A tomb, in a named city, on a datable weekend, examined in daylight by people who said they had eaten with the man afterward and put their hands on him, and who then went to their deaths saying so and never took it back. That is a different order of claim entirely. Not a private country reported by one traveler who came back alone and cannot show you where he was. A body, in a known grave, gone, offered up to friends and enemies alike, and to anyone else who wanted to go and check.

And here is the detail that undid the last of my resistance, because it comes from the one man you would expect to lean hardest on private vision. Paul had one. He describes it in a letter to Corinth, hiding himself in the third person out of something close to embarrassment: a man "caught up to the third heaven," who "heard things that cannot be told, which man may not utter" (2 Corinthians 12:2-4, ESV). If anyone ever earned the right to preach from his own trip past the veil, it was this man. He does something astonishing with it instead. He will not even say for certain what kind of experience it was. Twice over he says "whether in the body or out of the body I do not know, God knows" (2 Corinthians 12:2-3, ESV). The most visionary experience in the New Testament, and the one who had it refuses to build a single doctrine on it, refuses even to be sure what it was, and turns instead to boast about his weakness. The man who could have founded a religion on a near-death experience of his own would not do it. He knew where the foundation was, and it was not inside his own head. It was outside the city wall, in a garden, empty.

So I have stopped doing both of the things I used to do. I no longer mock these accounts. There is too much in them I cannot explain, and contempt was always the lazy man's certainty, the shortcut I mistook for rigor. And I do not worship them either. They cannot bear the weight, and pretending they can does a quiet cruelty to the people who trust them. I hold them the way you hold a flashlight found in the dark. It is real light. It is worth something. A person feeling along the wall of a strange room can be grateful for it and still know it is not the reason the room exists.

The sun does not need the flashlight to vouch for it. When morning finally comes through the window the whole apparatus of little proofs goes quiet, not refuted, just outshone. The tomb was open before anyone wrote a book about a tunnel. It is still open. And whatever waits past the border that you and I will not cross and come back from, it does not hang on the testimony of those who only came near.

They saw a light. He left an empty grave.
