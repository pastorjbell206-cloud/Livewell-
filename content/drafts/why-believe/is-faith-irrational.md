The word usually arrives like the end of a conversation instead of the middle of one. You press a question as far as it will go, and somewhere near the bottom a good and sincere person looks at you and says: you just have to have faith. What they mean, most of the time, is that the questioning should stop now. And if you are the kind of person who cannot make the questioning stop, that sentence does not sound like an invitation. It sounds like a door closing, with the interesting part of the room on the other side.

I know how it sounds because I said it to myself for years, and I said it as a verdict. Faith was the name I gave to the thing reasonable people had outgrown. I had reason. They had faith. The line between us was the line between looking at the evidence and looking away from it, and I knew which side I was on, and I did not think it was close.

So let me put my old position on the table in its full strength, because a version you can flick aside is not worth the paper. The objection is not fussy. It is clean.

## The objection, at full strength

The charge is that faith is belief without evidence. Richard Dawkins built much of *The God Delusion* (2006) on exactly that definition: faith is the thing you reach for precisely when you have no reasons, a way of holding a conclusion that argument can neither install nor remove. There is an older, sharper version that every schoolboy has heard: faith is believing what you know is not true. And there is a version with a philosopher's rigor behind it. In 1877 the mathematician W. K. Clifford published an essay called *The Ethics of Belief*, and he laid down a principle he meant to bind everyone, believer and unbeliever alike: "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence." Not unwise. Wrong. A moral failure, a small act of dishonesty against yourself and against everyone who will act on what you claim to know. Clifford tells the story of a shipowner who talks himself into believing his old vessel is seaworthy, sends it out, and drowns the passengers, and he insists the man was guilty the moment he chose to believe past his evidence, whether the ship sank or not.

I found that argument bracing when I was on the other side, and I still do. Here is the part believers are tempted to hurry past, and I will not hurry past it. A great deal of religious faith really is what the objection says it is. It really is credulous. I have watched people believe a thing because a confident man said it from a stage, and hold it tighter the more it was contradicted, and call the tightening loyalty. The church has too often praised not-thinking as though it were a virtue, has treated the person with questions as a weaker brother rather than a serious one, has used "just have faith" as a lid to keep on a pot it did not want boiling. When a skeptic says that "faith" frequently names an evasion, he is not slandering us. He is quoting our own worst Sundays back to us. I have preached to rooms where the word was doing exactly that work, and I have felt it do that work in my own mouth. If the argument stopped there, it would simply be right.

But watch what happens when you ask two questions the objection assumes it has already answered. What did the word ever actually mean. And is the person making the charge standing where he thinks he is standing.

## What the word actually means

Start with the second question, because it is the one I never asked myself.

The whole force of Clifford's principle rests on the idea that we could, in principle, believe only what our evidence compels, and that anything beyond that line is a choice we are guilty of making. So take him at his word and try it. Try to believe nothing that your evidence does not force.

You will not last a morning.

You trust that your reason is reliable, that the mind doing the auditing is not systematically deceiving you. You cannot prove that without using the very reason you are trying to certify, which is to assume the thing in question. You trust that other minds exist, that the people around you are not elaborate automata but persons with an inner life like yours. There is no experiment that reaches inside another skull to check. You trust that the future will resemble the past, that the sun that has risen every morning will rise tomorrow, that bread that nourished you yesterday will not poison you today. David Hume saw the size of that one in 1748 and could not talk his way out of it: there is no argument from experience that the future will be like the past which does not already assume the future will be like the past. We believe it anyway. We stake our lives on it every time we eat, cross a bridge, or plan for a child. Not because we proved it. Because we could not live otherwise, and because it has held.

The chemist Michael Polanyi spent the back half of his life on this, having started as a working scientist rather than a theologian, and his conclusion was that there is no such thing as knowledge without a knower who is committed. Underneath every proof there is something the prover simply trusts and cannot articulate, a set of commitments held before they are examined and mostly never examined at all. He called it tacit knowing, the floor beneath the floor. Strip it out in the name of Clifford's rule and you do not arrive at pure certified reason. You arrive at paralysis. You cannot even begin.

This is the thing I had not seen, and I want to say it against myself plainly, because I spent years accusing believers of the exact posture I was standing in. I had called my own position pure reason. It was not. It was a structure of enormous trust: trust in my senses, trust in my logic, trust in the uniformity of a universe I had inspected an unimaginably small corner of, trust in a hundred authorities whose experiments I had never repeated and could not have repeated. I believed almost everything I believed on someone else's word. I had simply never counted it, because the trust was invisible the way water is invisible to the fish. Not the absence of faith. Faith I had refused to notice, and therefore never had to defend.

Now the first question, the one about the word. Because the caricature does not only misjudge the objection's own footing. It misreads the thing it thinks it is attacking.

The New Testament word translated *faith* is *pistis*, and in the ordinary Greek of the day it did not mean a leap into the dark. It meant trust, and it meant faithfulness, the reliability that runs in both directions between two parties who have proven themselves to each other. It is the word you would use for a person you had learned, over years, that you could lean your weight on. Biblical faith is not belief in the absence of evidence. It is trust extended to someone on the basis of what you already have reason to think is true about them, into the part of the future you cannot yet see. That is why the most quoted definition in the book does not say what people think it says. "Now faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen" (Hebrews 11:1). Things not seen is not the same as things disbelieved. The verse is about the unseen, the not-yet and the beyond-sight, not about the contradicted. And read one paragraph further and the writer does something the caricature cannot explain: he starts listing history. Abraham, Moses, the whole long file of people who trusted God, and the ground they trusted him on was the record of what he had already done. Faith in that chapter is memory pointed forward. It is trust with a track record behind it.

This runs straight through the book. "Come now, let us reason together, says the LORD" (Isaiah 1:18), which is a strange line to find in a text supposedly hostile to the mind. "Test everything; hold fast what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:21), which is the opposite of switching off the intellect at the door. Even the story most often used to prove that Christianity prizes not-looking proves the reverse when you read it whole. Thomas will not believe his friend is alive until he sees the wounds, and Jesus does not scold him for wanting evidence. He gives it. He offers the hands and the side, and only then says, "Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed" (John 20:29). That last line gets read as a rebuke to evidence. It is not. It is a blessing on the people who will trust the testimony of the witnesses without demanding to run the experiment again themselves, which is precisely how you and I hold nearly everything we know about history, about science we have not personally done, about a world too large to verify twice. Thomas got his proof. The blessing falls on those who trust reliable testimony. That is not credulity. That is how knowledge has always traveled between persons.

## Which faith is warranted

So the honest question was never faith against reason. That framing is the caricature, and it collapses the moment either side is examined, because reason is running on trust the whole time and biblical faith is reaching for reasons the whole time. The real question, the harder and better one, is which commitments are warranted. Everyone has them. Everyone is trusting something they cannot stand outside of and prove. The atheist trusts that the universe is finally intelligible and that his mind is fitted to read it, which is a large and unproven and, I would say, a hopeful thing to believe about a cosmos that his own account says was not designed to be understood by anyone. The believer trusts that the intelligibility is there because a mind is behind it. Neither of us is refusing to bet. We are betting on different things, and the grown-up conversation is about which bet the evidence better supports, not about who has kept his hands clean of belief.

That is a conversation I could not have while I was still congratulating myself for having no faith. You have to first admit you are holding something before you can ask whether you are holding the right thing.

None of this makes the credulous kind respectable. The lazy faith is still lazy, and the man who believes a thing because it soothes him is doing what Clifford accused, and I will not defend it, and neither will the book, which reserves some of its hardest words for exactly that. The gospel does not ask you to stop thinking. It asks you to keep thinking long enough to notice that the thinking itself is standing on trust, and then to ask honestly what is worthy of the trust you are already, unavoidably, giving to something.

I do not ask you to believe on no evidence. I would not have, and the demand would have insulted the boy I was. I ask you only to count the faith you already live by, the trust under the reason you are so sure is unmixed, and then to ask whether the object of that trust could bear its weight. The most honest prayer in the whole book is not the one that has it all resolved. It is the one a desperate father gasps with a dying child in his arms: "I believe; help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24). Belief and unbelief in the same breath, the man not pretending to a certainty he does not have, and reaching anyway. That is not the enemy of reason.

It is what all of us are already doing. Some of us have just stopped pretending we are not.
