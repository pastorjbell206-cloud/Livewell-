The best man in my old neighborhood did not believe in God, and neither did I. He was a machinist who kept an eye on the widow three doors down, shoveled her walk before his own, and once drove four hours to sit with a friend whose son had died. If you had told him his kindness needed a theological license, he would have laughed at you, and he would have been right to. I want to start there, on his side of the line, because the argument I am going to make collapses the moment it pretends to be an argument he was not.

So let me concede the whole of it at once, with no fingers crossed. You do not need to believe in God to be good. Atheists are not, as a class, worse neighbors, worse parents, or worse citizens than the people filling pews. The comparison is not close to what the tract-writers imagine, and in fifteen years of pastoring I have buried men who never darkened a church door and were kinder than deacons I could name. If the claim on the table were that unbelievers cannot behave well, it would be refuted by every Tuesday. It is not the claim. It was never the claim, and every time a Christian argues as if it were, he earns the contempt he then complains about.

## The question underneath the question

The real question is not whether an atheist can be good. It is what makes the word *good* point at anything real when he says it. When you tell me that torturing a child for pleasure is wrong, you are not saying you dislike it, the way you dislike anchovies. You mean it would be wrong if every person on earth approved, wrong in a Roman coliseum where the crowd cheered, wrong before there were crowds. You mean it is a fact about the act and not a fact about your feelings. And the only version of the moral argument worth anyone's time asks whether your picture of the universe has room for a fact like that.

Take the three accounts an honest atheism has on offer, because I held all three in turn. Morality is useful: cooperation outbred selfishness, and the tribes that protected their children left more descendants than the tribes that did not. Morality is evolved: the revulsion you feel watching cruelty is a piece of neural equipment installed by that same pressure, as real and as unchosen as the fear of heights. Morality is felt: strong, sincere, universal enough to build a civilization on, and still, at bottom, a feeling. Each of these is true as far as it goes. Not one of them makes cruelty wrong. They explain why I flinch. They do not touch whether the thing I flinch at is actually, mind-independently, evil, or whether my flinch is only weather in a skull.

## The oldest trap, and the door it hides

At this point the believer reaches for God, and the skeptic has a reply older than the church, and it is a good one. Plato set it down roughly twenty-four centuries ago in a dialogue called the *Euthyphro*, where Socrates asks whether a thing is holy because the gods love it, or the gods love it because it is holy. Put it in the modern form: is a thing good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good. Both doors open onto a cliff. If a thing is good only because God commands it, then goodness is arbitrary. Had God commanded cruelty, cruelty would be virtue, and calling God good means nothing more than that God does whatever God says, a circle closed on itself. But if God commands a thing because it is already good, then goodness stands above God, a standard he consults and does not author, and you have found your objective morality without needing him at all. Either God is arbitrary or God is redundant. I sprang that trap on believers with real satisfaction, and I thought it was the end of the conversation.

The answer that undoes it is not a dodge, though I assumed it was for years. The dilemma offers two doors and hides a third. Goodness is neither above God nor invented by God. It is what God is. The tradition running from Thomas Aquinas to the philosopher Robert Adams, whose *Finite and Infinite Goods* (1999) is the careful modern statement, locates the good not in a divine command that could have gone either way, and not in a standard God peers up at, but in God's own unchanging character, of which his commands are the expression. Ask why God could not command cruelty, and the answer is not that a higher law forbids him. It is that it would contradict his own nature, like asking why light could be dark. On that account goodness is objective, because it is anchored in something that does not answer to anyone's opinion, and it is not arbitrary, because it does not hang on a will that might have chosen otherwise. The child is not wrong-to-torture because a rule says so. The child is wrong-to-torture because the act is a violation of what is really there.

## The strongest version, given its due

But I promised the skeptic his strongest form, and the three accounts I listed are not it. The serious secular moral realist does not say morality is a useful fiction or a warm feeling. He says objective moral facts exist and need no God to hold them up, the way the truths of mathematics need no God. Two and two make four in every possible world, and gratuitous cruelty is wrong in every possible world, and both are simply necessary truths woven into the furniture of reality. Derek Parfit gave the last years of his life to this in *On What Matters* (2011), arguing that there are normative truths as real as any, discovered rather than invented, binding on every rational mind. Erik Wielenberg, in *Robust Ethics* (2014), makes the case squarely as an atheist: moral facts are brute, necessary, and require no explanation from outside themselves. This is not a man ducking the question. It is a serious metaphysics held by serious people, and it grants everything I have said about the reality of right and wrong while denying that God is needed to secure it. If you are going to reject the moral argument, reject it here, where it is strong, and not at the cartoon.

So let me press it where it is strong, and press honestly. The costs are two, and an atheist named each one.

J.L. Mackie, in *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977), looked hard at these supposed objective values and called them queer: entities and relations unlike anything else in the universe, facts that somehow, merely by existing, reach out and obligate you. Mackie found them so strange that he concluded they do not exist at all. He was an atheist and an error theorist, and he thought talk of objective moral facts was a beautiful mistake. That is a coherent place to stand. What is harder is Mackie's further admission, made elsewhere in his work: that objective moral values, if they did exist, would fit far more easily into a universe with a God in it than into one without. The secular realist keeps the queer facts and refuses the frame that would make them less queer. He is welcome to. But he is carrying a stranger cargo than he usually admits.

The second cost is sharper, and again it was an atheist who drew it. Grant that the moral facts are out there, timeless and binding. How did an animal come to know them? Our moral intuitions were shaped by natural selection, which rewards whatever helped our ancestors survive and breed, and there is no reason to expect that what helped them breed lines up with what is timelessly true. The philosopher Sharon Street pressed exactly this in a 2006 essay, and she is no friend of religion. She is an anti-realist who concluded that since blind evolution would not track independent moral truths, we should stop believing such truths are there. The believer and the anti-realist agree on the problem and part ways at the exit. If the moral facts float free of everything, our confidence that we perceive them is a lucky accident with no account of its luck. A universe ordered by a God who made us to know good from evil can say why the compass points north. A universe of brute facts and blind selection has a compass, and no ground for trusting it.

## Where I could not keep my footing

And this is the place I have to stop describing the skeptic and describe myself, because I was not consistent, and my inconsistency is the whole point. I said morality was evolved and useful and felt. I said it with conviction. And then I lived every waking hour as if some things were just wrong, wrong the way stone is hard, wrong before I felt anything about them at all. When I read of a man who abused a child, I did not think, *my neural equipment is firing*. I thought, *that is evil*, and I meant it as a report about the man and not about my nerves. I could not get my behavior to sit down inside my theory. The theory said preference. I lived conviction. For years I called that maturity. It was closer to a man standing on a floor he had spent his whole life arguing was not underneath him.

The most honest atheist who ever lived would have had no patience with me. Friedrich Nietzsche watched the educated Europe of his day quietly drop God while keeping every Christian conviction about the equal worth of persons, the duty to the weak, the wrongness of cruelty, and he called it what it was: a bill left unpaid. In *Twilight of the Idols* (1889) he argued that when you let go of the Christian God you forfeit the right to Christian morality, that the whole structure of Western ethics was a cathedral and God was its foundation, and that you could not knock the foundation out and act surprised, a century later, when the floors began to sag. Nietzsche was not being cruel. He was being consistent, more consistent than I was, more consistent than most of the kind unbelievers I have loved. He saw that the death of God was not a repair to the roof. It went all the way down. I did not want him to be right, and wanting had nothing to do with it.

## The choice that was actually on the table

Which leaves the honest unbeliever, and I was one, with a cleaner choice than the debate usually allows. Not between being good and being wicked. Between two accounts of the same good you already practice. On the first, when you say the torture of a child is wrong, you are reporting a fact about the universe, a fact that was true before there were minds to feel it and would be true if every mind denied it, and you owe someone an account of where, in a world of atoms and selection, such a fact could live and how you came to read it. On the second, you are reporting something about yourself, deep and sincere and evolved, and you are calling it a fact because you cannot bear to call it a preference. You are right not to be able to bear it. I could not either. That is the tell. The atheist who keeps living as though cruelty were really, bindingly wrong is not being irrational. He is being better than his own account of the world, and the gap between how he lives and what he can justify is not a small thing to wave off.

The Christian claim is not that unbelievers cannot see the moral law. It is the reverse. Paul writes that the Gentiles who never had the law still "show that the work of the law is written on their hearts, while their conscience also bears witness" (Romans 2:15, ESV). The knowledge of good and evil is not the private property of the religious. It is the common inheritance of everyone made in the image of God, which is why the machinist down the street could shame me at kindness without a single doctrine to his name. And when Scripture reaches for the ground of a moral demand, it does not reach for a rulebook. It reaches for a person: "He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?" (Micah 6:8, ESV). The wrongness of cruelty is not a regulation God issued. It is a description of the grain of things, because things have a grain, because they were made by someone whose own character is the good, and every human face carries his likeness: "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him" (Genesis 1:27, ESV). That is why the child may not be harmed. Not a rule. A likeness.

I am not going to tell you that you cannot be good without God, because you can, and you know decent unbelievers, and so do I, and pretending otherwise would be the lie that discredits every true thing standing near it. The question was always quieter and harder than that. You can be good without God. The trouble is saying what good *is*, and why it holds when no one is feeling it, and how a clever animal on a cooling planet came to be bound by something it did not invent. You already live as if the answer were yes, as if some things are simply, permanently wrong. I did too, for years, on a floor I swore was not under me. The floor held anyway. It is worth asking who laid it.
