There is an atheist in my pulpit every Sunday morning. He is not a visitor slipping in at the back. He is me — the man I was for most of my life, whose reasons I can still recite without notes, standing inside the man who now preaches. Most conversion stories have the earlier man dead by the second paragraph, a cartoon knocked over on the way to the good part. I will not do that to him. He was not a fool, and if I treat him like one, I will deserve the distrust of every reader standing where he stood. Some of you are standing there now, and you can smell a setup from a long way off.

So let me put the facts on the table before the argument touches them. I was raised without a father — not dead, just gone, an absence that was a decision someone kept making every morning by not coming back. I did not drift out of a childhood faith; there was no childhood faith to drift out of. I came to belief as a grown man, against my preference, and I have now spent fifteen years as a pastor — I lead First Baptist Church of Fenton, Michigan — and I am raising five sons on the far side of the word *father*. Everything I am about to say leans on those facts. None of them is decoration.

## The unbelief I actually held

Start with what I believed, because the atheism in most testimonies is a costume, and mine was a conviction. I believed the universe was what it was and owed no one an explanation beyond its own workings. I believed mind was what the brain does, the way digestion is what the stomach does, and that when the brain stopped I would stop, the way a candle stops. I believed morality was a survival strategy our ancestors dressed in the language of commandment, and that the sense of meaning stitched into things was the last and most stubborn illusion — the projection of a small warm animal onto a cold enormous sky. I did not hold any of this as a wound. I held it as maturity. There is a real dignity in facing what you take to be the truth without flinching, and I had it, and I do not disown it now.

I found Bertrand Russell at nineteen and read him the way another young man reads Scripture. In "A Free Man's Worship" (1903) he writes that man is the product of causes that had no prevision of the end they were achieving — that his origin, his hopes, his loves, his beliefs are the outcome of "accidental collocations of atoms." Most people hear that sentence as bleak. I heard it as clean. It asked nothing of me and it promised nothing to me, and after a childhood built around a promise that was not kept, a universe that made no promises felt like the first honest thing anyone had offered me.

I also believed — and believe still — that religion has real crimes on its ledger. It has blessed wars and burned women and shielded men who hurt children, and the reflex of its defenders to change the subject when this comes up seemed to me as damning as the crimes. I will not change the subject now. The church's failures are not a misunderstanding the skeptic needs corrected; they are facts the believer needs to carry. When I finally came to faith, it was not because someone explained those facts away. No one can. It was in spite of them, and a believer who will not say that plainly is asking you to trust a version of history neither of you believes.

And underneath the arguments there was a smaller, harder fact, the one I could not see for years. I was a fatherless boy who had decided very early that the first duty of a mind is to refuse the comforting lie, because somewhere a man had made promises to a woman and a child and had not kept them. I want to be careful here — the same care I would want from you. It would be too easy to say I rejected God the Father because I had no father. Too easy, and it insults the arguments, and it insults the boy. The arguments were real. Some of them remain the best available to the position, and I do not wave them away from this side of the line. But it would also be a lie to leave the absence out of the room. A doctrine that promised a Father arriving felt, at the level below argument, like cruelty dressed as comfort. Something in me had settled the question before the philosophy arrived to defend it: fathers do not come. My unbelief was a conclusion. It was also a home — the one place the father who did not come could not hurt me, because in that place there were no fathers anywhere, and a cold that falls on everyone is not a rejection. I had made the whole sky cold so that my patch of it would stop meaning what it meant.

## What actually moved

If you are waiting for the argument that got me, there was no argument. There was an erosion, and it took years, and for most of those years I did not know it was happening. Conversion stories reach for lightning because lightning sells. The truth is slower and stranger, and it does not flatter the convert.

The first thing that moved was small, and I filed it. At the end of good days — work done well, people I liked — there was a residue. A sense of something owed that no one was paying. An appetite with no object. I had a theory for it, and the theory was good: evolution built into us a hunger for meaning because the hunger was useful, and the hunger firing with nothing to feed on is exactly what the atheist account predicts — a vestigial appetite, like the craving for sugar in a world that no longer requires us to hoard calories. I still think that is the best answer available to the position. But I noticed, over years, that the answer explained the ache without ever once reducing it. A pain that is truly a misfire should get smaller when you see the wiring. This one did not. A hunger that survives its own explanation is a strange kind of hunger, and I did not press on the strangeness, because pressing on it frightened me in a way I could not have justified.

The second thing was a category I met before I believed in its object. Rudolf Otto, in *The Idea of the Holy* (1917), named an experience he called the *mysterium tremendum* — the presence before which a human being trembles and toward which he is drawn, both at once, dread and desire refusing to separate. I read Otto as an unbeliever and recognized the description, which unsettled me more than any proof could have, because I had the experience and my account of the world said the category was empty. Certain music. Certain silence. Moments when the ordinary tilted and something enormous seemed to stand just behind it. I had been calling those moments aesthetics. Otto had the nerve to call them data, and I could not shake the suspicion that he was being more empirical than I was — that I was the one explaining evidence away to protect a theory.

The third thing was reading the wrong book, which is how it usually happens. In *Surprised by Joy* (1955), C.S. Lewis describes the end of his own atheism: "In the Trinity Term of 1929 I gave in, and admitted that God was God, and knelt and prayed: perhaps, that night, the most dejected and reluctant convert in all England." The first time I read that, I took it for a pose — English understatement performing humility. Then it began happening to me, and I understood it was a report. He describes being brought in like a prodigal who comes kicking and struggling, eyes darting for a way out, and that detail is what makes the account credible. A man who wants to believe and talks himself into it has done something psychological. A man who has arranged his whole intellectual life around not believing, and who finds himself believing anyway — against his preference, against his pride, against the boy who decided fathers do not come — has run into something outside himself. I did not want Lewis to be right. Wanting has nothing to do with it. That was his point.

So the arguments did not save me. They cleared the ground. I lost confidence in the clean cosmology one beam at a time — the ache that would not reduce, the category that would not stay empty, the reluctant witnesses who had stood exactly where I stood and been overtaken — until a night came when the resistance simply had nothing left to stand on, and what I had spent years calling loneliness turned out to have been a knock. I did not walk an aisle. There was no music, no stage, no hand on my shoulder. There was a man alone at the end of his reasons, and the oldest and most embarrassing word in the religious vocabulary is the only accurate one. I was found.

## The father who runs

I have to tell you what the text did, because the text did what the philosophy could not. Luke 15 opens with a complaint. The religious professionals are scandalized by the company Jesus keeps — "This man receives sinners and eats with them" (Luke 15:2, ESV) — and he answers the complaint with three stories about lost things: a sheep, a coin, a son. The third story had been waiting for me my whole life.

You know the outline even if you have never opened a Bible. The younger son demands his inheritance early — which, in that world, is telling your father you wish he were dead — takes the money to a far country, and burns it. Famine comes. He ends up feeding pigs and envying their food. And he composes a speech, the way desperate men do, rehearsing it on the road: *Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you. I am no longer worthy to be called your son. Treat me as one of your hired servants* (Luke 15:18–19). It is a good speech. It is theologically correct. It asks for employment instead of sonship, because he has done the math on what he deserves, and the math is right.

Then the line that ended my defenses. "And he arose and came to his father. But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20, ESV). Read it slowly, the way I could not stop reading it. *While he was still a long way off* — which means the father had been watching the road. You do not see a figure at that distance unless you are looking for it, and you do not look for it once. The watching was a habit, the posture of a man who never stopped expecting. *His father saw him and felt compassion* — before the speech, before the apology, before one line of the account is settled. *And ran.* The son starts his rehearsed confession, and if you lay verse 21 against verse 19 you will notice that he never reaches the hired-servant clause. The father is already talking over him, calling for the robe and the ring and the feast (Luke 15:22–24). The speech that was going to negotiate the terms of his diminishment is interrupted by a father who will not let the word *servant* be spoken over his son.

I had spent a lifetime assembling evidence for a single proposition: fathers do not come. It was the axiom under the arguments, the cold under the sky. And here, at the center of the book I had dismissed without reading, was a story built to scandalize exactly that axiom — a father who does not wait to be approached with terms, who has been watching the road the whole time, who runs. The Scriptures say it flatly elsewhere: "Father of the fatherless and protector of widows is God in his holy habitation" (Psalm 68:5, ESV). I did not read that verse and then believe. I believed, and then read that verse, and sat with it a long time, like a man finding his own name in a will.

## What it lost me

I told you this would not be triumphant, and it will not be, because I did not win. I lost. The thing I had spent years building and defending was taken from me, and the losses were real, and I can still itemize them.

I lost the home. Unbelief had been the place where the absence could not reach me — no fathers anywhere, a cold that fell on everyone, a grief universalized until it stopped singling me out. Faith took the roof off that shelter. It handed me back my particular wound with my particular name on it and said the wound had been seen the whole time, which is a comfort, and which is also among the most painful things I have ever been told.

I lost the man who could not be fooled. My unbelief carried more of my identity than any doctrine I now hold; I was the one who saw through things, and I belonged to the company of the clear-eyed, and belonging is not nothing to a boy who belonged to no one. Believing meant becoming, in the eyes of that company, exactly what I had always most feared to be: taken in. There are men I respect who regard a conversion like mine as a failure of nerve. I understand them perfectly. I would have said the same, and said it well.

And I lost the simplicity. Nobody warns you about this. The atheist's problem of suffering is an argument; the believer's problem of suffering is a relationship, and fifteen years of pastoring — hospital corridors, gravesides, marriages coming apart in my office — have made the old objections heavier to me, not lighter, because now they have faces. I pray the word *Father* with no muscle memory for it, learning the vocabulary from the far side, and I am raising five sons while I learn it, trying to be for them the evidence I never had. That weight did not exist when I believed nothing. Unbelief asks less. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

## For the reader still on the other side

If you are standing where I stood, I owe you at the end the same honesty I promised at the beginning, so I will say plainly what I am not doing. I am not going to grab your collar. I spent years on the other side of that grip and I hated it, and no one was ever loved into anything by a checkmate. I do not think you are stupid. Your arguments may be better than the arguments of the believers around you. Mine were.

I will only ask of you the one thing I refused to do, the thing that costs nothing and risks everything. Press on the strangeness. Find the ache your theory explains but never reduces, and instead of filing it, sit with it long enough to notice whether it behaves like a misfire or like a signal. Audit your certainty the way you audit everyone else's — ask how much of it is reasoning and how much is the shape of the room you happen to be standing in, and whether the room has a wound in it, as mine did. You do not have to believe anything at the end of that exercise. That is not mine to ask, and the pressure you are braced for is not coming.

But I will tell you the last thing I know, the thing I could not have known when I was you. The approach does not feel like an approach. It feels like ordinary life — like reading and working and lying awake, like an ache you have explained and a strangeness you have filed and a book you keep not quite throwing across the room. The man being approached is the last to know. I was, for years, standing in the clean cold, certain of one thing above all the others: that no one was coming.

Somebody was watching the road.
