The most respectable thing you can say about Jesus in polite company is that he was a great moral teacher. It costs nothing and offends no one. You get to keep the Sermon on the Mount, the love of enemies, the care for the poor — all the parts modern people still admire — without the embarrassing supernatural machinery. Wise man, ethical genius, perhaps the finest teacher who ever lived, on a shelf next to Socrates and the Buddha. It is the verdict of the thoughtful, secular, generous-minded person, and for years it was mine.

C.S. Lewis thought it was the one verdict the evidence would not let you keep, and he said so in a paragraph that has annoyed clever people ever since.

**Here is what I will argue, and I will sharpen the objections to it before I rest any weight on it: the comfortable verdict — great teacher, nothing more — is the one position the historical Jesus makes almost impossible to hold, because the teaching you admire and the claims you want to discard come from the same mouth, in the same breath, and cannot be cleanly separated.**

## The argument that will not leave you alone

Lewis made it in *Mere Christianity* (1952), and even people who reject it concede it has teeth. A man who was merely human and said the kinds of things Jesus said would not be a great moral teacher. He would either be a liar, or a lunatic, or something far worse. The teacher who tells you to love your neighbor is one thing. The teacher who also tells you that he can forgive your sins, that he existed before Abraham, that he and the Father are one — that man is not handing you ethics. He is making a claim about himself that, if false, is either deliberate fraud or delusion of a clinical kind. You can call him Lord, Lewis said, or you can call him a madman or worse, but the one thing you cannot call him, on the evidence, is simply a wise teacher, because no merely wise teacher talks that way. *That is the one thing we must not say.*

It is a sharp argument. It is also, in the popular form, too quick, and a skeptic is right to push on it. So let me push on it myself, harder than most defenders do.

## The objections, taken seriously

The trilemma — liar, lunatic, or Lord — has at least two real holes, and pretending otherwise is how Christians lose people who can think.

The first hole is the one most often named: legend. The trilemma assumes Jesus actually said these things. But what if he did not? What if the high claims were put in his mouth by a later church that had already decided he was divine, while the historical Jesus was a humble rabbi who would have been horrified to hear himself called God? This is not a fringe position. It was, for a long stretch of the twentieth century, the mainstream scholarly default — the assumption that the divine Jesus was a slow theological accretion, layered onto a simpler human figure over generations. If that is right, the trilemma collapses, because there is a fourth option Lewis left out: legend. Not liar, lunatic, or Lord, but a good teacher whose followers mythologized him after the fact.

The second hole is subtler. Even granting Jesus said remarkable things about himself, the categories may be too clean. Sincere religious figures across history have made elevated claims without being either frauds or psychiatric cases in any simple sense. Human self-understanding is stranger and more layered than three tidy boxes allow. The trilemma can feel like a forced choice that rigs the outcome by limiting the options.

There is a third objection, the gentlest and in some ways the most modern, and it deserves naming because it is the one most thoughtful people actually hold today. It is the pluralist move: that Jesus, like other great teachers, was pointing past himself to a universal truth, and that his followers misunderstood a humble mystic's self-effacement as self-exaltation. On this reading he is closer to a sage who said "the kingdom of God is within you" than to a man claiming to be God. It is an attractive picture. It keeps Jesus admirable and keeps him safely one option among many. The difficulty is that it requires setting aside not stray verses but the entire shape of how he spoke and acted and died, and replacing it with a figure no first-century source actually describes — a serene mystic invented largely in the modern West, retrofitted onto a Galilean who got himself crucified for blasphemy. The pluralist Jesus is the most recent of the legends, and the least anchored in the record.

These objections are fair, and the first is the one that matters most, because if the legend theory holds, the rest is moot. So that is where the historical work has to be done, and it is here that the conversation has shifted in a way most skeptics have not caught up with.

## The legend theory ran out of time

The whole legend account depends on time — on a long enough gap between the human Jesus and the divine Jesus for the myth to grow. Take the time away and the theory has nowhere to stand. And the time has been taken away.

Larry Hurtado spent his career on exactly this question, and his major work, *Lord Jesus Christ* (2003), assembles the evidence that the worship of Jesus as divine did not develop slowly over centuries. It erupted astonishingly early — within the first years after his death, among Jewish monotheists, the very people whose entire religious identity was built on the refusal to worship anyone but the one God. These were not pagans for whom another god was no scandal. These were people who would die before bowing to an idol, and they began, almost immediately, to pray to Jesus, sing to him, baptize in his name, treat him with the devotion reserved for God alone. Hurtado's point is that this is historically extraordinary and demands explanation. Devotion this high, this fast, among these people, does not behave like a legend slowly accreting. It behaves like a response to something that had already detonated their categories.

The early creed Paul quotes in his letters — material older than the gospels, traceable to within a few years of the crucifixion — already treats Jesus in the highest terms. The divine Jesus is not the end of a long evolutionary chain. He is there at the documentable beginning. N.T. Wright has argued the same across his historical work: the evidence does not show a human teacher gradually promoted to godhood by a forgetful church, but a movement that, from its earliest recoverable moment, was already doing the unthinkable thing of worshiping a crucified man within the framework of Jewish monotheism. The legend needed centuries. The record gives it years, and barely that.

N.T. Wright has added a dimension to this that closes a different exit. The popular debate often gets stuck on whether Jesus walked around announcing "I am God" in so many words, and a skeptic can fairly point out that the most explicit statements cluster in John's gospel, which is generally dated later. But Wright's historical work shows the claim does not depend on the explicit formula. It is built into what Jesus did, in the earliest and most securely historical layers, and it is built in the way a first-century Jew would have read it, not the way a modern reader scanning for a creedal statement would. Jesus acted as though he carried an authority the Scriptures reserved for God alone. He spoke as if the temple, the center of Israel's entire encounter with God, was being relocated around himself. He behaved as though Israel's God was returning to his people, in person, in him. To his contemporaries this was not a vague spirituality. It was a set of actions with a single unmistakable meaning, and they tried to kill him for it well before any gospel was written. The high claim is not a late verbal addition. It is the earliest layer's account of why he was executed.

## What he actually said

Strip the question to the texts, and the claims are not buried in later editorializing. They are woven through the earliest layers, often in the most Jewish material, exactly where invention would be hardest.

To a paralyzed man he said, simply, *your sins are forgiven* — and the scribes watching understood the offense instantly, asking who can forgive sins but God alone (Mark 2:5–7). They were right to be scandalized. He was not pronouncing a general truth about God's mercy. He was personally absolving, taking to himself a prerogative that belonged, in their entire theology, to God and no one else. He did not correct their reading. He doubled down by healing the man to prove the point.

To his opponents he said, *before Abraham was, I am* (John 8:58) — and they picked up stones, because the construction was not merely a claim to predate Abraham by centuries. It echoed the divine name God spoke to Moses from the bush. They reached for rocks because they heard, correctly, a man applying the name of God to himself. Elsewhere, plainly, *I and the Father are one,* and again the stones come out, and when he asks which good work they are stoning him for, they answer that it is not for any work but for blasphemy, because *you, being a man, make yourself God* (John 10:30–33). The hostile witnesses in the text understood the claim better than the modern admirer does. They did not hear a great teacher. They heard a man claiming the unclaimable, and they responded the way first-century monotheists would.

And when he asked his own disciples directly who they thought he was, and Peter answered that he was the Messiah, the Son of the living God, Jesus did not deflect or correct the title (Matthew 16:13–16). He received it. He blessed the man for seeing it. A merely humble rabbi, horrified to be called divine, had every opportunity in these scenes to say *no, you misunderstand, I am only a teacher.* The texts never record him saying it. The texts record him accepting worship, forgiving sins, and applying the name of God to himself — and dying, in the end, on the specific charge of blasphemy.

## Why the comfortable verdict fails

Now the trilemma can be put back together, stronger for having survived its objections, and aimed where it actually bites.

The legend escape is closed, or at least badly weakened, by Hurtado and the early creeds and the speed of the devotion: the high claims are not late additions but early bedrock. And once that escape is closed, the comfortable position is in real trouble, because the comfortable position depends on cleanly separating the ethics you admire from the claims you reject — keeping the Sermon on the Mount and discarding *before Abraham was, I am* — when both come from the same person, in the same sources, in the same voice. You cannot run a scalpel between them. The man who told you to love your enemies is the man who told you he could forgive their sins against God. If he was wrong about the second, you have no obvious grounds to trust him on the first, because a man that wrong about himself is not a reliable guide to anything, however lovely his aphorisms.

That is the force of Lewis's argument once it has been pressure-tested. It does not prove Jesus was God. A skeptic can still conclude he was deluded, or that the sincere claims of a remarkable figure resist the neat categories. Those remain open. The one position that does not remain open, on the actual evidence, is the flattering middle — the wise teacher who never disturbs you. That man is the invention. The historical Jesus is the one who disturbs.

Consider what the trilemma's remaining options actually cost, because the comfortable view survives partly by never pricing them. Take the lunatic reading: a man under a delusion of divinity grand enough to forgive sins and accept worship. Clinical grandiosity of that order does not generally coexist with the teaching attributed to Jesus — the precision, the moral depth, the penetrating realism about human nature, the steadiness under interrogation. The Sermon on the Mount is not the work of a disordered mind, and the parables are not the productions of a man who has lost contact with reality. The same evidence that makes us want to call him a great teacher is the evidence that makes the lunatic verdict hard to sustain. Now take the liar reading: a deliberate fraud who manufactured the claims for gain. But he gained nothing a fraud seeks. He acquired enemies, poverty, and an execution he saw coming and walked toward. Frauds do not generally die for the con when recanting would save them. Each remaining option carries a weight the admirer rarely weighs, and the weight is what makes the comfortable verdict feel light by comparison — it is light because it has quietly declined to pay anything at all.

## The verdict you cannot avoid

I held the comfortable view sincerely, and I want to name what was really going on in it, because I think it is common. I admired Jesus. I quoted him. And I had arranged the admiration so that it never cost me anything — I had filed him under great teachers, which is the file you keep things in when you want to honor them without obeying them. The great-teacher verdict is not the conclusion of careful thought. It is the most comfortable place to set down a person you find too compelling to dismiss and too demanding to follow.

The historical Jesus will not stay in that file. He made claims that a great teacher does not make, that the legend theory cannot explain away, that his earliest Jewish followers responded to with the worship they owed to God alone. You are left with the choice his contemporaries faced, and it was never the polite three-way of the seminar. It was the one he put to his friends on the road, and then waited for the answer.

*Who do you say that I am.* He is still standing there, and the one reply he has made unavailable is the one most people reach for first.
