Before it is a debate, it is a person.

It is a fourteen-year-old who cannot explain to you why the body they woke up in feels like a coat that belongs to someone else, only that the feeling does not lift when they are told to pray it away, and does not lift when they are told it is a phase, and has been there so long they cannot remember being without it. It is a mother in a car in a parking lot, not going in yet, because she does not know how to hold her child and hold her church in the same two hands, and she is terrified that whichever one she puts down, she loses. That is where this actually lives. Not in the panel discussion. In the car.

So I want to say the first thing plainly, and I want it to cost the people on my own side of the aisle something to read. The distress is real. It is not invented, it is not a trend a kid tried on for attention, and for most people who carry it, it was not chosen any more than you chose the color of your eyes. Gender dysphoria, the deep and persistent ache of being at war with your own body, is a real form of suffering, and it is documented, and the church does not get to wave that away because it finds the subject uncomfortable. I am not a physician, and nothing here is medical advice. But I have sat with enough people in enough dark rooms to know the difference between a person who is faking and a person who is drowning, and the first thing the church owes anyone drowning is to stop debating the water and get in it.

We have not done that. Much of the time we have done the opposite. We have taken one of the smallest and most frightened populations of human beings and turned them into a plank in a platform, a thing to be against, a line in a fundraising letter. I have to name that as sin before I say anything else, because it is mine to name. I have stood in rooms where a trans person was discussed the way you would discuss a policy, and I did not always stop it, and the silence was a kind of consent. If a suffering person cannot walk into a church without becoming somebody's political point, we have already failed them before the theology ever gets a word in.

## The case I am asked to caricature, at full strength

Let me give the accommodating view the strongest form its most serious holders would actually sign, because if I beat a straw man here I have lied to you.

It goes like this. A person in the grip of dysphoria is not confused about a doctrine. They are in pain, sometimes the kind of pain that does not let them see a future. The compassionate reading of Scripture, this view says, begins where Jesus began, with the person in front of him and not with the rule about them. He touched the untouchable. He let himself be interrupted by the bleeding woman the law said made him unclean. He was found, over and over, on the wrong side of the respectable, and the people he was hardest on were never the suffering. They were the ones who used holiness as a wall.

And so, this view continues, when we meet someone whose distress lifts, whose sleep returns, whose desire to be alive comes back when they are allowed to live as the gender they experience themselves to be, we are not looking at rebellion. We are looking at relief. To withhold that relief in the name of a principle, when the alternative may be a person who does not survive their own body, is to love an abstraction more than a neighbor. The people who hold this are not, at their most serious, trying to abolish the idea of male and female. They are trying to keep a child alive, and they are asking the church a question it has been very slow to answer honestly: what is your certainty worth, if it is paid for with their life?

That question deserves to land. I am not going to pretend it away.

## The conviction I am asked to abandon, at full strength

Now turn, and give the historic Christian conviction the same refusal to caricature, because it is not what its critics say it is either. It is not disgust with a fancy vocabulary. At its best it is not about controlling anyone. It is about a claim regarding what a human being actually is.

The claim is this. The body is not a costume the real self wears and may exchange. It is not luggage the soul is carrying. It is you, part of the actual you, given and not chosen, and that givenness is a mercy and not a prison. "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27, ESV). The historic reading hears in that verse something good and load-bearing: that being made male and female is not an accident evolution left on us to be corrected, but a created reality, spoken by God over the world before anything went wrong, and called good along with the rest of it. On this account the ache to be free of your body is real, and it is also part of the deep wrongness that runs through the whole world after the fall, the same wrongness that puts cancer in good cells and grief in good love. The answer to a body at war with itself, this view says, is not finally to remake the body to match the mind, because the mind is no more infallible than the body. The answer, the one we are all still waiting on, is resurrection, the day the whole self is made whole.

And there is a second thing in this view that the culture war never lets you hear, so let me say it slowly. A person's worth has nothing to do with how well they perform masculinity or femininity. Nothing. The boy who is gentle and hates the things boys are supposed to like is not less a man, and the church that told him so was preaching a culture, not a Christ. Half the freight a dysphoric kid is carrying was loaded onto them by a world, sometimes a church, that decided there was one narrow way to be a boy or a girl and made them a stranger for failing it. The historic conviction, held rightly, does not add to that weight. It lifts it. "I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made. Wonderful are your works; my soul knows it very well" (Psalm 139:14, ESV). The body you have is not a mistake you must escape to be loved. That is meant to be good news, and we have too often preached it as a threat.

## Naming the order, and refusing the shortcut

So I have to be honest about what kind of question this is, because we sort questions badly and it kills people.

There is a first-order center here that the creeds do bind: that the human being is God's good creation, made in his image, body and soul together, and destined not for deletion but for resurrection. That is not up for a vote. But the pastoral and medical particulars, what compassion actually requires in a given life, at a given age, in a given body, with a given history, are hard and genuinely contested, and they are contested among people who share every word of that first-order faith. Serious, Christ-loving believers who agree entirely that the body is a good gift still disagree, sometimes sharply, about what faithfulness looks like across the desk from a suffering fifteen-year-old. Anyone who tells you that part is simple is selling you the comfort of a tribe, and it does not matter which tribe.

And this is where I refuse both shortcuts with the same hand, because I have watched both of them do damage.

The church's shortcut is cruelty dressed as conviction. It is the mockery, the sneer, the man in the pulpit who gets a laugh at the expense of a category of people he has never once sat with. It is turning a doctrine of the body's goodness into a weapon against particular bodies. Whatever the historic view is, that is a betrayal of it, because you cannot preach that a person is fearfully and wonderfully made and then treat them as a punchline in the same breath. I have to own that my own side produced that sneer, and often excused it, and called the excusing courage.

The culture's shortcut is the pretense that none of it is hard. It is the confidence that every question here is already settled, that there is no cost anywhere, no grief, no wisdom required, that the only possible reason for hesitation is hatred. That is not true, and it is not kind, because it hands a frightened family a certainty that the actual research and the actual medicine and the actual long human record do not yet support, and it does it with the same untroubled ease the church uses on the other side. Both shortcuts spare the speaker the weight. Both make the suffering person carry it instead.

## What I will not do, and what I will

I am not going to stand on a website and rule on your child's medicine. I do not know your child. I do not know their body or their history or the particular edge they are standing on, and a man who does not know those things and issues a verdict anyway is not being brave. He is being reckless with a life that is not his. Those decisions belong to the person living them, to the family who loves them, to doctors who can actually examine them, and to a pastor who has earned the right to be in the room by staying in it. Not to me, and not to a stranger with a strong opinion and no skin in your family's grief.

What I will do is tell you what I am fairly sure the church owes, underneath the part we cannot yet resolve.

We owe presence before position. A trans person, or a parent in that parking lot, should be able to walk into a congregation and be known by their name and their face before they are known by this, and be loved in a way that does not depend on the argument coming out where we would prefer. We owe honesty about our own failures, the boy we shamed for being soft, the family we let walk out alone. We owe the refusal to lie in either direction, neither pretending the questions are cost-free nor pretending a hurting person is a threat. And we owe a path to real help kept always in view: a wise pastor who will stay, a counselor trained for this weight, real medical care from people who know you, and, if you are reading this and you have reached the place where you are not sure you want to be here at all, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, which you can call or text right now, tonight, because staying is not weakness and reaching for help is one of the most faithful things a person can do. That last one is not a footnote. For someone reading this, it is the only sentence that matters, and I would rather you skip everything else and keep that number.

## Where I leave you

I am not going to resolve this for you, and I want to be honest that it is not because I am hiding my hand. It is because the thing itself is not yet resolved on this side of the kingdom, and the people who claim otherwise, on the right and on the left, are the ones I trust least in the room with an actual child.

But I can tell you the company you are in. You are in the company of a Savior who was himself, in the words of the prophet, "despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief" (Isaiah 53:3, ESV), who knows exactly what it is to be looked at and turned away from, who was not fooled by the world's confidence about who was worth touching. When his disciples looked at a suffering man and asked whose fault it was, he said, "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him" (John 9:3, ESV). He would not let the suffering be a verdict. He would not let it be a debate. He went to the man.

So if you are the teenager who cannot stand to be in your own skin, hear at least this: you are not a category, you are not a mistake, and you are not alone in the dark as much as it feels like it. And if you are the mother in the parking lot, still not going in: go in. Not because the church has all of this figured out. It does not, and I have told you so. Go in because you should not have to carry your child by yourself, and the whole point of the thing, when it is being what it is supposed to be, is that you do not have to. Bring the child God gave you, exactly as afraid as you both are. That is not the failure of faith. On the worst nights, it is the whole of it.
