When I was an atheist, I kept a short list of arguments I considered closed, and this one sat near the top. If your God is good, and your book is his word, then somewhere across its thousands of verses it should manage four words: own no human being. It never does. It regulates the thing instead. It tells masters how to treat their slaves and slaves how to obey their masters, and for the better part of two thousand years men who read it in the original languages, who preached it on Sundays and broke bread at its table, used it to buy and brand and sell other human beings. I did not hold this as a difficulty for the faith. I held it as the end of the conversation, the card you put on the table when the believer across from you has run out of others.

So let me put the objection down at its full weight, because I carried it at full weight, and a version you can knock over is not the version that keeps an honest person awake.

## The card I used to play

Here is the argument, and I will not shave a corner off it. This is a God who found the breath to forbid boiling a young goat in its mother's milk, who legislated the fibers in a garment and the shellfish on a plate, who spelled out in exhausting detail the handling of a hundred smaller things. On the one institution that turns a person into property, that takes a face and files it under livestock, he went quiet. Worse than quiet. He gave instructions for running it. Read Leviticus 25 to the end and you reach the part nobody prints on a coffee mug: "you may buy male and female slaves from among the nations that are around you" (Leviticus 25:44), and you may leave them "to your sons after you to inherit as a possession forever" (Leviticus 25:46). That is not a loan with a due date. That is ownership, and it is heritable, passed down like a field or a herd.

And the New Testament, where the objection expects the correction to finally arrive, does not arrive at abolition either. When Paul writes to the churches and the moment comes to say the plain thing, he tells slaves to obey their earthly masters (Ephesians 6:5). He sends a runaway named Onesimus back to the man who owned him. The book that could have ended it chose, at every hinge, to manage it.

Now grant the history, all of it, because the history is not a misunderstanding the skeptic needs corrected. It is a debt the believer has to carry. George Whitefield, the great revivalist, lobbied to legalize slavery in the colony of Georgia and held enslaved people himself. Jonathan Edwards owned them. When the American crisis came, the most confident biblical arguments in the room were the pro-slavery ones. Southern pulpits ran through "Slaves, obey your masters" and the so-called curse of Ham in Genesis 9 and the return of Onesimus, and they built a theology of bondage that a great many devout, literate, churchgoing people found more scriptural than the abolitionist reading. Frederick Douglass, who had been owned, drew the line himself in the appendix to his 1845 Narrative: he loved what he called the Christianity of Christ and despised what he called the slaveholding, hypocritical Christianity of the land, and he insisted the two were not the same religion but as opposite as heaven and hell. He earned the right to that sentence in a way I never will. If you do not feel the force of what he was saying, you are not ready for the next paragraph, and neither was I for most of my life.

So I am not going to tell you the problem is smaller than it looks. I am going to tell you what I found when I finally did the one thing I had refused to do, which was read the whole book instead of the verses that armed me.

## What the distinction is, and what it is not

The first thing an honest reader has to say is that the word slavery is carrying two very different cargoes, and the modern ear hears only one. When an American hears the word, the picture is fixed: a race-based system of permanent, heritable ownership, sustained by kidnapping across an ocean, in which a human being was chattel from birth to death and their children were born into it. That is the Atlantic trade. Most of what the Hebrew Scriptures regulate is a different institution: debt-servitude, a way a person crushed by poverty in a world with no bankruptcy court and no welfare could sell his labor to survive, with the law forcing a release. "When you buy a Hebrew slave, he shall serve six years, and in the seventh he shall go out free, for nothing" (Exodus 21:2). Every fiftieth year the trumpet sounded and the debts collapsed and the land reverted: "proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants" (Leviticus 25:10). That sentence is carved on the Liberty Bell. The abolitionists knew exactly where they got it.

That distinction is real. And I want to be careful, because it is the exact place a defender of the faith is tempted to stop, plant a flag, and call it a victory. It is not a victory. It does not touch Leviticus 25:44 and 46. The permanent, heritable ownership of foreigners sat right there in the same law, and no amount of context dissolves it into something comfortable. An honest believer holds both hands open: one holding the release laws that have no parallel in the ancient world, the other holding a permission that troubles him and that he does not get to explain away. If the distinction were the whole answer, I would not have needed the rest of the book. I did.

## The seeds planted to detonate later

Here is what I had never noticed, because you cannot see the shape of a thing while you are using it as a weapon. Scattered through the same Scriptures that regulate slavery are lines that cannot coexist with it. Not one grand decree. A set of charges laid quietly under the foundation, timed to go off centuries after they were set.

Begin with the founding story of the whole people. Israel does not start with a philosophy or a king. It starts with a God who hears slaves. "I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters. I know their sufferings" (Exodus 3:7). The defining act of this God, the one the nation was commanded to recite forever, is the breaking of a slave economy and the walking of its labor force out into the desert as free people. A religion can carry a lot of bad readings. It cannot carry them forever when its central memory is a jailbreak.

Then the charge that condemns the entire Atlantic trade at its root. Three lines after the verse that armed the slaveholders, the same chapter of Exodus says this: "Whoever steals a man and sells him, and anyone found in possession of him, shall be put to death" (Exodus 21:16). Manstealing. Kidnapping a person to own or sell him. In the Torah it is not a misdemeanor. It is a capital crime, ranked with murder. And the Atlantic slave trade was, from the first raid on an African village to the last auction block in a Southern square, one unbroken chain of manstealing. Every person in it had been stolen, or was the child of someone stolen. By the plain letter of the law the slaveholders claimed to obey, every trader and every buyer and every heir in that system stood condemned to death. Paul says it again in the New Testament, listing "enslavers" among the worst of the lawless (1 Timothy 1:10), using a Greek word that means precisely the slave-trader, the man who deals in stolen people. The book they used to justify the trade sentenced them in two testaments.

There is more, and it runs against every instinct of a slave society. The Torah forbids returning a runaway: "You shall not give up to his master a slave who has escaped from his master to you" (Deuteronomy 23:15). Read that beside the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, which compelled Americans to hunt and return the escaped, and you see two laws pointing in opposite directions, and only one of them is in the Bible.

And then the two lines that finished the argument the whole book had been building. Paul sends the runaway Onesimus back, yes, but read what he tells the owner to receive him as: "no longer as a bondservant but more than a bondservant, as a beloved brother" (Philemon 16). Once you have called the man you own your beloved brother, the institution has already died inside you; it is only a matter of time before it dies around you. And to the churches Paul writes the sentence that cannot share a room with a slave market: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28).

## Why plant a seed instead of swing a sword

So the honest question sharpens. Not why the Bible is silent, because it is not silent. Why it works this way, laying charges instead of issuing the decree, planting what will grow up through the floor rather than kicking the door down on day one.

I think the answer is a pattern that runs through the whole of how this God moves, and I resisted it for years because it asks for patience and I wanted a verdict. He does not, as a rule, abolish a fallen order by fiat and leave the hearts inside it unchanged. He plants the thing that will make the order unbearable to the people who have been changed. A decree bans a practice. A brother cannot be owned. The first can be repealed by the next regime. The second, once it is truly believed, unmakes the man who believes it, and then he unmakes the practice himself, and it does not come back. It is the slower way and it is the more thorough one, and the cost of it is generations, and the people who pay that cost while the seed grows are real, and I will not pretend that arithmetic is painless. It is not. It is the hardest part of the whole account.

And when the harvest finally came, it came from exactly where the seeds were planted. Abolition in the English-speaking world was not, whatever the modern telling suggests, mainly a gift of secular reason to a backward church. William Wilberforce, who drove the trade's abolition through Parliament in 1807 and lived just long enough to hear that slavery itself would end in the British Empire in 1833, dying three days later, did it out of an evangelical conversion he dated to 1785 and never stopped naming as the engine of the work. John Newton, who had captained a slave ship before he wrote "Amazing Grace," turned and wrote against the trade and mentored Wilberforce in the fight. The Quakers moved first and moved hardest. And in America the deepest reservoir of abolitionist conviction was the Black church, the enslaved themselves, who heard the Exodus read and knew whose story it was, who sang Moses down into Egypt to tell old Pharaoh to let the people go. They did not get that from the Enlightenment. They got it from the book their masters had handed them expecting it to make them docile, and it made them free instead.

That is the fact the objection cannot hold and I could not hold when I was making it. The same Bible was quoted on both sides. It was. But one side had to reach for scattered verses and squint past the arc of the whole; the other side had the Exodus, the manstealing law, the runaway protected, the brother who cannot be owned, and the sentence that there is no longer slave nor free. One reading was faithful to the book's own center of gravity, and one was a hostage-taking of its margins, and history did not finally leave that question open.

## The verdict I owe my younger self

I told you I used to play this as a trump card, and I want to end by being fair to the man who did. It was not nothing. He was pointing at real texts and real blood, and the church's complicity in that blood is not a debating point to be swept up and filed. It is a wound the believer carries into the room and sets on the table before he says anything else. I carry it now.

But a trump card is a thing you play to end a game, and I had confused ending the game with finding the truth. The book did not stay silent. It hid a death sentence in the fine print of the slaveholder's own law, called the owned man a brother, and made the freeing of slaves the story a whole people were commanded never to forget. It did not ban slavery in a sentence. It buried the charge that would blow the institution apart, and then it waited for people who believed it enough to light the fuse.

I wanted a God who would have said the four words. I have come to think he did something harder to argue with. He made the men who owned slaves read, every year, about the God who set them free.
