Every invitation to believe you have ever received was trying to sell you something, and you knew it before the words landed. The tone gives it away. The warmth that arrives a little too fast, the promise of peace with no visible price, the sense that the man across the table needs your yes more than you could ever need his God. You have learned to distrust that, and you are right to. A pitch that costs the buyer nothing is a pitch with something to hide.

So I am not going to pitch you. I am going to hand you the bill first, itemized, before you decide anything, because the most dishonest thing the modern church does is bury the cost under the welcome. If you are starting to take this seriously, you deserve to know what serious means. It does not mean a better weekend.

Let me start by conceding the objection I held for most of my life, because it is a good one and you can feel it even if you have never read the men who sharpened it.

## The crutch

The suspicion goes like this. Religion is a comfort mechanism dressed as truth. It exists because the alternative is unbearable: a universe that did not plan for us, a father in the sky invented to replace the ones who fail us down here, a story that makes death survivable and suffering meaningful and our own smallness less humiliating. Sigmund Freud made the case with real force in *The Future of an Illusion* (1927). Belief in a divine father, he argued, is not a conclusion reached from evidence but a wish, the projection of a child's longing for protection onto a cosmos that has none to give. Decades earlier Karl Marx had already named the same thing from a different angle, calling religion the opium of the people, the sigh of the oppressed.

I want to grant this at full strength, because I once made the argument myself and made it well. There is something in the human animal that wants the universe to be a home rather than an accident, and a story that promises exactly that will always be under suspicion of having been engineered to answer the want. If you smell wish-fulfillment near faith, you are noticing something real. Whole industries of religion are built on precisely that smell: the comfortable God, the therapeutic God, the one who exists to make you feel chosen and calm. That version deserves every ounce of your suspicion.

Now watch what happens when you take that suspicion and lay it against the man the whole thing is supposedly about.

## The recruiter who talks you out of it

A crutch is designed to bear weight for you. It takes the load off. That is its entire function. If Christianity were the comfort mechanism the objection describes, then the founder of it would be a recruiter, easing the terms, lowering the bar, closing the sale. Read the accounts and you find the reverse so consistently that it becomes the strangest feature of the record. Jesus keeps talking people out of following him.

The crowds get large in Luke's fourteenth chapter, exactly the moment a salesman softens the offer, and instead he turns and raises it. "For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it?" (Luke 14:28). It is a builder's warning, not an altar call. Do the arithmetic before you commit, he says, and then he lands it without a cushion: "So therefore, any one of you who does not renounce all that he has cannot be my disciple" (Luke 14:33). No man selling comfort tells the crowd to renounce everything and count the cost before they sign. That is what you say when you are trying to make sure the people who follow you know precisely what they are surrendering.

Then there is the man who came ready to buy. He runs up, kneels, asks the right question about eternal life, keeps all the commandments, and he is exactly the convert any movement would want: earnest, moral, wealthy, sincere. Mark records that "Jesus, looking at him, loved him, and said to him, 'You lack one thing: go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me'" (Mark 10:21). The man's face falls. "Disheartened by the saying, he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions" (Mark 10:22). Here is the tell. Jesus loves him, watches him leave, and does not chase. Does not amend the terms. Does not call after him with a smaller number. He lets him walk, and grieves, and holds the price where it was.

And underneath the individual encounters sits the image he chose to describe the whole thing, an image so violent his first hearers could not have romanticized it the way we have. "If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me" (Mark 8:34). The cross was not a metaphor for a hard week. It was the instrument the empire used to execute its condemned in public, and everyone in that crowd had seen one used. To take up your cross was to walk out carrying the beam of your own execution down the road toward the place where you would die on it. That is the recruiting poster. Deny yourself and bring the means of your death. This is not the language of a system that runs on making you comfortable.

## The bill, itemized

So let me be as plain as he was. Here is what following him costs, and none of it is your weekend.

It costs your autonomy. You are no longer the final authority on your own life. This is the deep one, under all the others. The modern self is built on exactly the opposite premise: that I am the author of my own meaning, the one who decides, answerable to no one. The New Testament says the transaction runs the other way. "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price" (1 Corinthians 6:19-20). Read as an insult, it is intolerable. Read as it is meant, it is the end of the exhausting project of being your own god, a job you were never qualified for and cannot quit until something larger relieves you of it. Every other cost is a footnote to this one.

It costs money its grip on you. Not necessarily your money, though for the rich young man it was exactly that, but its grip, its status as the thing you finally trust. "No one can serve two masters, for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and money" (Matthew 6:24). Notice he does not say it is unwise to serve both. He says it is impossible, that money is not a neutral tool but a rival lord with a claim on your worship, and following him means firing it as the thing that tells you whether you are safe.

It costs you the right to define yourself. Deny himself, the sentence said, and the modern ear hears cruelty in it, the erasure of the self we have spent a lifetime being told to discover and express. But the self being denied is the self as sovereign, the one who insists on writing his own moral law and calling it freedom. You do not get to keep that seat. Something else sits there now.

And it can cost you relationships and standing, which is the cost people discover last and feel worst. Jesus was clear that the road runs through a certain kind of loss: "you will be hated by all for my name's sake" (Matthew 10:22). Not by everyone, not always, but by some, and sometimes the some are close. There are dinner tables that go quiet. There are men I once respected who regard a conversion like mine as a failure of nerve, and I understand them perfectly, because I would have said the same and said it well. The clear-eyed company you belong to when you believe nothing does not applaud when you leave it.

## Cheap grace

The church knows all of this, and the church has spent centuries figuring out how to keep the welcome and lose the bill. Dietrich Bonhoeffer gave the counterfeit its permanent name. Writing in Germany in 1937, in the book that reached English readers as *The Cost of Discipleship*, he called it cheap grace, and he called it the deadly enemy of the church. Cheap grace, in his account, is grace we grant ourselves: forgiveness preached without repentance, comfort offered without surrender, the benefits of the faith unhooked from the following that gives them their meaning. It is grace without discipleship, grace without the cross. It is the church selling absolution at a price so low that nothing about the buyer has to change, and Bonhoeffer's charge is that this bargain rate is not mercy but the death of the thing it claims to give away.

He had standing to say it. Costly grace, the real article, cost him his life. He was hanged by the Nazi regime in April 1945, weeks before the war ended, for his part in resisting it, after choosing to return to a Germany he could have safely stayed out of. A man does not walk back into that on the strength of a comfort mechanism. He wrote the sentence about the cost of grace and then paid it, which is the only kind of author worth reading on it.

## What the price is evidence of

Here is the turn, and it is the whole reason I bothered to itemize the bill instead of hiding it the way I was taught to.

A con artist sells you an easy yes. That is the entire mechanics of the con: lower the resistance, shrink the ask, get the signature before the buyer does the math. Nobody runs a scam by handing the mark a bill this size and daring him to walk away. The soft sell is the swindle's native tongue. The demand you have been reading is its opposite in every particular. It counts the cost out loud, tells the earnest rich man the price and lets him leave, hands you the beam of your own execution, and asks you to sit down and be sure before you build.

I am not claiming that difficulty proves truth. Plenty of false things are hard, and cruelty can wear the costume of demand. But the specific suspicion you brought to the table, that this is a comfortable lie engineered to relieve your fear, cannot survive contact with what the thing actually asks. Whatever else it is, it is not comfortable, and it was not engineered to relieve you. The size of the demand does not settle whether it is true. It does settle whether it is a crutch. A crutch is the one thing it structurally cannot be.

## The strange arithmetic

Which leaves the hardest question, the one the skeptic in me held longest. If the cost is real, and it is, then is the whole thing just an expensive way to lose your life.

Read the sentence that comes immediately after take up your cross, because he did not leave the demand standing alone. "For whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake and the gospel's will save it" (Mark 8:35). This is the arithmetic the gospel runs on, and it is not a slogan. It is a claim about the structure of a human being. The self you are asked to surrender, the sovereign self that authors its own meaning and clutches its money and its standing and its right to define itself, is not the self that was ever going to make you free. It is the one that was quietly killing you, the way a clenched fist cannot receive anything because it is too busy holding on. What the following costs you is precisely the thing that could not have saved you. The surrender is not the price you pay to get the life later. The surrender is where the life turns out to have been the whole time.

I cannot prove that to you. I can only tell you that I spent years defending the sovereign self as the last honest thing a clear-eyed man had left, and that I was wrong about it, because the wound in me had a vested interest in the argument. When it finally cost me that self, I did not experience the loss the objection predicts. I experienced it as the roof coming off a shelter I had mistaken for a home.

## What remains

So I will not ask you to decide anything tonight. The whole point of handing you the bill was to refuse the trick of the easy yes, and it would be a poor ending to smuggle the trick back in at the door.

I will only ask you to hold two things together long enough to feel the tension in them. The first is that this costs more than anyone selling it to you has probably admitted: your autonomy, money's grip, your right to be your own final word, sometimes the table you sit at and the people who admired you. The second is that a thing which asks for all of that, out loud, before you commit, letting the rich man walk rather than shave the price, is behaving in the one way a comfortable lie never does.

You can still refuse it. People did, to his face, and he let them go without chasing. But if you refuse it, refuse it for the right reason. Not because it is a crutch. It is the heaviest thing anyone will ever hand you. Refuse it, if you must, because you have counted the cost and found it too high, and then sit for a while with the question the counting leaves behind: what would have to be true, on the other side of a bill that size, for the price to be worth paying.
