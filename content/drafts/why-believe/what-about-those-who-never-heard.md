The question that undoes people is not usually the one about whether God exists. It is the one a teenager asks on the drive home from a mission conference, and it goes like this: what about the man who lived and died on some mountainside a thousand years before a missionary ever crossed the water, who never heard the name of Jesus, not once, not from anyone. Is he in hell for missing a message that was never sent to him. Ask it out loud in most churches and watch the room get careful. It is the soft place in the exclusive claim, the one that makes even settled believers change the subject, because it seems to make salvation a lottery run by geography, and a God who runs that lottery is a God a decent person cannot worship without lying.

I want to take this one slowly, because it is the kind of question people leave the faith over, and they usually leave holding an answer they were handed too fast, in either direction. I have been handed both. I have been told, briskly, that yes, of course the man is condemned, that is what the seriousness of missions depends on, and any softness here is the first step out the door. And I have been told, just as briskly, that a loving God would never, that everyone is fine in the end, sleep well. Both answers share a family resemblance I have learned to distrust. They both resolve the ache too quickly, and they both claim to know something the Scriptures guard more carefully than either camp admits.

Let me give the hard position its full due first, because it is not the cartoon its critics make of it. The case that saving faith requires actually hearing and trusting Christ is not cruelty for its own sake. It is built on a text. Paul writes to Rome, "How then will they call on him in whom they have not believed? And how are they to believe in him of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without someone preaching?" (Romans 10:14). That is a chain, and it runs from hearing to believing to being saved, and it is the engine of every missionary who ever left home. Take it away and you have, the hard position warns, cut the nerve of the Great Commission. If the unreached are already safe in their unreachedness, then the most loving thing the church could do is stop telling them, leave them undisturbed, let sleeping nations lie. The restrictivist is guarding something real: that the message is not decoration, that it matters eternally whether it arrives. Do not wave that away. A gospel it makes no difference to hear is not the gospel the apostles died to carry.

But hold that against another set of texts, ones the confident restrictivist tends to hurry past. In the very same letter, two chapters earlier, Paul says something that does not fit the tidy chain. He writes that "when Gentiles, who do not have the law, by nature do what the law requires, they are a law to themselves, even though they do not have the law. They show that the work of the law is written on their hearts" (Romans 2:14-15). Paul is willing to say that people who never received the revelation still have something of God's requirement inscribed in them, and that God's judgment will reckon with what they actually had, not with what they never received. And then there is Peter, standing in the house of a Roman soldier who had never been a Jew and had certainly never been a Christian, saying, "Truly I understand that God shows no partiality, but in every nation anyone who fears him and does what is right is acceptable to him" (Acts 10:34-35). Whatever that means, and it is debated, it does not mean that God is trapped by a man's zip code.

So the church has held more than one faithful answer here, and honesty requires naming them rather than pretending yours is the only one a serious Christian could reach. There is the restrictivist answer: explicit faith in this life, and God is just in ways we must trust past our sight. There is the inclusivist answer, held by many who love the Scriptures no less: that the only ground of anyone's salvation is still the cross of Christ and nothing else, but that God may apply that finished work to a person who never heard the name, the way, everyone agrees, he applied it to Abraham and Moses and every saint who died before Bethlehem, none of whom ever heard the name either, all of whom were saved by a Christ they knew only as promise and shadow. C. S. Lewis reached for this in a story, when he let a lifelong worshiper of a false god be received in the end by the true one, on the grounds that the man's honest seeking had, without his knowing it, always been aimed at the only God there is. You may find that too generous. Others find it the only reading that lets God be both just and the Savior of the OT saints in the same breath. The point is not that the question is easy. The point is that it is genuinely open, that Scripture speaks to it in more than one register, and that this is not first-order ground where the creeds have bound us. It is a place where brothers disagree and remain brothers.

What I want to say is that we have been arguing about the wrong thing, or at least arguing about it in the wrong order. We keep trying to settle the mechanism, to get God to hand over the roster so we can stop feeling the ache. He has not handed it over. What he has handed over is something better and harder to hold, which is his own character, put on the record in the one place the question was first raised. Long before any missionary conference, Abraham stood in front of God, who was about to judge a wicked city, and Abraham did not flatter him. He argued. He said the thing every honest doubter since has wanted to say: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Genesis 18:25). And the staggering thing is that God does not rebuke the question. He honors it. He lets Abraham press him, again and again, down to a handful of righteous, and he yields ground every time. The Bible does not treat the worry about God's fairness as impertinence to be silenced. It puts that worry in the mouth of the father of the faithful and lets it stand.

That is the pivot the whole matter turns on, and I missed it for years because I wanted the list instead. We are not given the list. We are given the Judge. And the Judge is not a distant sovereign applying a policy from behind glass. The Judge is the one who did not stay behind the glass, who crossed every distance himself, who was born into an occupied backwater precisely so that no one could say God ruled on human life from a safe remove. When you ask whether the man on the mountainside will be dealt with justly, you are asking about the character of the specific God who went to a cross rather than spare himself. You already know more about his fairness than the question assumes. You know what he was willing to spend.

None of this loosens the church's feet from the road. This is the part the soft answer never sees. If I do not know that the unreached are automatically safe, and I do not, then their reaching is not optional, and my comfort is not the point. The uncertainty cuts toward the mission, not away from it. It was never our job to calculate who God can and cannot reach without us. It was our job to go, because the message is not decoration, and because the God who wrote his law on every human heart clearly wants that law answered by a face and a name and a cross, not left as a rumor in the conscience. The inclusivist who uses his hope as a reason to stay home has betrayed the very grace he claims to trust.

So I will not tell you where the man on the mountainside stands, because I do not know, and the people who told you they knew, in either direction, were telling you something the text withholds. What I will tell you is the one thing the text does not withhold. The Judge of all the earth will do what is just. He proved what he was willing to pay for that justice on a hill outside Jerusalem, in public, where anyone could watch. You are allowed to press him, the way Abraham did. What you are not allowed to do, having watched him die, is go on suspecting he is the kind of God who would damn a man on a technicality. That accusation died with him and got up without him. Whatever he does with the ones who never heard, he will not do it from a distance, and he will not do it unjustly, and if that is not enough to quiet the ache entirely, it was never meant to. It is meant to send you back to the road.
