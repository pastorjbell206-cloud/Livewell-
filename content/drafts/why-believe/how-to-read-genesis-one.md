The wall was never the resurrection first. It was the rocks.

Before I would let myself take the empty tomb seriously, I had already decided the book it sat in was disqualified on page one. I was an atheist, and I had run the arithmetic every skeptic runs. The earth is old. The fossils lie layered in an order. The light from distant galaxies has been traveling longer than a young world would allow. And here was a book that seemed to say the whole thing was assembled in six days a few thousand years ago, defended by people who seemed to be asking me to unlearn geology as the price of admission. I did not reject Christianity at the cross. I rejected it at the creation, and I never got far enough to reach the cross, because I thought the front door had already been shut in my face by the science.

So I want to write toward the person standing where I stood. And the first thing I would tell that person is the thing nobody told me. The church has never agreed on how to read Genesis 1, has not agreed for its entire history, and did not treat the disagreement as a threat to the faith. What I took for a locked door was a room with several chairs in it.

Before we sit in any of them, we have to name what kind of question this is, because the church has learned, at real cost, to sort its disagreements by weight.

Some of what Genesis 1 says is first-order. It is not on the table. That God created, that the world is not eternal and not an accident and not the overflow of some lesser god's mistake, that it was spoken into being out of nothing by a God who was under no compulsion to make anything at all. "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1). And that the creature made last is made different, stamped with something the stars and the whales are not given. "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27). Take those away and you do not have a different reading of Genesis. You have a different religion. The creeds bind them, and every Christian in every chair in the room confesses them together.

But the age of the earth, and the mode by which God made it. Whether the days are days or ages or a frame. Whether the first man was formed from literal dust in an afternoon or drawn up out of a long process God had been governing since before there was a sun. Those the creeds never bound. Not Nicaea, not Chalcedon, not the Apostles' Creed, which says God made heaven and earth and does not tell you how long it took. That silence is not an oversight. It is room, left on purpose, and I am going to leave it where the church left it.

Now the chairs.

Sit first with the young-earth reading, and refuse the caricature that it is only for people frightened of science. At its best it is not fear. It is a refusal to let the plain sense of a text be renegotiated every time the culture outside the church changes its mind. The young-earth Christian reads Genesis 1 and notices something the clever readings tend to hurry past: the text works hard to sound like history. It counts the days. "And there was evening and there was morning, the first day" (Genesis 1:5). It uses the ordinary Hebrew word for day, and everywhere else that word, numbered and paired with evening and morning, means a day. And the genealogies that follow do not read like poetry. They read like records, this man lived this many years and fathered this son, running in an unbroken line from Adam to Abraham to David to the man on the cross. Pull the historical Adam out of the front of that line and the young-earth reader asks a question that deserves an answer rather than a smirk: what happens to Paul. Because Paul builds the whole logic of salvation on a real first man. "Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned" (Romans 5:12). If there was no first man and no first sin, if death was already grinding through the animal world for a hundred million years before any Adam, the young-earth Christian wants to know what exactly the second Adam came to undo. This is not anti-intellectualism. It is a person taking the theology of the text with total seriousness and declining to trade it for the comfort of fitting in.

Now stand up, and sit in the second chair, and give it the same refusal to caricature. The old-earth Christian is not a compromiser keeping the science club happy. He is often a person who loves the text enough to read it as closely as the young-earth reader does, and finds in the reading itself the reasons to doubt the six-ordinary-day frame, before any geologist is consulted. Look at the text, he says. The sun is not made until the fourth day, and yet there is light, and there is evening and morning, on the first three. What is a day measured by, before there is a sun to measure it. He reads the six days and sees not a calendar but an architecture: two triads, the first three days making realms and the second three filling them, light then lights, sea and sky then fish and birds, land then land creatures and man. This is the framework view that the Reformed scholar Meredith Kline argued with care, that Genesis 1 is a deliberately ordered account whose sequence is theological rather than chronological, telling you who made it and in what majestic order of meaning, not clocking the hours. Others in this chair read the days as long ages, and point out that Scripture itself warns against pressing God's days into ours. "But do not overlook this one fact, beloved, that with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" (2 Peter 3:8). And behind them all stands Augustine, who in the fifth century, with no fossil record and no Darwin and no telescope, writing "The Literal Meaning of Genesis," concluded that the six days were not six of our days at all, and warned Christians not to talk nonsense about the natural world in the name of Scripture and so make the faith a laughingstock to the very people they hoped to reach. That warning was set down fourteen hundred years before the argument we imagine is modern.

Then there is the third chair, and this is the one I would have needed most as an atheist, and the one many Christians find hardest to sit in without flinching. Evolutionary creation, sometimes called theistic evolution, does not say God is a spectator who wound up the universe and walked away. It says the process the biologists describe, the long unfolding of life through descent and variation across the deep ages, is the process God has been superintending the whole time, that the how the scientists study and the who and why the Scripture proclaims are answers to different questions and were never rivals. Francis Collins, who led the sequencing of the human genome and then wrote "The Language of God" and founded BioLogos, is a serious Christian and a serious scientist who holds exactly this, that a person can believe every word about the image of God and also believe the body that image was pressed into came up through a governed evolutionary history. And this chair leans on a question about genre the other two cannot wave away. What was Genesis 1 written to do. Its first readers were not asking the questions a modern biology class asks. They were surrounded by neighbors who believed the world was made from the corpse of a slaughtered god, that the sun and the sea were gods to be feared, that human beings were an afterthought built to be the slaves of the divine. Into that, Genesis says one God, no rival, made it all good on purpose, and made you in his image rather than for his kitchen. That is a thunderclap against the whole ancient world, an answer to who and why, and it may simply not be pitched at the modern how at all. The scholar John Walton has argued that Genesis 1 speaks of function and order more than the material mechanics we come to it demanding. Even the old Princeton theologian B. B. Warfield, as fierce a defender of the Bible's total authority as the nineteenth century produced, thought a Christian could hold to evolution as the method God used and lose nothing he was bound to keep.

Three chairs. Before you pick one, notice what the people sitting in all of them refuse to give up.

Every one of them confesses that God is the Creator, that the world is his deliberate work and not an accident, that it was made from nothing and made good. Every one of them confesses that human beings are not one more animal but a creature made in the image of God, and that this is the ground of every human's worth. Every one of them takes the text as true. The disagreement is never whether Genesis 1 is telling the truth. The disagreement is about what kind of literature it is, and therefore what it means to read it truly, which is a different and a smaller question than the tribes make it. A poem is not a lie because it is not a lab report. A courtroom record is not a failure because it is not a hymn. Ask the wrong genre of a true text and you can make it seem to fail at a job it was never given.

I said I would not tell you where I land, and I will keep that. But I will name the temptation crouching in each chair, because I have felt all three, and the grief is the same one.

The young-earth reader can love the plainness of the text so much that he makes the age of rocks a test of fellowship, and hands the skeptic a choice the Bible never handed him, believe the earth is young or do not come in, and so becomes the very locked door I mistook for the whole house. The old-earth and the evolutionary reader can love the respect of the thinking world so much that he starts quietly trimming the text to fit whatever the moment requires, until the first man is a metaphor and the fall is a mood and nothing is left that could ever cost him a dinner-party friendship. One idolizes the letter and calls it faithfulness. The other idolizes the credibility and calls it honesty. I have wanted both idols. The instrument that cuts one has to cut the other, or it is not a scalpel. It is a flag.

So I will not settle for you which chair is the true one, because the church, with its eyes open, has not settled it either, and I have come to believe that the room with several faithful chairs in it is not a scandal to explain away but a mercy I needed. It was a mercy to the frightened believer told he had to choose between the fossil record and his Lord and never did have to. And it was a mercy to me, the atheist at the door, who assumed the whole house was condemned because one room was loud.

The heavens were doing their work the entire time I refused to look up. "The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above proclaims his handiwork" (Psalm 19:1). They did not declare a date. They declared a Maker. I spent years demanding that the book tell me how, treating its refusal to answer my question as its failure, when the whole time it was answering a larger one I was too proud to ask. Who made this. And why would he make anything at all. And why, of everything he made, did he stoop to press his own image into the dust and call it very good. Genesis 1 will not tell you the age of the earth. It will tell you whose earth it is. Start there, in whichever chair you can sit down in, and you may find you were never as far from the door as you feared.
