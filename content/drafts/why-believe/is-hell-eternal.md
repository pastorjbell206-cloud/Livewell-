The question does not usually arrive as theology. It arrives at two in the morning.

Someone is awake, and the person they are thinking about is dead, and that person died outside the faith, or seemed to, and the question in the dark is not whether there is a hell. They have made a kind of grim peace with there being a hell. The question is what is happening inside it, and whether it ever stops. Because there is a difference, and every grieving heart knows there is a difference, between a God who lets a soul finally cease to be and a God who keeps that soul alive forever precisely so that it can go on suffering without end. I have sat up with people holding that difference. I have been the person holding it. When I was an atheist, hell was not one of my objections to Christianity. It was the objection, the one I thought closed the case, and I am not going to pretend now, from inside the faith, that it is a small thing to hold. I will not make it easy. It was not easy for me.

So let me be careful about what is settled here and what is not, because the two get collapsed and the collapse does real damage.

That there is a judgment, that a person can be finally and truly separated from God, that this separation is a horror and not a metaphor for a bad mood, this the church has taught from the beginning, and it sits near the center of what Christians confess. Jesus said more about it than anyone else in the New Testament, which should trouble the sentimentalist who imagines the warnings are a later, harsher graft onto a gentle teacher. They come from his own mouth. That much is not the open question. Whether you can be lost is not what faithful Christians have divided over.

What they have divided over, and divided over while loving the same Bible and reading it on their knees, is the nature of that loss and how long it lasts. Is hell unending conscious torment? Does it end in the destruction of the one who refused God? Might it, in some hope the church has mostly refused to teach, finally empty? That is a second-order question. It is not the difference between a Christian and an apostate. It is a difference within the household, and I am going to hand you the three answers the household has actually given, each in the form its own defenders would sign, before I refuse to tell you which is mine.

Start with the traditional view, the majority report, the one most Christians who have ever lived have held: eternal conscious torment. Hear it at full strength before you flinch, because its defenders are not sadists, and the position is not cruelty looking for a proof text.

It begins with the plain shape of Jesus' own words. At the end of the parable of the sheep and the goats, he sets two destinies side by side in a single sentence: "And these will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life" (Matthew 25:46). One Greek word carries both halves. Whatever "eternal" does to the life of the righteous, it does, in the same breath, to the punishment of the lost. You cannot make the second temporary without reaching for scissors the sentence does not hand you. And John, given the last vision of the end, watches the final enemies cast down and writes that "they will be tormented day and night forever and ever" (Revelation 20:10). The traditional view stands on those texts and says it is not adding severity to Scripture; it is declining to subtract it.

Beneath the texts runs an argument about the weight of the offense. Anselm pressed it in the eleventh century and Jonathan Edwards pressed it in the eighteenth: sin is not measured only by the sinner but by the One sinned against, and a wrong done to an infinite and holy God carries a weight that a finite creature cannot discharge in any finite span. On this reading, hell is not God losing his temper for eternity over a temporary crime. It is the just and permanent shape of a creature's permanent refusal, a will that has finally and forever set itself against its own life. C. S. Lewis put the human side of it gently and terribly: the doors of hell are locked from the inside. The tradition is guarding something the softer views can lose, which is that God takes your "no" seriously enough to let it stand forever. To empty the warning is to tell the skeptic he was right, that the stakes were never as high as Jesus said. The traditional Christian will not tell him that.

Now turn, and give the second view the same refusal to caricature, because it is not liberalism and it is not wishful thinking. It is called conditional immortality, or annihilationism, and it has been held by serious, Bible-loving evangelicals who came to it not to comfort themselves but because they thought the texts drove them there.

Its case begins with a claim about what human beings are. We are not, on this reading, natively immortal; immortality is God's gift to the redeemed, not a possession every soul carries by default. So when Jesus warns, "And do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul. Rather fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell" (Matthew 10:28), the annihilationist takes the word destroy to mean what it says. Not preserve in agony. Destroy. And when Paul draws his sharpest contrast, "For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 6:23), the conditionalist asks why we read death as a coded word for endless life in torment, when its opposite, eternal life, we are happy to read straight. The Bible's fire language, they add, is consuming language: fire reduces a thing to ash and finishes. To call a fire unquenchable is to say nothing can stop it before it has done its complete work, not that it keeps a victim alive to feed it forever.

This is not a fringe held only by the unserious. John Stott, as careful and orthodox an evangelical as the last century produced, wrote in 1988 that he held the traditional view could not be treated as the only faithful option, and confessed his own mind drawn, tentatively and against the weight of tradition, toward the conditionalist reading. Edward Fudge argued the case at book length in The Fire That Consumes, verse by verse, and forced a generation of evangelicals to notice how much of Scripture's own vocabulary is the language of perishing rather than the language of endless conscious pain. The annihilationist is guarding something too: that the God who is love does not, on their reading, operate an everlasting torture chamber, and that the wages of sin, taken at its word, is finally death and not deathless dying.

I said I would not adjudicate between those two, and I will keep that. They are both inside the household. A Christian can hold either and love the same Christ, and the reader who walked in certain the first was the only faithful view should walk out knowing the second was held by men who would have died for the gospel.

Then there is a third voice, and I have to hand it to you with more caution, because the church has treated it differently, and honesty requires me to say why rather than smuggle it in as an equal.

Some in the church's history have held a wider hope, sometimes called restoration, the hope that hell is not the last word for anyone, that its fire is finally purgative and not permanent, and that in the end even the lost are drawn home so that, in Paul's phrase, God may be "all in all" (1 Corinthians 15:28). Gregory of Nyssa, one of the great theologians of the fourth century, a defender of the Trinity the whole church still leans on, seems to have held something like this. Its defenders point to the enormous reach of the texts about God's saving desire, that he "desires all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth" (1 Timothy 2:4), and they ask whether a love that finally loses most of what it made can really be called victory.

I have to be plain about where the church has stood, and I will not pad it. The tradition has, on the whole, refused to teach this as true. It has refused for reasons that are not cowardice. The warnings of the New Testament are severe and specific and come from Jesus, and a hope that quietly assures everyone the door never really closes has a way of making those warnings into theater, which the men who spoke them plainly did not intend. A love that cannot finally be refused is not obviously love; it may be the abolition of the creature God bothered to make free. And so the church has generally held this as a hope some have carried privately, a longing more than a doctrine, and not as settled ground you can stand a grieving person on. I hand it to you as that, and no more than that. Not as an answer. As a minority hope the tradition has resisted, and told you it resisted, and told you why.

Now let me put them in the same room, because that is where they actually live, and refuse to let anyone in it off the hook. To the traditionalist, the conditionalist says: you have made the God of love the eternal warden of a suffering he could end and will not, and you defend it with a metaphysics of the immortal soul you got more from Plato than from Moses. To the conditionalist, the traditionalist says: you have let your revulsion do your exegesis, and the same word that makes the life of the saved unending makes the punishment of the lost unending, and you cannot keep the one and dissolve the other by feeling. To both, the wider hope says: you have made peace too fast with most of humanity being lost. And to the wider hope, all of Scripture's severe and sober warnings say: you have made peace too fast with none of them being.

None of them gets to walk out clean. That is the part I will not spare you, and the part the confident preacher on any side always leaves out.

So hear what I am refusing. I am refusing the preacher who relishes the fire, who describes the torment with a heat in his voice that should frighten him more than it frightens his congregation, because a man who enjoys this doctrine has not understood it. And I am refusing, with the same hand, the sentimentalist who has quietly emptied the warnings until nothing Jesus said about this costs anything, who has decided in advance that a loving God could not mean it, and has therefore stopped listening to the One who said it most. Both of them have made hell easy. It is not easy. It was the hardest thing I ever had to walk back toward, and I did not walk back toward it because someone made it comfortable. I walked back because I could not make the God who went to a cross fit the caricature I had built.

That is the only place I know to leave you, and it is not a resolution. It is a Person. Whatever hell finally is, whether it is endless or whether it ends, it belongs to the God who did not stay at a safe distance from the loss but climbed down into it, who cried out that he was forsaken so that the forsaking might have somewhere else to go. You are allowed to find this doctrine terrible. I still do. What you are not allowed to do, having watched him die rather than lose you, is to believe he is careless with a single soul. He is not careless with yours. He was not careless with the one you are lying awake over. Whatever he does in the dark you cannot see into, he does it as the one who bled to keep people out of it, and if that does not settle the fear entirely, it was never meant to. It is meant to send you back to him with the fear in your hands, which is the only place it has ever been safe to carry it.
