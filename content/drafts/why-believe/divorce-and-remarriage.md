The paper comes in an envelope, or it comes as a sentence at the kitchen table, or it comes as the slow understanding that the person across the bed left the marriage years before either of you said the word out loud. However it arrives, it does not feel like a doctrine. It feels like a death.

You may be reading this with the ring still on and a house gone quiet. You may be reading it a decade later, with a second spouse in the next room and a shame that walks into church a step ahead of you. You may be the one who was left, or the one who did the leaving, or the one who can no longer tell which of those is true. I am not going to hand you a verdict. The church has carried this question for two thousand years without once setting it down, and the most honest thing I can do is carry it into the room beside you and refuse to pretend it is light.

Start with what is not in dispute, because nearly everything true here begins there. Marriage is good. Not good the way a policy is good, something the culture assembled and can therefore amend. It is older than the culture. Jesus, asked about divorce, does not begin with Moses. He reaches past Moses to the garden, to the way it was from the beginning, and he says of husband and wife, "So they are no longer two but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (Matthew 19:6). The New Testament will later make that one flesh a picture of Christ and his church, which is why the vow is not a convenience and the breaking of it is never small. That much the whole church confesses, and it sits near the center of Christian ethics, not out at the edge where we are free to trade things away. Hold onto it. Everything I am about to describe is an argument among people who all hold onto it.

Now the actual dividing line, and let me name its weight before I walk into it. Whether there are grounds on which a believer may rightly divorce, and whether a divorced believer may rightly remarry, is a question on which faithful churches have genuinely differed. This is not the deity of Christ. It is not the resurrection. It is a second-order question, the kind that has divided sincere Christians from one another without dividing any of them from Christ, and I am going to treat it as exactly that. Serious enough to labor over. Open enough that a brother on the other side is still a brother.

Take the strict view first, and take it at full strength, because the world has spent fifty years making it easy to sneer at, and it does not deserve the sneer. The strict reading says that what God joins, no one on earth has standing to pull apart, and that includes the two people standing inside it. Jesus did not loosen Moses. He tightened him. Moses permitted the certificate of divorce, and Jesus calls that permission a concession to hard hearts, not the will of the God who made them one. On this reading the bond is not a feeling that can die or a contract that can be breached. It is an objective thing God did, and a human court dissolving it on paper does not dissolve it in heaven. This is not a fringe severity. In various forms it has been the conviction of much of the church across the centuries, and it remains the formal teaching of the Roman Catholic Church, which holds a consummated Christian marriage indissoluble until death and will not call a second union, contracted while the first spouse lives, a marriage at all. The strict Christian looks at a culture that files for divorce more easily than it breaks a lease, at vows treated as sentiment that expires the moment it costs something, and says the thing no one wants to hear. Someone has to hold the line the world has erased. Someone has to testify with their own life that the promise meant what it said, and if the church will not, no one will. They are not being cruel when they say it. They are guarding the one human institution that still tells a child what a covenant is, and they are willing to pay for it in their own comfort to keep it true.

Now turn, and give the other side the same refusal to caricature, because it is not the party of loose vows. It is the party that took the exceptions Scripture actually wrote down and would not erase them to make the rule cleaner. Because Jesus himself, in the same breath where he raises the standard, names an exception. "And I say to you: whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another, commits adultery" (Matthew 19:9). Read the exception clause honestly and it will not disappear. He could have said whoever divorces and remarries commits adultery, and stopped. He did not. He carved out a case, and a plain reading finds in it one truth the strict view has to work hard to hold down: a spouse's betrayal of the one flesh is a wound that can end the marriage in fact before any court ends it on paper. Then Paul, facing a situation Jesus had not addressed, adds a second. When an unbelieving spouse walks out on a believer, "if the unbelieving partner separates, let it be so. In such cases the brother or sister is not enslaved. God has called you to peace" (1 Corinthians 7:15). Not enslaved. Paul will not chain a deserted believer to a marriage the other person has already abandoned.

And there is the word from Malachi that both sides have to hold with care. Malachi 2:16 in the ESV reads, "For the man who does not love his wife but divorces her, says the LORD, the God of Israel, covers his garment with violence, says the LORD of hosts. So guard yourselves in your spirit, and do not be faithless." Older translations render the opening as God saying "I hate divorce," and the anger survives in either rendering. It falls on the treachery, on the violence done under the roof, on the covenant a man breaks against the wife he swore to cover. Read that way, the verse is not a wall built against the abandoned. It is a verse standing on the side of the betrayed. And many faithful Christians reason from exactly there: that a spouse who batters, or who deserts, has already torn the covenant with their own hands, and that the church works its own kind of violence when it orders a battered woman back under that roof and calls her staying obedience. If you have ever watched that happen, you know it does not look like holiness. It looks like a man protecting a doctrine with someone else's body.

So here is the honest tension, and I am not going to relieve it for you. The same Jesus who says let not man separate says except for sexual immorality. Both sentences are his. The strict Christian is guarding something real, the permanence the world has cheapened until it means nothing. The Christian who permits divorce in some cases is guarding something just as real, the abandoned and the abused whom a flattened rule would crush under the weight of the very covenant the other person already broke. Refuse both of the easy men. Refuse the rigorist who binds a burden onto the wounded and steps back to watch them carry it. Refuse the sentimentalist who empties the vow of everything that made it cost, until "till death" quietly comes to mean "till it gets hard." Both of them have found a way to stop feeling the weight. The rigorist stopped feeling the person. The sentimentalist stopped feeling the promise. The gospel will not let you set either one down.

I have been the rigorist. I will tell you that plainly, because I am inside this indictment and not standing over it. I have reached for the rule in a counseling room because the rule was clean and the person in front of me was not, because a principle asks nothing of me and a bleeding human asks everything I have. I was raised without a father, in the wreckage a broken home leaves in a boy who never got a vote, and I have five sons of my own now, and none of that has handed me the verdict. It has only made me unable to hold this question at a safe distance. When I have gotten it wrong, I have gotten it wrong in the direction of protecting my theology from somebody's pain. That is a sin the strict and the loose can both commit. I have committed it.

Which is why I have to tell you the one thing this page cannot do. It cannot know you. A website does not know whether you were the betrayer or the betrayed, whether the marriage died of one catastrophic blow or ten thousand small withdrawals nobody could see, whether staying is faithfulness or is slowly ending you. A real decision about a real marriage does not belong to an essay. It belongs in a room, with a pastor who knows your name and will still know it a year from now, and often with a wise counselor beside him. If you are in danger, it belongs first with people who can actually keep you safe, and reaching for them is not a failure of faith. It is faith with its eyes open. Do not carry this alone because you are ashamed. Shame is the one counselor in the room who is always lying.

And hear this, whichever line you have already crossed. The blood of Christ was not spilled for the people whose marriages held. Grace is not the wage paid out on a record you kept clean. It is the thing that walks toward you precisely because you could not keep it. If your marriage has ended, you are not standing outside the reach of God, filed under some category his mercy has learned to skip. The woman Jesus met at the well had five husbands behind her and a sixth man who was not her husband, and he did not open by counting them. He asked her for a drink. Then he told her who he was, and she left her water jar and went back to the town that knew her whole story, and many of them believed because of what she said. He began where the grace was, not where the failure was.

So I leave you where the question actually lives, which is not in a rulebook but in a covenant that was always meant to image a faithfulness stronger than ours. Marriage is good, and its breaking is a grief God himself calls violence, and there are cases where faithful Christians have stood over the same wreckage and disagreed, in the fear of God, about what love requires next. I will not settle it for you from here. Settling it from here would be its own kind of violence, the cheap kind, done to a person I cannot see. But I will tell you the one thing I am sure of. Whatever your marriage became, you have not out-sinned the cross. It was built taller than your worst day on purpose. Go find the people who can help you carry this, and do not believe the voice that tells you the door is shut. It is not the door that is closed. It is only the night, and the night has never once been the end of the story.
