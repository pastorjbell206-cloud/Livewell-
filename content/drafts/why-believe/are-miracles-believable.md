I used to think I had a proof. Not a hunch, not a mood, a proof, the kind that closes a subject and lets a serious person walk away from it for good. It concerned miracles, and I had borrowed it from a Scottish philosopher who was dead two centuries before I found him, and the first time I read it I felt the specific pleasure of a heavy door swinging shut. The case was over. I was nineteen, and it took me years to notice that the thing I was calling a proof was a definition wearing a proof's clothes.

Let me put the philosopher on the table at his full strength, because a version of him you can knock over is not worth your time, and it is not worth mine. His name was David Hume, and his argument still does more quiet work in the modern mind than any other single page written against the faith. Most people who have never heard his name are nonetheless standing inside his conclusion.

## The argument, made as strong as it is

Hume set it out in the tenth section of *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, published in 1748, a section he simply titled "Of Miracles." The reasoning is spare and it is powerful. A miracle, he wrote, is "a violation of the laws of nature." Those laws are not casual generalizations we might revise on a slow afternoon. They rest on the entire accumulated, unbroken experience of every human being who has ever lived. Fire burns. The dead stay dead. A body does not walk out of a sealed tomb three days after execution. We do not hold these as guesses; we hold them because experience, as far back as experience runs, has never once contradicted them. That is what makes them laws.

So set the two things against each other. On one side, the total weight of human experience, uniform and unbroken, testifying that the dead remain dead. On the other, the testimony of a few witnesses that on one occasion a dead man did not. Which is more likely to be mistaken. Witnesses lie. Witnesses are fooled. Witnesses embellish, misremember, and want a thing to be true so badly that they come to see it. There is nothing rarer in the world than a broken law of nature, and nothing more common than a false report. Weigh them honestly and the scale never even trembles.

Hume gave the rule its final form in a single sentence, and I quote it exactly, because it is the hinge of everything he argued: "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact which it endeavours to establish." Read it twice. Before you accept that a man rose, you must be more astonished that his witnesses were wrong than you would be by the resurrection itself. And you almost never will be, because human error is the most ordinary thing there is. The proof against a miracle, Hume concluded, is as complete as any proof drawn from experience can possibly be.

I want to concede how much of this is simply true, and I will not do it grudgingly. Healthy skepticism is not the enemy of faith; it is a form of respect for reality, and the church has too often treated it as an insult. Most miracle claims are false. Some are frauds, most are the honest inflation of an ordinary event by people who needed it to be more. I have watched Christians pass around a viral healing video with the credulity of children, believing on Tuesday what they would have laughed at on Monday if a stranger had claimed it, and asking the world to respect a God whose evidence they themselves handle so cheaply. Hume's scorn for that habit is earned. If the price of belief is that I must swallow every wonder that flatters my side, then Hume has already won, and he should. A faith that cannot tell a sign from a rumor has no business lecturing anyone about evidence.

So I am not here to rescue every claim. I am here to ask whether the argument I once thought settled the whole question actually settles it. And the longer I looked, the more I saw the flaw is not at the edges. It is at the center.

## Where the proof quietly cheats

Notice what has to be true for Hume's scale to work. The uniform experience of mankind must count against every miracle. But how do we know experience is uniform against them. Only by assuming in advance that every reported miracle, including the one presently under discussion, is false. If even one has occurred, experience is not uniform, and the whole weight on that side of the scale drops away. Hume can only guarantee the uniformity he needs by counting the resurrection as a falsehood before he has examined the testimony for it. He puts the verdict into the premise and then acts surprised to find it in the conclusion.

This is not a believer's special pleading. The sharpest modern statement of it comes from John Earman, a philosopher of science who wrote not as an apologist but as a man with no religious stake to protect, in a book he bluntly titled *Hume's Abject Failure: The Argument Against Miracles*, published by Oxford in 2000. Earman's charge, argued in the cold language of probability rather than devotion, is that Hume's reasoning is circular and, worse, that it would forbid belief in any genuinely novel event, miracle or not. If we only ever believed what uniform past experience already permitted, we could never have accepted a report of a black swan, or a meteorite, or any of the discoveries that enlarged the world precisely by breaking the pattern. An argument that, applied evenly, would have kept us from every real anomaly is not a rule of reason. It is a rule for staying where you are.

Here is the move underneath the move. Hume defined a miracle as a violation of the laws of nature, and once you accept that definition the rest is arithmetic. But the definition smuggles in the conclusion. It assumes the laws are the whole story, a closed system with nothing outside it, so that any exception can only be a break in the machine. That is not something Hume proved. It is something he presumed, and it is the exact point in question. I did not have an argument against miracles. I had a definition of the universe as a locked room, and every deduction I was so proud of was just that definition talking to itself.

## The question that was actually on the table

Which forces a distinction I never made when I was certain. The real question is not whether miracles are common. They are not, and if they were they would not be miracles; the word means the rare exception, the thing that does not happen on schedule. Nobody in this argument believes the dead ordinarily rise. Christianity is not the claim that tombs are unreliable. It is the claim that on one particular morning, one particular tomb was, and that this was the exception announcing itself as an exception.

So the question is not frequency. It is authorship. If there is no God, Hume is not merely right, he is barely worth stating, because a closed system of matter has no one who could reach into it and nothing the reaching would mean. But if there is a God, if the regularities we call the laws of nature are not brute facts but the settled habits of a mind that holds them in being, then a miracle is a different kind of thing entirely. It is not a crack in the machine. It is the author of the machine writing, for once, in the margin. The God who set the constancy of things is not violating his own order when he acts within it any more than a novelist violates the grammar of her book by having a character do something new in the final chapter. The regularity is his, and so is the exception, and the exception is not a malfunction. It is a signature.

Put it plainly. A miracle is not a law of nature being broken. It is a person being present who is normally content to be hidden.

This is the whole hinge, and it is why the argument I loved was never a knockdown. If God's existence is so much as possible, then miracles cannot be ruled out in advance, before any evidence is heard, by the shape of a definition. They can only be weighed one at a time, on their own testimony, the way you would weigh any surprising report from a witness you have reason to take seriously. Hume wanted to settle the matter a priori, from the armchair, without troubling to look. And the refusal to look, dressed up as rigor, is the least scientific instinct there is.

Which is exactly where the resurrection stands, and where it has always asked to stand. Not as a general theory that the dead sometimes rise, but as a specific claim about a specific man, made by witnesses who gained nothing and lost nearly everything, who did not read as people telling a story that had gone to their heads. Paul staked the entire thing on it being a fact and not a feeling: "And if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14, ESV). He did not offer it as an inspiring symbol you could keep after the history collapsed. He offered it as an event, and he invited the check. That is not the posture of a man running from Hume's scale. It is a man setting his one claim on it and telling you to weigh.

## What I actually had

I said this would not be triumphant, and it will not be, because the person I most need to be honest about is the one I was. I did not lose the argument to a better argument. I lost it to the discovery that I had never had one. What I had was the feeling of finality, and the feeling came from the definition, and the definition was the very thing I was supposed to be proving. I had ruled out the exception by describing a universe in which no exception could occur, and then I had congratulated myself on the discovery that no exception occurs. It is the oldest trick in reasoning, and I had run it on myself, and I had called the result maturity.

I am not asking you to believe a wonder because it moves you. Do not do that; it is beneath you, and it is beneath the God in question. I am asking one thing, the thing I would not do for years. Stop ruling the possibility out before the evidence arrives. Notice whether your certainty that miracles cannot happen is a conclusion you reasoned to or a definition you started with. Because if it is the second, you have not weighed the resurrection at all. You have only refused, in advance, to put it on the scale.

"With man this is impossible, but with God all things are possible" (Matthew 19:26, ESV). The sentence turns entirely on the second clause. Rule out the God, and the impossible stays impossible, and Hume can go home undefeated. Leave the door so much as ajar, and the whole question changes, because then the rare thing is not a broken law. It is the lawgiver, present for a morning, and the only honest way to answer him is to look.

I thought I had a proof. I had a definition, and I had built a world around it small enough that nothing could ever surprise me in it. The surprise, when it came, did not break the world. It told me who had been holding it up the whole time.
