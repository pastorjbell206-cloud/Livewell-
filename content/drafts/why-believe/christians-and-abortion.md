The word that starts most of these arguments is not a word. It is a wall.

Two people stand on either side of it, each holding a sign the other cannot read, and the volume goes up in exact proportion to how little either of them intends to look at the person across from them. I have stood on both sides of that wall in my life, and I want to tell you the truth about what I was doing there. I was not thinking. I was belonging. The certainty felt like conviction, but most of it was the warm feeling of being on a team that had already agreed the other team was either a murderer or a bully. That feeling is available on both sides. It costs nothing. It has never once helped a woman make it through a Tuesday.

So before I say anything else, I want to name who is actually in the room when this subject comes up, because she is almost never the person the argument is about. She is a woman who has already made this decision, or is making it now, or watched someone she loves make it and has never said a word about the weight she still carries. She is reading this. If everything I write is aimed past her at a political opponent, I have done the exact thing I am about to accuse both sides of doing. So I am writing to her too, the whole way, and I am telling her at the top: there is no sentence coming that is designed to make her smaller.

Now let me do the hard work, which is to give each side the version its serious people would actually sign, before I weigh a single thing.

## The strongest case for abortion rights

Start here, and refuse the cartoon of it, because the cartoon is where Christians usually begin and it is a lie. The serious case for abortion rights is not a shrug about human life. It is not people who think a pregnancy is nothing. Its best defenders are often the ones most awake to how much a woman's body, her health, her whole future, is on the line in a way that no man's body ever is.

The argument runs like this. A pregnancy does not happen to a woman the way weather happens to a field. It happens inside her, at real cost to her blood and bone and years, and no one else can carry it for her or bear the risk in her place. To say the state may command her to carry a pregnancy she did not choose, or cannot survive, or cannot endure, is to say her body is finally not her own, and that is a thing we say to no one else about any other organ or any other risk. Then come the cases that are not abstractions. The girl who was raped and is now told the pregnancy is a gift. The woman whose own life is failing as the pregnancy advances, whose doctors are weighing whether she goes home to her other children. The couple who wanted this child desperately and learned the pregnancy will never become a living baby, and are now asked to carry a certain death to term for a principle. The serious defender says: whatever you think in general, look at her, and tell me the law knows her situation better than she does.

And the last move, the one that ought to land hardest on a Christian, is this. If you actually want fewer abortions, the evidence points less at the courtroom than at the conditions. A woman with paid leave, with a doctor she can afford, with a partner who stays, with a church that will not vanish when the belly shows, chooses differently than a woman staring down all of it alone. So the serious defender says the loudest voices against abortion are often the quietest about the things that reduce it, and that a movement more interested in banning than in helping has told on itself. Grant that. Do not flinch from it. Some of it is aimed at me.

## The strongest case that the unborn is not nothing

Now turn, and give this side the same refusal to caricature, because it too has a cartoon, and the cartoon is a man who stops caring the moment the baby is born.

The case begins not in the Bible but in the biology, because it does not require you to share my faith to feel it. From conception forward there is no later line at which something switches on. There is no morning the thing in the womb becomes a different kind of thing. It is, from the start, a distinct and continuous human life, the same organism that will one day argue about abortion, only younger and more dependent than it will ever be again. Every line we draw after that first moment, a heartbeat, a trimester, a breath, is a line we chose, and the person on the far side of it was already there. The defender asks a question that does not go away by being called a slogan: if it is not a human life, what is it, and if it is, what exactly permits ending it.

Then the church's witness, which is older and stranger than most people fighting about it today realize. This is not a position American Christians invented in 1973. In the first century, in a document called the Didache, the earliest Christians wrote down their ethic for a pagan world that exposed unwanted infants on hillsides as a matter of course, and they forbade abortion by name. They did it as a persecuted minority with no political power, no court to appeal to, nothing to gain. They did it because they believed the God who made every person had made these ones too, the unseen and the unwanted. That conviction runs straight back into the Scripture they read. "For you formed my inward parts; you knitted me together in my mother's womb" (Psalm 139:13). The claim is not that a fetus is precious because it is useful or wanted or viable. The claim is that God is already at work in there, on a person he already knows, before the mother knows and before the world assigns a value.

And the last move, the one that ought to land hardest on the other side. The measure of any society is what it does with the members who cannot argue for themselves, who have no vote and no voice and no way to make the powerful look at them. Proverbs puts the duty plainly. "Rescue those who are being taken away to death; hold back those who are stumbling to the slaughter" (Proverbs 24:11). The defender says the unborn are exactly the kind of voiceless the whole of Scripture keeps commanding us to see, and that a culture which has learned to look away from them has practiced a skill it will use again on someone else. Grant that too.

## Naming the order, because it is the whole thing

Here is the part both teams skip, and skipping it is how the shouting stays possible.

These two claims are not the same size, and pretending they are is the tribal shortcut. The conviction that a human being bears the image of God, and that the life in the womb is not nothing, is deep and old and near the center of Christian ethics. It is not a policy preference the church picked up recently. It goes back to the hillsides where the first believers gathered up the infants Rome had thrown away. On that, the church's voice across two thousand years is not divided, and I will not pretend it is to make peace in a paragraph. That is high ground, and the church has stood on it a long time.

But the hardest cases, and the question of what a law should therefore be, and how a state should weigh a mother's life and a doctor's judgment and a rape and a dying pregnancy, are exactly where sincere people, including sincere Christians who share the conviction above, disagree sharply and in good faith. A woman can hold that the unborn is a human life and still weep at a bill that offers her nothing but a threat. A man can believe every life is God's image and still not know what the statute should say when two lives are failing at once. To treat the deep conviction and the contested policy as one indivisible package, so that touching the second means betraying the first, is the move that has made this subject unspeakable in most churches. It is not honesty. It is a way of not thinking, dressed as courage.

So I am not going to hand you my ballot. Not because I have no convictions, and not to hide, but because the thing this platform owes you is the shape of the question stated truly, and the church's ancient witness named as the church's, and then the trust to carry it into a voting booth I do not get to enter with you.

## The two cheap versions, refused together, with the same grief

Now let me criticize both sides with one instrument, because the moment I only ever swing it in one direction, I have stopped doing theology and started doing party work.

There is a pro-life that loses interest at birth. It will fight for the child in the womb and then vanish when that child is a hungry six-year-old, or a terrified nineteen-year-old holding a test in a gas station bathroom. It has sometimes meant pro-birth and no more, a passion for the unborn that cools the instant a life would actually cost the movement something. I have sat in rooms where that was true and said nothing, because the room was mine. That is not a full doctrine of life. It is the easy half.

And there is a pro-choice that will not look at the ultrasound. That keeps the language abstract precisely because the picture is not, that says clump of cells to avoid the harder word, that treats any grief the woman feels as false consciousness to be corrected rather than a truth she is telling about what happened. It has sometimes meant a refusal to look at what is ended, because looking would cost the argument its comfort. That is not honesty either. It is the other easy half.

Same instrument. Same grief. If my guardrail only ever fires at the people I already disagree with, it was never a guardrail. It was a flag.

## The woman in the room

I said I would keep her in view the whole way, so let me end with her and not with the argument.

If you have had an abortion, hear this before anything else. You are not a talking point, not to God and not on this page. Whatever you carry from that day, whether it is grief you have never been allowed to say out loud, or relief tangled up with shame you did not expect, or a numbness you have learned to live behind, none of it puts you outside the reach of the only voice that finally matters. The gospel is not that you were never in the wrong. It is that Christ came for people who were, all of us, me first, and that there is no sin his cross was too small to cover. Grace is not the reward for having a clean history. It is the thing that walks into the history you actually have and refuses to leave. If the church ever taught you that this one was the exception, the church was wrong, and I am sorry for the part of it that was mine.

And if you are standing in that decision right now, in the dark, feeling like there is no one to tell and nowhere soft to land, please do not carry it alone. There are people who will sit with you and not perform at you, a pastor, a doctor you trust, a pregnancy center or a counselor who will tell you the truth without shaming you for needing it. You are worth more than the worst hour of your life, and the feeling that you are not is a liar, however loud it gets tonight.

So let me tell you where I land, since I said I would not hand you a ballot and I meant it, but a conviction is not a ballot. I land here. The unborn child is a neighbor. Knit in the womb, bearing the image of the God who knits, small and silent and unable to speak a word in its own defense, which is the exact profile of every person Scripture commands us to protect. I am pro-life, and I cannot unsee the one whose life is on the table.

But pro-life is the whole sentence or it is a slogan, and I hold it as the whole sentence. The same conviction that will not look away from the child in the womb cannot lose interest at the delivery room. It has to follow the child into a world of hunger and the children no one comes back for, and it has to carry the mother in the crisis that made the choice feel like no choice at all, before her decision and long after it. A movement that guards the womb and abandons the mother has not earned the name it wears, and I have sat in rooms where my own side did exactly that and called it faithfulness. The objection I have not answered is her, the woman at a wall with no good door, whom the people loudest about life have too often shamed instead of carried. I am pro-life, womb to tomb, and the day the church loves the mother as fiercely as it defends the child is the day the word will finally mean what it says.

I do not get to hand you a formula that makes this weightless, and I would not trust myself if I tried, because the flourish a man uses to make this weightless is almost always a flourish over someone's grave. What I can tell you is that the God who knits a person in the womb is the same God who knits the broken back together, and that he has never once been more interested in winning an argument than in finding the person the argument forgot. That is where I have to leave it. Not resolved. Held. The way you hold something you were never meant to set down cheaply.
