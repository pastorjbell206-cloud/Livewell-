There is a page in the Bible I used to keep a finger on when I argued with Christians. I did not need the whole book. I needed one command, read aloud, slowly, in a room where a believer had just told me God is love. Watch their face while you read it. That is where the argument was won, and I won it for years.

So I will not pretend to you now that I have never stood where you are standing. I have. Of everything the church asked me to swallow, this was the piece I found not merely hard but obscene, and I still think a person who reads these texts and feels sick is reading them correctly. If you do not feel it, you are not paying attention. I am going to make you feel it before I say one word in any other direction, because a defense that skips the horror has not earned the right to speak.

## The command, read the way I read it

Here is what the book actually says. Israel is on the edge of a land already occupied, and the instruction is not to negotiate, not to conquer and subjugate, but to erase. "But in the cities of these peoples that the LORD your God is giving you for an inheritance, you shall save alive nothing that breathes" (Deuteronomy 20:16). The list of peoples follows, six of them by name, and the reason given is that they will otherwise teach Israel their practices. When the campaign is narrated in Joshua, the language holds. At Jericho: "Then they devoted all in the city to destruction, both men and women, young and old, oxen, sheep, and donkeys, with the edge of the sword" (Joshua 6:21). Across the south: "He left none remaining, but devoted to destruction all that breathed, just as the LORD God of Israel commanded" (Joshua 10:40).

And then the one I kept my finger on, because it removes the last place to hide. Centuries later God tells Saul to finish an old account with the Amalekites: "Do not spare them, but kill both man and woman, child and infant, ox and sheep, camel and donkey" (1 Samuel 15:3). Child and infant. The text does not let you imagine only soldiers. It names the nursing baby and then commands the sword, and it puts the command in the mouth of God.

I want to say plainly what those sentences describe if you read them the way a decent person reads a newspaper. They describe the extermination of a people, the killing of children as policy, the thing our own century learned to call ethnic cleansing and swore it would never excuse again. Richard Dawkins, in *The God Delusion* (2006), ran through the divine résumé and landed on the phrase "bloodthirsty ethnic cleanser," and the reason that line drew blood is that it was quoting. He did not have to distort anything. He read the verses out loud. That was my whole method too.

So we are not going to move past this quickly. If the God who commands the death of infants is real, then either that God is a monster, or something is wrong with the way the words are reaching us, and a believer who reaches for the second option too fast is doing exactly what I always accused them of doing. I accused them of loving the conclusion more than the truth. Sometimes I was right.

## What I did not know I was assuming

For years I read these texts as a plain video of events, the way I read a wire report, and it did not occur to me that I was making a decision about genre when I did it. I was. And the scholars who spend their lives inside the ancient world kept pointing at something I had dismissed without examining.

The military texts of the ancient Near East do not talk the way our reports talk. When a Pharaoh or an Assyrian king recorded a campaign, total annihilation was the conventional idiom of victory, the expected rhetoric, not a body count. K. Lawson Younger's study *Ancient Conquest Accounts* (1990) laid the biblical conquest narratives beside the war annals of Egypt and Assyria and found the same stock formulas, the same hyperbolic totality: the enemy wiped out, none left alive, the land emptied, in inscriptions we know describe partial and ongoing wars. Kenneth Kitchen, an Egyptologist with no interest in softening Scripture, made the same case in *On the Reliability of the Old Testament* (2003): "all breathing things destroyed" was how that world announced a decisive win, and reading it as a literal census is a modern mistake imposed on an ancient convention.

Here is what turned it from special pleading into something I had to weigh. The Bible itself refuses to let the totality stand. The same book that says none were left alive turns the page and shows the survivors everywhere. Judah cannot dislodge the people of the plain. Benjamin fails to drive out the Jebusites, "so the Jebusites have lived with the people of Benjamin in Jerusalem to this day" (Judges 1:21). By the third chapter the situation is total in the other direction: "So the people of Israel lived among the Canaanites, the Hittites, the Amorites, the Perizzites, the Hivites, and the Jebusites" (Judges 3:5). The peoples reported as annihilated in Joshua are Israel's neighbors, in-laws, and snares two chapters later. Either the editor was too careless to notice a contradiction across adjoining scrolls, or the "utterly destroyed" language was never meant to be read as a literal count in the first place. The second is the reading the text forces on its own. I had been holding the author to a genre he was not writing in, and calling my error his crime.

That does not dissolve the difficulty. Hyperbole about a battle is still a battle, and dead noncombatants are still dead. But it moves the question off the ground I had staked it on. The horror is not a divine program of demographic extinction. It is a war, described in the brutal maximal idiom of its age, and the difference matters even if it does not comfort.

## What the war was against

The second thing I refused to look at was what the text says it was for, because I assumed I already knew: a tribal god clearing land for his favorites. But the Bible goes out of its way to demolish that reading before I could use it.

It is not framed as racial. It is framed as judgment on entrenched evil, and the evil is named. The practices in view include burning children alive as worship: "they even burn their sons and their daughters in the fire to their gods" (Deuteronomy 12:31). The land itself is said to be under sentence for it, and the warning comes with a threat aimed straight back at Israel: "the land vomited out its inhabitants" (Leviticus 18:25), and it will vomit you out too if you take up the same things. That last clause is the tell. The judgment is not attached to blood. It is attached to conduct, and the covenant people are told they stand under the identical standard and will meet the identical end, which is precisely what the prophets later say happens to them.

Then the line that took my favorite argument away from me. God tells Israel, in advance, that this is not a reward for their virtue: "Not because of your righteousness or the uprightness of your heart are you going in to possess their land, but because of the wickedness of these nations the LORD your God is driving them out from before you" (Deuteronomy 9:5). And the whole thing is set inside a clock that had been running for centuries. Generations earlier the promise to Abraham had come with a strange delay built in, a delay that was mercy to the very people later dispossessed: "for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (Genesis 15:16). The waiting was deliberate. Judgment held off across four generations. That is not the profile of a genocide, which wants its victims gone as fast as possible. It is the profile of a patience most readers never notice is there.

I am aware this can be made to sound like a tidy justification, and I am wary of it, because "they deserved it" is the oldest sentence in the mouth of every army that ever burned a village. So let me hold the line honestly. Naming the wickedness of Canaan explains why the text calls this judgment rather than conquest. It does not by itself resolve the death of a specific child who chose none of it. The Scripture gives me a category, judgment, not an anesthetic. I will not pretend the category makes the picture pretty.

## The thing that cannot be repeated

The move I dread most is the one where a reader closes this essay and feels licensed. If God did it there, the logic runs, then holy violence is on the table, and history is a graveyard of men who read these pages and reached for a sword. So I want to be as clear as language allows. The conquest is sealed shut. It is not a template, and everything in the shape of the text works to keep it from becoming one.

It is tied to one people, one land, one window in redemptive history, under direct command, and it is never generalized into a standing policy toward outsiders. Israel is forbidden to treat the nations this way as a rule; the herem is the narrow exception bound to that specific inheritance, not a doctrine of how to deal with unbelievers. No prophet extends it. No later king is told to resume it against the world. It closes.

And then the person Christians say is God in the flesh walks directly into the question and takes the sword off the table with his hands. When a disciple draws a blade to defend him, the answer is flat: "Put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword" (Matthew 26:52). When two of his men, standing in the old tradition, ask to call down fire on a village that rejected him, "he turned and rebuked them" (Luke 9:55). His kingdom, he says, does not fight, because it is not that kind of kingdom (John 18:36). Whatever the conquest was, the founder of the faith that carries these texts forbids his people to imitate it. Any Christian who reads Joshua as a warrant for violence has not misread one verse. He has walked past the cross to get to it. That reading is not a debatable interpretation to be weighed with the others. It is to be refused outright, and refused first, before we say anything else.

## What is left when the arguments run out

I promised at the start I would not hand you a comfort I could not accept myself, so I will end where honesty makes me end. Put all of it together, the genre we misread, the judgment on real evil, the centuries of waiting, the seal that shuts the conquest forever, and a weight still remains. The hyperbole reading eases the scale of it and does not empty the battlefield. The language of judgment gives the war a moral shape and does not raise the particular child who died inside that shape. I can push the horror back. I cannot make it vanish, and I distrust anyone who tells you they can, because the flourish they use to do it is usually a flourish over a grave.

What I can tell you is what changed for me, and it was not that the texts stopped being hard. It is that I stopped being able to use them as the clean proof I once thought they were. A plain reading turned out to be a genre mistake I had never examined. A tribal-favoritism reading turned out to be one the text itself dismantles, aiming the same judgment back at Israel's own head. A warrant-for-violence reading turned out to be one that Jesus takes out of my hand. Three of my certainties were softer than I had sworn they were, and a man who finds three of his certainties soft is obligated to wonder about the fourth.

I do not have the child. That grief is real, and I will not decorate it. But I no longer hold the God of these pages as the plain monster I once read out loud to win a room. I hold him now as the hardest thing in a book I have come to trust for other reasons, a difficulty I carry rather than a case I have closed. If you leave still troubled, we are troubled together, and that is the honest place to be. Anyone who is not troubled here has stopped reading the words.

Do not believe because the difficulty went away. It did not. Believe, if you ever do, in spite of it, the way I finally did, and keep your finger on the page.
