The question does not usually arrive as a question. It arrives as a person who has already decided you are not safe.

I have watched it happen more than once, and it tends to go the same way. A man sits in the church for the better part of three years. Second row, most Sundays, singing like he means it, and he does not say he is gay until he is most of the way out the door, and by then he is telling you why he is leaving, not asking whether he can stay. He did the math a long time before anyone noticed him. He watched how the room talked about people like him when it thought no one like him was in it, the jokes that were not quite jokes, the certainty that cost the certain nothing. He learned what we would do with the truth before he ever risked it on us. Three years of his voice and none of his life, and then he was gone, and we found out what we had been pastoring only after we had failed to.

I have to say that before I say anything else, because if I do not, the rest of this is just a man arranging arguments over a wound he refuses to look at. The church has been cruel here. Not in the abstract. In rooms I have stood in, from pulpits I have shared, in a tone I have used myself before I knew who was listening. We singled out one sin, made it the sin, the one you could be known by and defined by and kept at the door for, while the gossip and the greed and the divorces in the third pew got a nod and a casserole. If you are gay and you grew up in the church, you already know all of this in your body, and you do not need me to perform sorrow about it. You need me to not lie. So I will not. The cruelty was real, it was ours, and a great deal of what has been said in defense of the historic teaching has been the ugliest thing in the room while claiming to be the holiest.

If you cannot feel the weight of that, you are not ready for the next sentence.

Because here is the harder thing. Naming the cruelty does not by itself settle what the church should teach, and pretending it does is its own kind of dodge. There is a live disagreement here, and it runs down the middle of serious, Scripture-loving Christians, and whole traditions have come down on opposite sides of it without either side being stupid or evil. Some hold it as a boundary of the faith. Others hold it as a difference between brothers who will be in the kingdom together. I will tell you where I land, and I will not hide it. But not yet, and not before I have handed you both cases at their strongest, because a verdict you are handed before you have felt the full weight of the other side is not a conviction. It is a reflex. So let me do the harder thing first. Let me hand you both cases at their strongest, in the form their best defenders would actually sign, and refuse to let you off the weight of it.

Start with the affirming case, and refuse the cartoon of it first. It is not a shrug. It is not people who stopped believing the Bible and dressed it up.

The most serious version begins with the texts themselves, read closely rather than shouted. There are only a handful, and the affirming scholar asks what each one is actually looking at. When Paul writes in Romans that "their women exchanged natural relations for those that are contrary to nature; and the men likewise gave up natural relations with women and were consumed with passion for one another" (Romans 1:26-27), the argument is that the frame is idolatry and excess, desire boiling over its own banks, the exchange of the Creator for the created, and not two people building a faithful covenant. The world Paul knew, the argument goes, had no category for a marriage between two men. What it had was the older man and the boy, the master and the slave, the temple and its excess. When Paul reaches for a word in 1 Corinthians, one of the words the ESV renders "men who practice homosexuality," the affirming scholar points out it is a term so rare we are partly guessing at its edges, and asks whether Paul is condemning an orientation the ancient world did not name or the exploitation it saw everywhere. Matthew Vines and James Brownson have pressed this at length and in good faith, and they are not hacks. They love the book they are arguing about.

Then the affirming case does the thing that is hardest to wave off. It points at the fruit. "Thus you will recognize them by their fruits" (Matthew 7:20), the Lord said, and the affirming Christian says: look, then. Look at the gay believer who prays, who serves, who has been faithful to one person for thirty years, whose life grows patience and kindness and self-control, the very fruit of the Spirit Paul names in Galatians. If the tree is bad, where is the bad fruit. And it makes the trajectory argument, the one that should not be dismissed with a joke: the Spirit has led the church to go back and read its own Bible again before. We quoted chapter and verse for slavery, whole libraries of it, and we were wrong, and it was the Spirit working through the excluded that dragged us to see it. What if we are standing in that same place again, defending with a handful of verses a cruelty our grandchildren will not believe we could not see. That argument has moral seriousness. Refuse to feel it and you have not understood it.

Now turn, and give the other side the identical refusal to caricature. Because the historic teaching is not bigotry with a proof-text stapled on, and treating it that way is its own failure of honesty.

The traditional case starts further back than any of the disputed verses, which is exactly why its defenders think the affirming reading is fighting on the wrong ground. It starts in Genesis, with a pattern laid down before any prohibition: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). Male and female, made for each other, the difference itself part of the design. And the traditional Christian notes who reaches back for that verse. Jesus does. Asked about marriage, he goes to the beginning: "he who created them from the beginning made them male and female" (Matthew 19:4), and grounds the whole of marriage in that pattern. The historic reading does not hang on Paul being angry in Romans. It hangs on a shape the Lord himself treats as the given.

And it goes one step further, into why the shape might matter that much. When Paul quotes the one-flesh verse in Ephesians, he says something staggering about it: "This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). Marriage, on this reading, is not merely a good arrangement for raising the young and easing loneliness. It is a living icon, the union of two who are genuinely other, the way Christ and the church are other, made one without being made the same. Take away the difference of male and female, the traditional Christian argues, and you have not adjusted a rule. You have blurred the picture the thing was made to paint. That is why they cannot treat it as a small matter, and it is not homophobia that makes them say so. It is a theology of the body they think they did not invent.

Then comes the part that gets left out, and it is the part that matters most for whether the teaching is cruel. The historic call is not a special burden built for gay people. It is the call to chastity, and it falls on a crowd. It falls on the single man who wanted to be married and never was. On the widow who buried the only person she ever touched. On the divorced woman who did nothing wrong and is alone anyway. The church has always taught, however badly it has lived it, that a faithful life outside of marriage is not a lesser life, that Jesus himself lived it whole and unmarried, that Paul commended it. Wesley Hill, who is gay and celibate and writes about it without a shred of self-pity, keeps insisting the tradition is not asking him for something it does not ask of millions of straight believers too. If chastity outside marriage is a death sentence for the gay Christian, it is the same sentence for a great many others, and either it is livable for all of them or the church has been lying to the single and the widowed for two thousand years.

The people inside this argument have even given themselves names for it, Side A and Side B, the affirming and the celibate-traditional, and the reason they needed names is that they are both trying to follow Jesus and both refuse to leave. That should tell you something about how far this is from a simple case of the faithful against the faithless.

So put the two questions in the same room and refuse to spare either.

To the affirming Christian, the traditional one says: you have read the fruit honestly, but you have made experience the judge of Scripture, and once you do that, tell me what it cannot revise. Every generation finds its own desires natural and the text embarrassing. If the plain sense of Genesis and the words of Jesus about male and female can be dissolved by how sincerely we want them dissolved, what survives the next thing we sincerely want. You called it the Spirit. How do you know it is not just the age you happen to live in, wearing the Spirit's coat.

To the traditional Christian, the affirming one says: you say it is not a special burden, but look at what you actually did with it. You did not treat gluttony as a boundary of the faith. You did not fence the table against the gossips. You found the one prohibition that costs straight people nothing and made it the wall, and then you told the gay teenager it was about your high theology of the body when it was about your comfort. Your doctrine may be right. Your fruit has been rot. Do not hand me Ephesians with the same mouth that drove that boy out of my church.

Neither of them has a clean answer to the other. That is not a failure of my writing. It is the actual shape of a question the church has not closed.

And I have to name where I am standing, because I am not above this scene, I am in it. I am the pastor who did not notice for three years. When I line up the arguments I can feel my own hands wanting the version that would cost me the least, and I do not fully trust the wanting, on either side. The temptation on the traditional side is to love being right more than I love the person the rightness lands on. The temptation on the affirming side is to love being kind more than I love telling the truth, to buy peace in the room with a softness that is really just my fear of being hated. Both of those are in me. Neither of them is the Holy Spirit. I know that much.

Here is what I will not do. I will not stand over your particular life and hand down a ruling on who you should marry or how you should carry a desire you did not choose, as though I could read your years off a page. The historic teaching of the church is what it is, and I have set it in front of you at full strength, and I have set the serious case against it there too, and I have told you plainly that faithful Christians who love this book are not agreed. What order of question this is, whether it is a boundary or a difference, is itself part of what they are not agreed on, and I am not going to pretend from a keyboard that I have settled for you what the church has not settled for itself.

What I can tell you is that you are not an issue. You never were. You are not a position I am arguing against or a talking point I am defending. If you are gay and you have read this far, past everything the church has already done to make sure you would not, then you have shown more willingness to sit in the hard room than most of the people who talk loudest about you have ever shown you. You are welcome to keep reading here. You are welcome to argue with every line. The door is not the thing my church made you think it was.

So where do I land, since I said I would not hide it. I land with the historic teaching, the one the church has held across every culture for two thousand years: that God made us male and female, that the covenant of marriage is the one place he set apart for sexual union, and that a same-sex sexual relationship falls outside that design, however much love is in it. That is what people call Side B, and I hold it the way Side B holds it. Same-sex attraction is not a sin to be hated out of a person. The call is the call to chastity that lands on every believer who is not married, the widow and the single man and the divorced woman right alongside the gay Christian, and it is not a lesser life, because Jesus lived it whole and unmarried and Paul commended it. The celibate gay believers I have known are not second-class saints. Some of them are the truest disciples in the room.

I will not pretend that costs nothing, and the cost is the objection I have not fully answered. I am asking a gay believer for a lifelong chastity I was never asked to bear, and the church that asks it has mostly failed to build the friendship, the family, the belonging that would make such a life anything but lonely. If we require the cross and withhold the community, we have preached a burden and kept back the body that was supposed to carry it, and the fault for that is ours, not theirs. I hold the historic teaching. I hold it with a debt I have not paid.

I keep going back to that second row. To three years of a voice and none of a life, and to the fact that I got the doctrine into all my sermons and missed the boy entirely. Whatever it cost me to land where I have landed, I am sure of one thing about the day I stand before God to give an account of that pew. He is not going to ask me first whether I was Side A or Side B. He is going to ask me why the boy did not think he could tell me the truth. And I do not yet have an answer that will hold.
