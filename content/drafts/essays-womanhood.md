# Essays — Womanhood

Five essays. Written with women, not over them. Honest about both the
patriarchal failures of evangelical womanhood teaching and the
egalitarian-progressive overreach. The voice has to be one that women in
James's church and the PCN women's network would recognize as serious,
respectful, and not patronizing.

**Editorial note for James:** strongly recommend running these drafts past
two or three women you trust — including at least one who would lean
egalitarian — before publish. The track is too important to get wrong, and
men writing on womanhood always need the correction.

## 51. What Evangelical Womanhood Teaching Got Wrong

**Slug:** `what-evangelical-womanhood-teaching-got-wrong`
**Track:** `womanhood` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~11 min

**Positioning sentence:** The womanhood teaching American evangelicalism delivered to two generations of women was a mix of real biblical insight and serious cultural baggage that did damage. The damage has to be named honestly before the recovery is possible.

**Argument beats:**
1. The mid-twentieth-century evangelical womanhood model. Submission as primary identity. Domesticity as theological vocation. Quiet as virtue. The Proverbs 31 woman as the universal template.
2. What was right in it. The recovery of the household as a site of theological labor. The honoring of the often-invisible work of forming children. The refusal to let secular feminism define women's value purely by professional achievement.
3. What was wrong. The conflation of cultural mid-century American norms with biblical revelation. The use of "biblical womanhood" rhetoric to police women into specific economic and political positions that scripture does not require. The intentional cultivation of dependence rather than agency. The silencing of women's gifts in teaching, leadership, and theological argument.
4. The data on cost. Christian women's mental health. Christian women's experience of abuse inside Christian marriages, frequently with explicit theological justification. The exodus of women — and especially of theologically literate women — from churches that did not honor them.
5. The Beth Allison Barr argument (*The Making of Biblical Womanhood*) deserves serious engagement, including by men who land in different places on the complementarian/egalitarian question. Her historical work is mostly correct even when her conclusions are debatable.
6. Self-implication: I have preached versions of the womanhood teaching I am now repenting of. The repenting has been slow. It is not finished.
7. The verdict: the evangelical womanhood tradition delivered both gift and damage. Name both. Repair the damage. Keep the gift.

**Opening sketch:** "I am writing this essay as a man who has, from a pulpit, taught versions of evangelical womanhood I no longer believe and am still repenting of. The teaching had real biblical insight in it. The teaching also did serious damage. The damage cannot be repaired until the damage is named. I want to name it. I am writing this for the women who endured the teaching, the men who delivered it, and the next generation who deserves something better."

**Closing weight:** The evangelical womanhood tradition gave the church real goods — the honor of the household, the dignity of formative work, the resistance to a feminism that valued only paid labor. The tradition also harmed real women in real ways, with real theological justification, and the repair has not yet been done. The repair will require honest reading of scripture, honest naming of cultural baggage, and the genuinely hard work of building a theology of womanhood that is both biblical and free. The women who endured the prior version deserve our repentance. The women coming after deserve a better account. Both are owed.

**Sources:** Beth Allison Barr (*The Making of Biblical Womanhood*); Aimee Byrd (*Recovering from Biblical Manhood and Womanhood*); Sandra Glahn; Sarah Bessey; Tish Harrison Warren; Hannah Anderson; the work of Wendy Alsup (with appropriate disagreement noted).

## 52. Strength That Is Not Mistaken for Loudness

**Slug:** `strength-that-is-not-mistaken-for-loudness`
**Track:** `womanhood` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** The strong Christian woman of the older tradition was strong in ways the contemporary celebrity-feminism conversation cannot recognize. Recovering that strength is the work — and it is different from both the muted submission the church taught and the perform-your-power frame the surrounding culture has offered as the alternative.

**Argument beats:**
1. The cultural choice presented to Christian women: be quiet (the older evangelical version) or be loud (the contemporary cultural version). The choice is false.
2. The biblical record. Deborah. Huldah. Phoebe. Priscilla. Lydia. The women at the tomb. The women in Romans 16. They were strong, central, theologically literate, and in no case did the New Testament require them to perform their strength as loudness. They simply did the work.
3. The historic Christian record. Macrina the Younger (Gregory of Nyssa's sister and theological teacher). Hildegard of Bingen. Catherine of Siena. Teresa of Ávila. Susanna Wesley. Each was a force. None was loud in the contemporary sense.
4. What strength as not-loudness looks like: presence, durability, theological depth, the willingness to be wrong about something and revise, the refusal to let the surrounding noise dictate the tempo of your work.
5. The constructive: a woman raising her daughters in the present moment needs to teach them a strength that does not require performance, that does not require viral exit, that does not require defining yourself against men. A strength that lives in the long work and gets honored, sometimes, generations after.
6. The verdict: be strong. Be strong in the way Macrina was strong. The loud version is exhausting and brittle. The deep version lasts.

**Closing weight:** Macrina the Younger taught her brother — one of the Cappadocian Fathers — most of what he knew. The teaching happened in conversation, in argument, in the slow work of decades. She did not have a platform. She did not perform her strength. She was strong, and the strength survived the centuries because it was real. Be that woman. Or raise her. The world needs her more than it needs another loud one.

**Sources:** Gregory of Nyssa (*The Life of Macrina*); Catherine Mowry LaCugna (*God for Us*); Phyllis Tickle's work on the women mystics; Hannah Anderson (*Made for More*); Aimee Byrd; the work of Marg Mowczko.

## 53. The Wife Who Refuses to Manage Her Husband

**Slug:** `the-wife-who-refuses-to-manage-her-husband`
**Track:** `womanhood` · **Audience:** `couples` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** A common pattern in evangelical marriages: the wife manages the husband's emotions, schedules his social life, civilizes his behavior, and keeps the marriage's emotional infrastructure running. The pattern is exhausting for the wife and infantilizing for the husband. The marriages that survive into the third decade are the marriages that refuse this division of labor.

**Argument beats:**
1. The pattern. Wives carry the emotional labor of the marriage. They notice when something is wrong, they raise it, they manage the children's emotional weather, they keep the calendar, they hold the friend network, they remember the birthdays. Husbands consume the labor and do not, often, recognize it as labor.
2. The data. Sociological work on emotional labor in heterosexual marriages. Wives' mental health correlates with how much of it they are carrying.
3. The theological reading. The pattern is not what scripture describes as the husband-wife relationship. Ephesians 5 is not asking the wife to manage the husband. It is asking the husband to lay down his life for her, which is the opposite of being managed by her.
4. The shape of the recovery. The husband has to learn to manage himself emotionally — to notice his own state, raise his own concerns, maintain his own friendships, repair his own relational ruptures with his own children. The wife has to learn to stop covering for the husband's failure to do these things.
5. The hard part. Husbands who have not done this work are not going to start because they read an essay. The work usually requires a crisis, a pastor or therapist, or a wife willing to refuse the role she has been silently carrying for fifteen years.
6. Self-implication: Susanna managed me for too long. I am still learning what she carried. The learning is incomplete.
7. The verdict: refuse the management role. The marriage will be worse for two years. It will be better forever after.

**Closing weight:** The wife who refuses to manage her husband does not get to be the manager anymore. The husband who has been managed does not get the manager anymore. Both of them have to grow up. The growing up is hard and worth it. The marriage that survives the growing up is the marriage that has stopped being a parent-child relationship dressed up as a marriage, and has started to be the marriage scripture describes — two adults, both responsible, both forming the household together.

**Sources:** Arlie Hochschild (*The Second Shift*; *The Managed Heart*); Allison Daminger's work on cognitive household labor; Tim and Kathy Keller; John Gottman; Sue Johnson (*Hold Me Tight*); the recent work of Eve Rodsky (*Fair Play*).

## 54. The Single Woman the Church Has Not Honored

**Slug:** `the-single-woman-the-church-has-not-honored`
**Track:** `womanhood` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** The American evangelical church has built almost everything for the nuclear family. The single Christian woman — younger or older, never married, widowed, divorced — has often been treated as an unfinished category waiting for marriage. The treatment is theological malpractice. Singleness is a vocation in scripture, and the recovery of that vocation is among the most pressing pastoral works of the moment.

**Argument beats:**
1. The pattern. Most evangelical churches structure their ministry, language, and community around marriage and family. The single woman attending is welcomed but rarely *honored* as such. She is treated as transitional. She is not.
2. The biblical record. Paul is single and recommends it. Mary Magdalene is single. Many of the women in Acts and the letters who carry the early church's work are single (often widowed). The history of the church features whole orders of women who took singleness as their vocation and were among the most theologically formed Christians of their eras.
3. The historical loss. Protestant Reformation closed the convents. The closing recovered the dignity of marriage, but it also abolished the institutional honor of female singleness. Five hundred years later, evangelicalism has still not built a replacement.
4. The pastoral cost. Single Christian women report some of the highest rates of loneliness, displacement, and church-fatigue of any demographic in the contemporary Western church. Many of them leave. The churches that have not built for them have lost them.
5. The constructive. The church has to build for the single woman as a permanent, honored category. Community, friendship, family-like inclusion in households not her own, recognition of her vocational work, theological formation that treats her as a full participant, not as a marriage-waiting room.
6. The household piece. The Christian household is supposed to include the unmarried — single relatives, widows, adopted aunts. Most evangelical households do not. Recovering this is one of the most concrete things a married Christian can do for the single Christian women in his life.
7. The verdict: build for her. Honor her. Include her in the household. The church that does this will be a healthier church across the board, and the single women in it will not have to leave to be seen.

**Closing weight:** The single woman in your church is not a project. She is a sister, a colleague, a saint, a vocation. The church that treats her as transitional has misunderstood the gospel — which has, since Paul, recognized singleness as one of the two vocations of Christian life and refused to rank them. Recognize her. Honor her. Include her in the meal. The household that does this is doing what the church has always been supposed to do, and most contemporary households have stopped.

**Sources:** Paige Benton Brown's classic essay "Singled Out for Good"; Sam Allberry (*7 Myths About Singleness*); Christina Edmondson; Ed Shaw; the historical work on the female religious orders (Caroline Bynum, *Holy Feast and Holy Fast*); Catherine LaCugna.

## 55. The Mother Whose Work Is Invisible

**Slug:** `the-mother-whose-work-is-invisible`
**Track:** `womanhood` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** Most of the formative work mothers do is invisible to the surrounding culture, often invisible to husbands, and frequently invisible to the mothers themselves. The recovery of theological language for this work is one of the most important formation tasks the church can undertake.

**Argument beats:**
1. The premise. Forming a person is the slowest, hardest, most consequential work most humans ever attempt. The work is done largely by mothers, largely in households, largely outside the surrounding economy's recognition.
2. The cultural diminishment. The "stay-at-home mom" framing renders the work invisible — *staying* and *home* both suggesting passivity and absence from "real" labor. The framing is false on its face. The work is more demanding than most paid work.
3. The theological recovery. Augustine's mother Monica formed Augustine. He says so explicitly. The church has names for the forming work: discipleship, catechesis, formation. The mother is doing all of these. The work just does not get a paycheck.
4. The data on what mothers do. The hours. The cognitive load. The emotional regulation. The teaching of language, of moral categories, of self-control, of the awareness of others. The lullabies, the bedtimes, the prayers, the bathroom routines. None of this appears in any official measurement of value.
5. The hard part. The mother who is doing this work full-time often experiences a crisis of identity precisely because the culture cannot see what she is doing. The crisis is theological — the church can offer her language for what she is doing that the culture cannot.
6. The recovery is for working mothers too. The mother who is also employed outside the home is doing both jobs simultaneously, and her invisible work in the household is even more compressed and more costly. The recovery of theological language applies to her too — perhaps especially to her, since she has the least margin to do the formation work and is doing it anyway.
7. The verdict: name the work. Honor the work. Theologize the work. The household formation a mother does is the foundation everything else is built on. The economy that does not pay her is not the economy that finally measures value. The kingdom does.

**Closing weight:** Monica formed Augustine. The forming did not appear in the imperial census. It produced one of the most consequential Christians in the church's two thousand years. The mother in your life — your wife, your friend, yourself — is doing the same kind of work. The surrounding culture does not see it. The church should. The theology of the household has been the church's particular gift, and it is the theology most of us have forgotten. Recover it. The work is real. The work is sacred. The work is being done. Name it.

**Sources:** Augustine (*Confessions*, on Monica); Hannah Anderson (*Made for More*); Jen Wilkin; Carolyn Custis James; Caroline Bynum; the work of David Brooks on the family and Wendell Berry on the household economy.
