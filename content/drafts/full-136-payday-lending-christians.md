The Mosaic law was not neutral on the question of lending money to the poor.

Exodus 22:25: "If you lend money to any of my people with you who is poor, you shall not be like a moneylender to him, and you shall not exact interest from him." Leviticus 25:35–37 extends the prohibition and ties it explicitly to the memory of Israel's own bondage: you were in Egypt, you know what it is to be crushed by systems that extract from the vulnerable. Deuteronomy 23:19 is categorical. These are not suggestions offered as pastoral guidance to those who feel led in that direction. They are commands embedded in the economic structure of the covenant community, because God understood something that American Christianity has largely forgotten: the capacity to extract interest from the desperate is the capacity to trap them permanently in dependency. It is the mechanism of bondage wearing the clothing of a transaction.

Payday lending — short-term loans at annual percentage rates that commonly exceed 300% [cite — confirm] — is not a new concept. It is usury with modern paperwork. The person who borrows $200 to cover a gap between paychecks and repays $260 two weeks later, only to need another loan immediately because the $260 came out of the rent money, is not experiencing a free market service. They are in a trap designed by people who understood exactly what they were building.

I have not said this from a pulpit. I have preached about personal financial responsibility — the dangers of debt, the virtue of savings, the discipline of the tithe. I have not named the industry that profits specifically from people who cannot practice those virtues because they are already underwater. That silence was not neutrality. It was a choice about whose interests my silence served.

The industry's defenders make a recognizable argument: in the absence of payday lending, where do the poor go for emergency credit? Banks will not lend to them. Credit cards are unavailable or at similarly punishing rates. The payday lender is the lender of last resort, and eliminating it leaves the desperate with nothing. This deserves honest engagement. There are people who would face genuine catastrophe without some form of emergency credit access. The critique of the industry cannot be naive about that reality.

But the existence of a gap does not justify filling it with extraction. The question is not only "is this better than nothing?" It is "what does this do to people, and what does our endorsement of it say about us?" A system that profits specifically from the financial desperation of its clients — that is structurally designed to extend and renew loans rather than resolve them — is not providing a service. It is selling a trap, and it is marketed with particular intensity in low-income communities and communities of color. [cite — confirm] That targeting is not accidental. It is the business model.

The Hebrew word for interest that these passages prohibit is neshek — literally, a bite. The image is deliberate. Interest on loans to the vulnerable was understood as something that consumes the person from whom it is taken. Ezekiel lists lending at interest alongside murder and adultery as sins that defile the covenant community (Ezekiel 18:13). Not peripheral sins. Defiling ones.

The church that is silent on a predatory lending industry while preaching personal financial responsibility has separated the individual virtue from the structural vice that makes it nearly impossible. That separation is not a neutral theological position. It is a choice. It protects the comfort of people whose investment portfolios include financial services companies. It protects the donors whose political preferences include deregulation. It does not protect the woman on the east side of the city who is paying a bite-rate on money she borrowed to keep the electricity on for her children.

The law of Moses was embedded in a story: you were slaves in Egypt, and a God who hears the cry of the poor brought you out. The church that has forgotten that story has forgotten something foundational about who it is and what it owes.

The people in the payday loan office at noon on a Tuesday are not strangers to the biblical text. They are its primary concern.
