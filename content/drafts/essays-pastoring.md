## 28. Why Pastors Quit (And How to Stay)

**Slug:** `why-pastors-quit-and-how-to-stay`
**Track:** `pastoral-ministry` · **Audience:** `pastors` · **Reading time:** ~12 min

**Positioning sentence:** Pastors are quitting in numbers we have not seen in a generation. The reasons are not what most leadership writing names. The path to stay is harder than the books admit, and more accessible than most pastors think.

**Argument beats:**
1. The data. Barna's reports on pastoral burnout post-2020. The numbers of pastors who have left the calling. The numbers who say they have considered leaving.
2. The reasons leadership writing names: workload, congregational politics, lack of self-care. These are real but secondary.
3. The reasons leadership writing does not name: theological loneliness (the pastor who cannot share the questions he is carrying); spiritual abuse from elder boards (more common than most pastors admit, including the ones inflicting it); the absence of any pastor for the pastor; the cumulative weight of holding other people's confidences for twenty years.
4. The honest pastoral economics: most pastors are paid below their education level, in jobs without overtime, with insurance gaps, in housing situations the IRS treats strangely, with no real path to retirement security.
5. The honest pastoral spirituality: most pastors stopped having a personal devotional life years ago because the professional engagement with scripture replaced it, and the replacement is not the same thing.
6. The path to stay: structural changes (sabbatical, elder boards that actually shepherd the pastor, real compensation), spiritual changes (a spiritual director who is not your congregation, a personal practice that is not sermon prep, a community of other pastors who tell you the truth), relational changes (a marriage that is not part of your ministry, friendships outside the church).
7. The verdict: the calling is real. The cost is real. The path is hard. The staying is worth it. But the staying requires the things most pastors are not yet willing to ask for. Ask for them.

**Opening sketch:** "I have spent five years inside the Pastors Connection Network watching pastors quit. Some of them quit because they should have. Most of them quit because no one had built them a way to stay. The leadership writing I read in seminary did not name what is actually breaking them. I want to name it. And I want to name what staying actually requires."

**Closing weight:** Pastoring is the only calling I would still recommend to a young person who asked. It is also the calling I would warn that young person about more honestly than I was warned. The staying is worth it. The leaving is sometimes right. The path between them is the path the PCN exists to walk with you. You do not have to walk it alone. Do not walk it alone. The aloneness is what makes most of the leavers leave.

**Sources:** Eugene Peterson (*The Pastor*); Andy Crouch (*Strong and Weak*); Paul Tripp (*Dangerous Calling*); Glenn Packiam (*The Resilient Pastor*); the Barna *State of Pastors* reports; PCN's own pastor wellness data.

## 29. The Loneliness of Knowing What You Know

**Slug:** `the-loneliness-of-knowing-what-you-know`
**Track:** `pastoral-ministry` · **Audience:** `pastors` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** The pastor carries weight no one else in the congregation knows is being carried. The carrying is silent by professional necessity. The silence is the loneliness. This is the secret cost of the work.

**Argument beats:**
1. The premise: pastors know things about their congregations they cannot share, will not share, must not share. The accumulated weight of those things is the pastoral burden no one outside the work understands.
2. The specific texture: the affair you know about before the spouse does. The mental health diagnosis you carry with the family. The financial collapse you are helping conceal until the conceal is no longer possible. The doubt the elder is in that the elder cannot share. The dying that no one outside immediate family knows about yet.
3. The professional ethic: confidentiality is non-negotiable. The breaches of it that we read about in scandals are part of why pastoral authority has shrunk. But the unbroken confidentiality is also the thing that isolates the pastor from the rest of the community, including from his own spouse, including sometimes from his own elder board.
4. The pastoral loneliness Bonhoeffer named in *Life Together* — the loneliness of the one who lives among others he cannot share himself with, who shares Christ to them but cannot share the burden Christ has placed on him with them.
5. What helps: a spiritual director outside the congregation; a small group of other pastors who hold each other's burdens under strict confidence (PCN is built for this); a confessor — actually using the confessional structure the historic church developed for exactly this purpose; a marriage that is honored as a marriage and not a ministry partnership; the discipline of a body of literature, prayer, and study that is for the pastor's soul and is not used in the sermon.
6. Self-implication: I have failed to find these things, found them late, lost them in seasons, and the failure has cost me visibly. I am writing this from inside the recovery.
7. The verdict: the loneliness is structural. The structure can be changed. The changing is most of what makes the calling sustainable for a lifetime.

**Opening sketch:** "I am writing this from a hotel room in a city where I came to officiate a funeral. The funeral was for a member of my church who died in a way the church does not yet know about and may not, in some details, ever know. I am sitting with what I know. The sitting is the pastoral loneliness I want to name. Most of the pastors I have ever known have been carrying versions of this. None of them was warned."

**Closing weight:** The pastor's loneliness is the cost of the trust the pastor has been given. The cost is not removable. The cost is, however, distributable — across a spiritual director, a confessor, a pastor's group, a wise spouse, the historic practices the church developed because the people doing this work have always known what the work costs. Find your people. Carry the weight together. The aloneness will kill you. The carrying together will not.

**Sources:** Bonhoeffer (*Life Together*); Peterson (*The Contemplative Pastor*; *Working the Angles*); Henri Nouwen (*The Wounded Healer*); the desert fathers; PCN's own work on pastoral confession and accountability.

## 30. When the Church Should Close

**Slug:** `when-the-church-should-close`
**Track:** `pastoral-ministry` · **Audience:** `pastors` · **Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** Most leadership writing assumes the church must survive at all costs. Most leadership writing is wrong. Some churches should close. Knowing which is the most pastorally important judgment of the season.

**Argument beats:**
1. The thing leadership writing will not say: not every church that exists should continue to exist. Some churches should close — for the sake of the gospel, the witness, the remaining members, and the surrounding community.
2. The four conditions under which closing is the faithful move: (a) the church has become so theologically corrupt that closing is the only way to stop the harm; (b) the church has become so small that continued attempts to survive consume all energy without producing any actual ministry; (c) the church has become so toxic in interpersonal dynamics that no pastor can do any pastoring inside it; (d) the church has become a building maintenance project with no congregation left to maintain.
3. The hardest pastoral judgment in the season: distinguishing decline that is judgment from decline that is winnowing. Both look the same from inside. The judgment requires outside perspective.
4. What a faithful closing looks like: deliberate, honored, with a funeral. The closing of a church should be performed as the funeral it is. Members should mourn. The building should be passed on with intention. The remaining members should be commended to other congregations.
5. The denominational and institutional resistance to closing. The financial and identity-protective reasons institutions keep dying things on life support past the point of dignity.
6. The constructive: the closing of a church can be the most gospel-shaped act the church performs. The willingness to die — and to die well — is what the church has been preaching for two thousand years. The church that practices its own death is the church that has been listening to its own sermons.
7. The verdict: most churches should not close. Some should. Knowing which is the pastoral judgment of the season. Do not be afraid to make it.

**Opening sketch:** "I have closed two churches in my pastoral career. One I knew should close; closing it was the most pastoral thing I did all year. The other I did not know should close; I tried to save it for three years, and the trying cost more than the closing would have. I want to talk about churches that should close. Most leadership writing will not. Most leadership writing is wrong."

**Closing weight:** The church that practices its own death is the church that has remembered its own theology. The willingness to die — and to die well — is the willingness to trust that the resurrection is not just doctrine. Some of the churches in your area should close. Some of them should have closed a decade ago. The faithfulness is in the discernment, the honesty, the willingness to perform the funeral the institution has earned. The funeral is the last sermon the church preaches. Preach it well.

**Sources:** Andrew Root (*The Church After Innovation*); Stanley Hauerwas (*A Community of Character*); Christian Smith (*American Evangelicalism*); the denominational reports on church closures (UMC, PCUSA, Episcopal, SBC) over the past decade.
