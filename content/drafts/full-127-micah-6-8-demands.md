*He has shown you, O mortal, what is good. And what does the Lord require of you? To act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God.*

We have printed this on coffee mugs. We have put it in church lobbies in hand-lettered calligraphy. We have used it as a caption. And in doing all of that, we have managed to receive the most demanding verse in the prophetic canon as a kind of spiritual aesthetic — something beautiful to aspire toward when we are feeling generous, which is to say: something we have made optional.

The text is not interested in being optional.

Stand it in its context. Micah 6 opens as a lawsuit — the Hebrew form is *rib*, a legal controversy — in which God summons the mountains and the hills as witnesses and brings a charge against Israel. The charge is not theological error. It is not liturgical failure. The charge is the systematic exploitation of the vulnerable by those with enough power to get away with it: the wealthy taking land that did not belong to them (Micah 2:2), judges taking bribes, prophets speaking for hire, priests teaching for money (Micah 3:11). The religious machinery continues to function while the poor are consumed by those who run it.

Into that context, Micah places the question the people are already asking: what do we need to do to make this right with God? Do we need more sacrifices? More elaborate burnt offerings? He meets the question and then deflates it. You already know what is required. God has already shown it to you. Not elaborate religious performance — *this*: do justice, love kindness, walk humbly.

The word translated *act justly* is *asah mishpat* — to practice or execute justice, to do the active work of establishing right order where it has been disrupted. Not to hold a conviction about justice in the abstract. To *do* it. The active verb carries weight. Justice, in the prophetic tradition, is always something that must be accomplished against resistance, because the arrangements that produce injustice are backed by power that does not give way easily.

The word translated *love mercy* is *ahav hesed* — to love steadfast covenantal kindness, the word that describes God's loyal love toward Israel across centuries of unfaithfulness. You cannot manufacture hesed. It is relational warmth that has been tested over time and found to hold. To *love* hesed is to have it become the disposition from which you act — not occasional generosity, but the steady orientation of a heart that has received more than it deserved and understands what that means for how it treats others.

The third requirement — *walk humbly with your God* — carries the others. Humility here is not self-deprecation. It is the orientation of a creature who knows whose world this is, who holds power with open hands because the power was given and will be required back. The person who walks humbly with God cannot use power to consume the vulnerable, because she knows that power was not given for that.

This is not a summary of the law. It is a diagnosis of what Israel had lost and what the people of God are always at risk of losing: the integration of justice, mercy, and humility into a single life that reflects the character of God.

I have held this verse for years and still find the gap between what it describes and what I practice. I am better at talking about justice than I am at absorbing its costs. I am better at teaching hesed than at extending it to the person who has made my life difficult. I am better at the language of humility than at the daily surrender it requires.

The verse does not accept that gap as a permanent condition. It describes what is required. Not what is recommended, not what the spiritually ambitious might aspire to — what the Lord requires.

That word *requires* is the one the coffee mug does not say.
