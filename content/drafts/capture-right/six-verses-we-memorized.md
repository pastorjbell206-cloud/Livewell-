Count the verses your church has actually memorized. Not the ones in the pew Bible — the ones in the bloodstream, the ones a member can finish if you start them. In most conservative American congregations the list is short and it is sharp. John 3:16. Jeremiah 29:11. Philippians 4:13. And, when the conversation turns to the government, Romans 13:1 — *let every person be subject to the governing authorities* — deployed with a confidence that suggests the verse settles something. That is six or seven verses doing the work of a whole political theology.

Now count the ones nobody memorized. The roughly two thousand verses about money and the poor that scholars have been tallying for decades. Leviticus 19, where the law commands Israel to treat the immigrant as a native-born citizen. Amos, who calls a prosperous nation's worship services an offense to God. The parable in Luke 16 where a rich man ends up in torment for the simple crime of not noticing the beggar at his gate. We did not tear those pages out. We just never put them in bold.

I am a conservative-leaning pastor writing to conservative-leaning Christians, so let me make the claim plainly and then spend the rest of this essay paying for it. **A tribe does not edit the Bible by deleting verses. It edits the Bible by deciding which verses are load-bearing and which are decoration — and the American right has built a working canon out of maybe a dozen texts while quietly filing the other sixty under "context."** The result is a Bible that, read by emphasis alone, would be unrecognizable to the prophets who wrote half of it.

## The honest case for the verses we kept

Start by being fair, because the verses we memorized are real verses, and the instinct behind them is not stupid.

Romans 13 exists. Paul did write, to Christians living under Nero of all people, that the governing authority is established by God and that the believer who resists it resists what God has appointed. That is not a fringe text smuggled in by a political action committee. It is Scripture, and it carries a genuine conviction the modern church desperately needs to recover: that order is a gift, that anarchy is not holiness, that the Christian is not licensed to burn down every structure he dislikes in the name of conscience. A faith that cannot say a word in favor of law and stability is a faith that has read only half the Bible too.

The same goes for the texts on borders, on nations, on the difference between order and chaos. Genesis does describe God scattering humanity into nations at Babel. Acts 17 does say God determined the boundaries of the peoples. There is a real biblical theme that human life is meant to be ordered, bounded, governed — that the dissolving of every distinction is not the kingdom of God but the flood. A Christian conservatism that prizes order, lawful authority, and the moral weight of the nation is not pulling those concerns out of thin air. It is pulling them out of the text.

So the problem is not that the right reads Romans 13. The problem is what happens to Romans 13 when it is the only government text you have memorized, and what happens to the sixty verses standing right beside it that never made the list.

## What Romans 13 says when you read the whole thing

Here is the trouble with a memorized verse. You stop at the part you can recite.

Romans 13 does not end at verse one. Paul keeps writing, and a few sentences later he says the authority is God's servant *for your good* — meaning the authority is accountable to a purpose, not a blank check. Then he lands the paragraph on a sentence almost nobody quotes: *owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law* (Romans 13:8). The same chapter that commands submission to government commands, in the same breath, that love is the fulfillment of the law and that we owe it to everyone. Paul did not write a chapter about obedience. He wrote a chapter about love that includes obedience as one of its forms.

Christopher Wright, whose *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God* (2004) spent four hundred pages refusing to let readers pick a verse and run, made the point that you cannot extract a single command from Scripture and treat it as the whole counsel of God on a subject. A verse is a brick. A brick is not a building. When you build an entire doctrine of the Christian's relationship to the state out of Romans 13:1 and stop reading at the period, you have not honored the text. You have quarried it.

**You can obey a verse and disobey the Bible at the same time, simply by memorizing the half that flatters you.**

## The sixty verses standing next to the six

Now turn to the texts the tribe skipped, and notice they are not obscure. They are not buried in genealogies. They are some of the most direct commands in the entire canon.

Leviticus 19:33–34 is the one that should end the argument before it starts. *When a stranger sojourns with you in your land, you shall not do him wrong. You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt.* That is not a hint. That is not an inference. That is a direct command, grounded in Israel's own memory of being the immigrant, to extend to the foreigner the same standing as the citizen — and to love him with the same love the law had just commanded for the neighbor. The God who, in Genesis, set the boundaries of the nations is the same God who, in Leviticus, told his nation that the immigrant inside its boundaries gets treated like family. Both verses are in the book. We memorized one.

Then there is Matthew 25:31–46, the only passage in the Gospels where Jesus describes the final judgment scene at length, and the criterion he names is not doctrinal precision or national loyalty. It is this: I was hungry and you fed me, a stranger and you welcomed me, in prison and you came. *As you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me.* The word translated stranger is the word for foreigner, the immigrant, the one from outside. Jesus put welcome of the foreigner into the description of the last judgment and then identified himself with that foreigner so completely that how you treat him is how you treat Christ. This is not a liberal reading. It is a reading of the words on the page.

Luke 16 is sharper still, because it names no sin at all. The rich man is not accused of fraud or cruelty. He simply lives well while Lazarus starves at his gate, and he ends in torment, and the gulf is fixed. The condemnation is for the not-noticing. Walter Brueggemann spent a career arguing that the prophetic imagination exists precisely to make a comfortable people notice the suffering their comfort had trained them to walk past — and Luke 16 is that argument in a single parable. The damnable thing was a man who could step over a dying neighbor on his own doorstep and feel nothing.

And Amos 5, where God says to a religious, prosperous, ascendant nation: *I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies* (Amos 5:21). The most chilling thing in that chapter is that the worship was good worship. The festivals were biblical festivals. God rejected them anyway, because *let justice roll down like waters* (Amos 5:24) was the actual test, and the nation had aced the liturgy and failed the test. Amos was preaching to people who had memorized the right verses and skipped the right neighbors.

James 2 closes the loop in the New Testament. Show me a faith that lets you tell a hungry man *go in peace, be warmed and filled* while you give him nothing, James says, and I will show you a faith that is dead (James 2:15–17). The brother of Jesus drew the line exactly where his brother drew it.

Set these side by side and the scale of the editing becomes visible. Six or seven verses about order, obedience, and personal belief — memorized, recited, deployed in every argument about the state. And against them: a command to treat the immigrant as a citizen, a judgment scene that turns on welcoming the stranger, a parable that damns the man who failed to notice a beggar, a prophet who told a successful nation its worship disgusted God, and an apostle who called a stingy faith a corpse. These are not the marginal verses. They are some of the most direct moral commands in the entire canon, and a tribe that knows Romans 13:1 cold could not, on its best day, produce three of them from memory. The neglect is not random. It is curated.

What makes the curation so durable is that it never feels like a choice. No one in the pew experiences the skipped sixty as skipped. They experience the memorized six as simply *the verses that matter*, and the rest as background the serious people have already weighed and set aside. That is the genius of editing by emphasis. You never have to argue against a verse you have rendered invisible. You only have to keep the spotlight pointed somewhere else, year after year, until a whole generation of believers can quote you their faith and not one line of it costs them anything they did not already want to give.

## Why we kept these particular verses

The interesting question is not *that* a tribe edits Scripture by emphasis. Every tribe does. The interesting question is *why this set.* Why these six verses and not those sixty.

Look at the pattern and it is not hard to read. The verses we memorized cost us almost nothing. Romans 13 on submission asks me to obey laws I mostly already like. The border texts ask me to defend a national order that already protects me. The verses about hell and personal salvation ask me to believe something, which is free. Now look at the sixty we skipped. Every one of them costs money, or comfort, or the willingness to be inconvenienced by a person from outside my tribe. Leviticus 19 costs me my preference for people who look like me. Matthew 25 costs me my Saturday. Luke 16 costs me the right to not notice. Amos costs me the comfort of believing my good religion is the same thing as a good life.

Lesslie Newbigin, writing after forty years in India to a Western church that could no longer see its own assumptions, warned that the most dangerous distortions of the gospel are never the ones a culture argues against. They are the ones it cannot see, because the culture supplied the lens. We did not consciously decide to skip the verses about the poor and the stranger. The tribe handed us a lens, and the lens made some verses bright and others dim, and we read by the light we were given and called it the plain meaning of Scripture. **The most edited Bible in America has not had a single word removed. It has only had its emphases assigned by a political party that never opened it.**

My own shelf indicts me. There are verses I have preached forty times and verses I have never once built a sermon around, and the dividing line between them is not their importance in the text. It is their cost to me and my people. I have given Romans 13 its full Sunday. I am not sure I have ever given Leviticus 19:34 a single one. That is not a curriculum decision. That is an editorial one, and I made it without ever admitting I was holding the pen.

And the lens does more than dim the inconvenient verses. It rewrites the bright ones. When a tribe reads Romans 13 as a standalone doctrine of obedience, it has not only skipped Leviticus 19 — it has changed what Romans 13 means, because a verse read alone always means something narrower and harder than a verse read in its paragraph. The proof-text is not a smaller version of the whole. It is a different thing wearing the whole's clothes. Newbigin's warning applies twice over: the culture supplies the lens, and the lens does not just hide what it darkens — it distorts what it lights. A church can be most confident it is reading the Bible plainly at the exact moment it is reading it least.

## The reading that judges every tribe

Here is the turn, and it does not let the other side off either.

The left has its own short list and its own skipped sixty. It memorizes Matthew 25 and Amos and somehow never arrives at the verses on sexual holiness, on the reality of judgment, on the cost of following Jesus that falls on the disciple's own desires. It quarries the prophets for justice and skips Paul on personal righteousness. The editing runs both directions. Nobody comes to this with clean hands, and a man who only ever indicts the tribe across the aisle has simply found a more flattering way to skip his own sixty verses.

But I am writing to my own people, and to my own people I have to say the harder thing. We did not lose those sixty verses to liberalism or to the academy. We lost them to ourselves. We let a political identity decide which Scripture was central and which was context, and we did it while believing, the whole time, that we were the ones taking the Bible seriously and everyone else was the one playing games with the text. That is the particular trap of the conservative reader. The sin does not feel like editing. It feels like faithfulness.

Christopher Wright's whole argument was that the canon resists this. You can ignore a verse, but you cannot make it stop being there. Leviticus 19:34 is still in the law. Matthew 25 is still the judgment scene. Luke 16 still ends with the rich man in torment for the sin of not noticing. They sit in the book, undimmed by our neglect, waiting.

## The verdict

So here is the test, and I have failed it.

Open the Bible to the verses you have never preached, never memorized, never built a conviction around — and ask why. If the answer is that they were unimportant, the prophets will dispute you. If the answer is that they were unclear, Leviticus 19 and Matthew 25 are among the clearest commands in the canon. The honest answer, for most of us, is that those verses asked something of us that the memorized ones did not, and we are practiced at reading right past the cost.

A tribe that loves order is not wrong to love order. A tribe that fears chaos is not wrong to fear it. But a tribe that uses Romans 13 to command the poor man's obedience and skips Leviticus 19 commanding its own welcome of the stranger has not read the Bible conservatively. It has read it selfishly, and called the selfishness reverence.

The sixty verses are still there. They were there the whole time. We were the ones who decided they did not count, and the God who wrote all eighty thousand of them did not ask our permission, and he is going to ask, on a day none of us schedules, why we knew six of them by heart and could not finish a single one of the rest.

He is going to ask it as the stranger. He told us he would.
