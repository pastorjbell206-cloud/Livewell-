A bakery loses a lawsuit and a famous pastor calls it the catacombs. A coach is told to stop leading prayers at midfield and the word from the conference stage is *martyrdom.* A chaplain gets a memo from HR and somewhere a fundraising email goes out with the word *persecution* in the subject line, because that word moves money, and we have learned exactly how much it moves. None of these people lost their lives. Most of them kept their jobs. Several of them got book deals. And every one of the events was described, by serious Christians, with a word the New Testament reserves for being killed.

This is the thing I cannot stop turning over. **The American church is the most powerful religious body in the history of the world, and it has spent the last two decades teaching itself to feel hunted.** That is not a contradiction the church has failed to notice. It is a contradiction the church has come to depend on. The hunting feeling does work. It raises money, it wins elections, it fills rooms, and it relieves us of the much harder question of whether we have anything left to say that a free society would actually want to hear.

So I want to be careful here, because the anxiety underneath the word is not fake. That part I will not mock.

## The fear is real, even when the word is wrong

Start with what is true, because the version of this argument worth making is the fair one.

Something did die. A man who is fifty-five years old grew up in a country where the public school assumed his faith, where the stores closed on Sunday, where the President quoted his Bible and the culture, whatever it actually believed, at least flattered what he believed. He raised his children in a country where the school assumes nothing, the stores never close, the culture finds his convictions quaint at best and bigoted at worst, and his grandchildren may grow up not knowing a single practicing Christian their own age. That is not nothing. That is an enormous loss, and the people feeling it are not imagining it. The ground genuinely moved.

Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), gave the loss its real name, and the name is not persecution. It is the end of a "naïve" faith — the condition in which belief was simply the water everyone swam in, unquestioned, the default setting of a whole civilization. Taylor's point is that we have moved from a world in which it was nearly impossible *not* to believe to a world in which faith is one contested option among many, held against pressure, never assumed. The church did not get attacked. The church got *optional.* And to a body that spent a thousand years as the unquestioned center, becoming one option among many feels, from the inside, exactly like being attacked. The nervous system cannot tell the difference between losing a fight and losing a throne.

So I will grant the whole emotional truth of it. The disorientation is real. The grief is legitimate. A man who feels the world he was promised slipping out from under his children is not a liar.

He is just using the wrong word. And the wrong word is not a small mistake. The wrong word is the whole problem.

## A church that wrote the script now calls itself the victim

Here is what makes our use of the word not merely inaccurate but almost comic, if it were not so corrosive.

Tom Holland — the historian, not the actor — spent his career as a secular classicist admiring the hard, glittering cruelty of Rome, until he noticed something that unsettled him. In *Dominion* (2019) he argues that the moral instincts the modern West takes for granted — the dignity of the weak, the conscience of the powerful, the very idea that the victim has a claim on us and the strong owe the strong nothing — are not natural to humanity at all. They are Christian. They are the slow, total revolution of a faith that put a tortured laborer at the center of the universe and declared that God is found among the crucified and not among the crucifiers. Holland's stunned conclusion is that even the secular activist denouncing the church is denouncing it in the church's own borrowed moral vocabulary. The water everyone swims in is Christian water.

Sit with what that means for our persecution talk. The civilization the American church claims is hunting it is a civilization the church built. The human-rights language, the concern for the marginalized, the suspicion of raw power — the very instruments now turned against us were forged by us. **We are not being thrown to the lions. We are being asked, by a culture we evangelized, to live up to the ethics we taught it.** When a Christian institution is held to a standard of fairness or honesty and falls short and is criticized for it, that is not Rome. That is the gospel's own children handing the gospel its own report card.

A persecuted church does not get to file lawsuits. A persecuted church does not have a voting bloc that politicians court on their knees. A persecuted church does not own the radio stations, the publishing houses, the tax-exempt billions, the megachurch campuses with their own coffee bars and parking ministries. The early church had none of that. We have all of it, and we have convinced ourselves we are the early church. That is not analysis. That is theater, and the audience we are performing for is ourselves.

## What the word actually meant when Jesus used it

Now the turn that costs me, because I preach to people who reach for this word and I have not always corrected them gently.

Read the Beatitude slowly. *Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Blessed are you when others revile you and persecute you and utter all kinds of evil against you falsely on my account* (Matthew 5:10–12). Two things are buried in that sentence that we routinely skip. The persecution is *for righteousness* — not for being on the wrong side of a culture war we picked, not for being rude, not for losing an argument we should have lost. And it is *on my account* — for Jesus, for the cross, for the offense of the gospel itself, not for the offense of our politics wearing the gospel as a jersey.

This matters because most of what we are calling persecution fails both tests. We are not reviled for righteousness. We are, often, simply reviled for being unkind, or partisan, or hypocritical, and we have learned to relabel the consequence of our own behavior as suffering for Christ. Peter saw this coming with disturbing precision. *Let none of you suffer as a murderer or a thief or an evildoer or as a meddler. Yet if anyone suffers as a Christian, let him not be ashamed* (1 Peter 4:15–16). Peter draws the line we refuse to draw. There is suffering that comes from being Christ's, and there is suffering that comes from being a meddler, and the gospel honors the first and gives no glory to the second. Much of our "persecution" is the suffering of the meddler, dressed up in the robes of the martyr.

And Jesus was blunt about the source. *If the world hates you, know that it hated me before it hated you. If you were of the world, the world would love you as its own* (John 15:18–19). The hatred he promised was for nearness to him — for holiness, for refusing the world's terms, for a love so total it indicted the comfortable. Paul says the same without flinching: *all who desire to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted* (2 Timothy 3:12). Notice what is missing from every one of these texts. None of them promise persecution for losing political power. None of them treat the loss of cultural dominance as a martyrdom. The cross they describe is the cross of *being like Jesus,* and Jesus did not die because Rome stopped flattering his religion. He died because he told the truth to power and power killed him for it.

We have inverted the whole thing. We grieve the loss of being the power, and we call the grief a cross.

## The persecuted church is a real address, and it is not ours

There are Christians being persecuted right now. They are simply not us, and the distance between their experience and ours should silence us.

Rodney Stark, in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996), did the sociology that the church romanticizes and never reads. The early Christians who grew the faith from a few thousand to a few million did not do it from a position of strength. They did it as a despised, illegal, periodically slaughtered minority — and Stark's argument is that they grew *because* of how they suffered, not in spite of it. When the plagues came and the pagans fled their own dying, the Christians stayed and nursed the sick, their own and everyone's, and died doing it, and the city watched. The witness was the willingness to lose. The early church had no cultural power to defend, so it had nothing to do with its faith but live it and die for it, and the world could not look away. They did not ask to be the center. They asked to be faithful at the edge, and the edge converted the center.

The persecuted church today lives at that same edge. The believers in Plateau State whose villages are burned. The house churches in places where a baptism is a criminal act. The convert from another faith who knows that confessing Christ may cost her family, her marriage, her body. These are people for whom the word *persecution* is not a fundraising line. It is Tuesday. And here is what should stop the breath of every comfortable American Christian who reaches for the word: those believers, the actually persecuted ones, are not asking to run their countries. They are not nostalgic for a throne they never had. They are asking only to be allowed to follow Jesus, and they are following him whether or not they are allowed. They have the thing we lost, and they got it precisely by not having the thing we are grieving.

Lesslie Newbigin saw the American and British churches headed for exactly this confusion and tried to warn us. Having spent decades as a missionary in India — a true minority, a real margin — he came home to a West that had quietly stopped believing and found a church that did not know it was now a mission field. His charge in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989) was that the church must learn to be a faithful minority again, to bear witness from the margin rather than rule from the center, and that its great temptation would be to mistake the loss of Christendom for the death of the gospel. He was describing us before we existed. **We were handed the missionary's opportunity and we have responded with the emperor's grievance.**

I have used the word loosely myself. I will not pretend I have stood outside this. I have felt the pull of the hunted feeling — felt how it warms a room, how it bonds people, how much easier it is to rally the wounded than to convict the comfortable. The persecution narrative is not just dishonest. It is *delicious,* and that is what makes it dangerous, and that is why I distrust it most in the moments I most want to believe it.

## What the lie does to us

Walk it forward, because the cost is not abstract. A church that believes it is persecuted behaves in specific ways, and none of them look like Jesus.

It becomes defensive when the gospel calls it to be self-giving. It reaches for power when the gospel calls it to relinquish it, because the persecuted have a right to fight back and we have granted ourselves that right by adopting the label. It treats every neighbor who disagrees as an enemy combatant, because that is what a persecutor is. And it cannot evangelize, because you cannot win the people you have decided are hunting you. The persecution narrative does not just misdescribe our situation. It poisons the only posture from which we could actually reach the people we say we want to reach. The world Stark's church converted was watching Christians die for them. The world our church is trying to convert is watching Christians sue them, fear them, and campaign against them while calling it a cross.

There is a final cruelty in it, and it is the cruelty of the wolf crying victim. Every time we call an inconvenience persecution, we spend down the word, and the word belongs to people who are actually dying. We are running up a debt against a vocabulary that the burned villages of Nigeria have already paid for in blood. When the genuinely persecuted church looks to its rich American siblings and hears us describe a lost bakery contract as the catacombs, what we have told them is that we do not see them at all — that their dead grandchildren and our HR memos occupy the same category in our imagination. That is not solidarity. That is theft.

And the lie costs us the one thing the loss could have given us. A church that loses its privilege and knows it has lost its privilege is freed — freed to stop managing the culture and start loving it, freed from the exhausting work of policing a country that stopped agreeing with it decades ago, freed to be small and strange and clear the way the church is at its best. Taylor's secular age is not a verdict against the gospel. It is a return of the conditions under which the gospel was first preached, to people who had no power and no cultural cover and nothing to commend their faith but the faith itself. That is not a catastrophe. That is an opportunity dressed as a catastrophe, and we cannot accept the gift because we have spent all our energy insisting we have been robbed. The man who will not admit he has lost his throne can never discover that he has been handed a cross — and the cross was always the better thing.

So here is where it leaves me, and I do not get to leave myself out of it.

We were never persecuted. We were comfortable, and then we were a little less comfortable, and we could not tell the difference between losing a throne and carrying a cross — because we had held the throne so long we had forgotten the cross was ever the point. The gospel does not promise us the center back. It never promised us the center at all. It promised us a way to be faithful at the edge, the way the church has been faithful at the edge in every century but the one we happened to be born into and mistook for normal.

The early church grew because it was willing to lose everything and did. We are shrinking because we are unwilling to lose anything and have started lying about what we have lost. The cross we keep claiming is the one thing we have refused to pick up.
