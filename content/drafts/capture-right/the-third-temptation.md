The devil does not begin with sin. He begins with strategy. Read the wilderness account slowly and the thing that should unsettle you is how reasonable the offer sounds — how it never once asks Jesus to do evil, only to take a shortcut to the good. Turn stones to bread: feed the hungry, which the Messiah came to do anyway. Throw yourself from the temple: prove God's care, which is true. And then the third, the one that drops the disguise. *The devil took him to a very high mountain and showed him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory* (Matthew 4:8). Not money. Not pleasure. Not even survival. The crowning temptation, the one the tempter saved for last, was power — and the price was a single act of worship.

That is the temptation the church keeps failing. And we fail it the way Jesus was tempted to fail it: convinced the whole time that we are reaching for something good.

I am going to make a claim and then spend the essay earning it. **The third temptation — *all this I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me* — was always the deepest one, because power is the thing the church wants most and confesses least. And the church that grasps political power to win the world for Christ has not found a clever way to advance the kingdom. It has accepted the exact offer the King himself refused, on the exact terms the tempter named.**

## Read the offer again, without flinching

Be fair to the temptation, because the version worth refusing is the strong one, and the strong version is genuinely seductive.

Satan does not lie about what he is holding. *To you I will give all this authority and their glory, for it has been delivered to me, and I give it to whomever I will* (Luke 4:6). Jesus does not correct him. Jesus does not say *the kingdoms are not yours to give.* In some real and terrible sense, the kingdoms of the world were on offer, and the price was not murder or theft. The price was worship — bend the knee, just once, to the power that runs the world, and the world is yours. And here is what makes it the deepest temptation of the three: Jesus *wanted* the kingdoms. He came for them. The whole point of the incarnation is that the kingdoms of the world will become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ. The tempter was not offering Jesus something foreign to his mission. He was offering Jesus his own mission's endpoint, by a road that skipped the cross.

That is the genius of it. The temptation is never *abandon the goal.* The temptation is always *reach the goal without the suffering — take the throne without the tree.* And the answer Jesus gives is not an argument about whether the kingdoms are worth having. It is a refusal of the terms. *You shall worship the Lord your God, and him only shall you serve* (Matthew 4:10; Luke 4:8). He will have the kingdoms. He will not have them on the mountain. He will have them from the cross, and the difference between those two roads is the difference between the church and its counterfeit.

## The King who ran from the crown

The clearest commentary on the third temptation is not in the wilderness at all. It is the day the crowd tried to make Jesus the thing the tempter offered, and watch what he did.

He had just fed five thousand. The political moment was perfect. Here was a leader who could produce bread, heal the sick, draw the multitudes — and the people did the natural thing, the thing every movement does with a man like that. *Perceiving then that they were about to come and take him by force to make him king, Jesus withdrew again to the mountain by himself* (John 6:15). Read it against the temptation and the symmetry is exact. Satan took him *up* a mountain to offer him the crown. The crowd would have carried him *up* to a throne. Both times the mountain meant power handed over. And both times the King walked away from it — not because the crown was beneath him, but because the crown offered on those terms was a counterfeit of the one he was actually going to wear.

**The world's instinct, when it meets real spiritual authority, is to turn it into political power. The church's calling, when it meets that same instinct, is to do what Jesus did and withdraw.** We have not been withdrawing. We have been climbing the mountain, telling ourselves the crowd is the Holy Spirit.

Walter Brueggemann spent a career describing the alternative the prophets held against this exact pull. In *The Prophetic Imagination* in 1978 he set two things against each other that we keep collapsing into one — the royal consciousness and the prophetic imagination. The royal consciousness is the mind of empire: it manages, it secures, it numbs, it builds Solomon's temple with conscripted labor and calls it the work of God. The prophetic imagination stands outside the palace and grieves, and dreams, and announces a God who is not domesticated by the throne. Brueggemann's point is that Israel's disaster was not that it lacked power but that it acquired it — that the moment the people of God got a king like the nations, they began to think like the nations, and the prophetic voice had to be smuggled back in from the margins by men the court wanted dead. The church that runs to political power is asking, again, for a king like the nations. We have read the warning. We keep volunteering anyway.

## The Grand Inquisitor knew

Dostoevsky saw the whole thing, and he put it in the mouth of the one character who understood the temptation better than anyone — because he had accepted it.

In *The Brothers Karamazov*, published in 1880, Ivan tells his brother a story he has invented. Christ returns to earth during the Spanish Inquisition, in Seville, and is promptly arrested by the church that bears his name. The old Cardinal, the Grand Inquisitor, comes to his cell at night, and he does not deny Christ. He accuses him. *You,* he says, *had your chance.* In the wilderness the spirit offered you the three things that could have bound humanity in happy obedience — miracle, mystery, and authority — and you refused all three because you wanted men to follow you freely. And free following is too hard. Most men cannot bear it. So we, the church, took up the offer you rejected. *We have corrected your work,* the Inquisitor says, and founded it on miracle, mystery, and authority, and the people are grateful, and they lay their freedom at our feet, and they are happy as children are happy. *We are not with you. We are with him* — and he means the tempter on the mountain.

It is the most devastating thing ever written about the church, and it was written by a man who believed, about the institution he loved. The Grand Inquisitor is not a portrait of unbelievers. He is a portrait of the most religious men in the world, who decided that the kingdom would come faster if they simply accepted the offer Christ had been too pure, or too foolish, to take. **They took the kingdoms of the world in order to give them to Christ, and somewhere in the taking the thing they were holding became the tempter's, not the King's.** That is the warning Dostoevsky leaves in your hands. The church is most likely to bow on the mountain at the exact moment it is most certain it is serving Christ.

## The shape of the King is the opposite of the shape of power

Now the place where the New Testament settles it, because Paul put the whole argument into one hymn and the hymn is the antidote to the mountain.

*Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus, who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant* (Philippians 2:5–7). Stop on the word *grasped.* The hymn says Jesus did not grasp at the highest thing in the universe — his own equality with God — but let it go, emptied himself, went down. The whole motion of the gospel is downward. God did not climb the mountain to seize the kingdoms; God came down the mountain to die for them. And the result, Paul says, is that God exalted him and gave him the name above every name — the kingdoms, in other words, the very thing offered in the wilderness — *because* he refused to grasp, and went to the cross instead. The crown came by the tree. There was no other road, and the road through the mountain was always a road away from the crown, not toward it.

Jesus made the same point to his own grasping disciples, who had been arguing about who would sit at his right hand — which is to say, arguing about political power, which is the argument the church never stops having. *You know that those who are considered rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their great ones exercise authority over them. But it shall not be so among you* (Mark 10:42–43). Read that as the policy statement it is. He is not describing a different way to use power. He is describing a different thing altogether, the inversion of power — *whoever would be great among you must be your servant* — and he grounds it in himself: *the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many* (Mark 10:45). The kingdoms of the world run on lording-over. The kingdom of God runs on laying-down. **You cannot serve a kingdom that lays itself down by seizing the levers of a kingdom that lords it over. The two run in opposite directions, and the church that grasps the second has, in that instant, stopped advancing the first.**

## Resident aliens, and the colony that forgot it was one

Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon named our particular American failure with a precision that still stings.

In *Resident Aliens* in 1989 they argued that the church spent its long Christendom centuries forgetting what it was — a colony, a community of resident aliens whose true citizenship is in another country, whose first political act is not to seize the state but to *be* the church, visibly, in a way the state cannot reproduce. The disaster of American Christianity, they said, is that we came to believe our job was to run the country, to make the nation Christian by making it obey, and so we poured the church's energy into the machinery of the state and let the church itself — the actual community of the Sermon on the Mount — go thin and powerless and indistinguishable from the culture around it. We traded the slow, unglamorous, cross-shaped power of being a peculiar people for the fast, flattering power of the ballot and the bench. We took the mountain. And the strange thing Hauerwas and Willimon point at is that the church got weaker the more political power it won, because the power it won was not its own kind of power, and borrowing the tempter's kind always costs you your own.

Bonhoeffer is the man who proved you could resist the offer and pay for it. He watched the German church do the thing the third temptation describes — fall down before national power and call the worship Christian, drape the cross in the flag, bless the kingdoms in exchange for a place at the table. The German Christians of 1933 did not think of themselves as apostates. They thought of themselves as patriots, as realists, as men finally giving the faith a seat where decisions got made. They had been handed the kingdoms of their world, and they bent the knee, and they were certain the bending was righteousness. Bonhoeffer named it for what it was: not a tactical error but apostasy, the church worshiping on the mountain, the bride of Christ selling her birthright for the thin soup of national influence.

And the cost of refusing it was the cost the gospel always names. He went down. He lost his teaching post, his freedom, and at last his life, hanged at Flossenbürg in April 1945, weeks before the regime he resisted collapsed. He had every chance to take the easier road. He had the credentials, the platform, the pedigree that could have bought him a comfortable place inside the new order. He refused it, and the refusal killed him. He did not take the kingdoms. He took the cross, which is the only way the church has ever actually changed the world, and which is the one road the tempter's offer is forever designed to help you skip. The German church that grasped the kingdom in 1933 is a ruin and a warning. The man who refused it is read in every seminary on earth. That is not an accident. That is the arithmetic of the third temptation, worked all the way out.

## The offer is still on the table

Here is the test, and it is brutal, and I have failed it.

The next time you feel the surge of it — the prospect of a court reshaped, a law passed, an election won, the levers of the kingdoms of the world finally in Christian hands — ask one question. *On whose terms am I being offered this.* Because the offer is real, and the kingdoms are real, and the good you would do with them might even be real. The tempter never offered Jesus something worthless. He offered him the world, and he offered it for a single bend of the knee, and the bend felt, in the moment, like the fastest road to the very kingdom Jesus came to bring.

I have wanted the mountain. I have felt the pull of a Christianity that wins — that stops being the strange, small, cross-shaped colony Hauerwas described and becomes instead a force, a bloc, a power the world has to reckon with. It is an intoxicating thing to want, and it does not feel like the third temptation. It feels like faithfulness. It feels like finally taking the kingdom seriously. That is precisely how the tempter dressed it on the mountain, and precisely how the Grand Inquisitor justified it in his cell, and precisely how the German church explained itself to itself while it draped the cross in the flag.

The church does not change the world by grasping the kingdoms of the world. It never has. It changes the world the way its King did — by refusing the mountain, withdrawing from the crown the crowd would force on it, and going down, all the way down, to the cross that the tempter's whole offer was built to help it avoid.

*All this I will give you,* he said, *if you will fall down and worship me.*

He is still saying it. He says it from a very high mountain, with all the kingdoms spread out below, and he says it to a church that wants them, and the only answer that has ever been the church's to give is the one the King gave first.

We have not been giving it. That is not the world's failure. It is ours.
