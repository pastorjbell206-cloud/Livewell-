You can usually tell within the first ten minutes. Not from the sermon — the sermon is often fine — but from the room. There is a way a congregation laughs at a certain kind of joke, a way it goes warm at a certain name and cold at another, a shared set of assumptions in the air that has nothing to do with the Apostles' Creed and everything to do with how the people in the seats vote. You can walk into a church that has never once endorsed a candidate from the platform and know, by the temperature of the room, exactly which lever every person in it pulls. The endorsement was never necessary. The room had already become a precinct.

This is the quiet thing that happened to a large part of the American church, and it did not happen by anyone deciding it should. No board voted to convert the congregation into a voting bloc. No pastor stood up and announced that the gospel was being merged with a political program. It happened the way most captures happen — slowly, agreeably, one accommodation at a time, until one day the church had a brand, and the brand had a party, and the people who did not fit the brand had quietly stopped coming and nobody noticed the seats were emptier on one side of the aisle.

I am writing as a pastor to pastors and to the people who sit under us, and I am writing the indictment from inside it. **A local church does not get captured by politics when it endorses a candidate. It gets captured when belonging to the tribe and belonging to Christ become the same feeling — when the outsider can no longer tell where the gospel ends and the precinct begins, because the church itself has stopped being able to tell.** Once that line dissolves, the church can keep every word of its doctrinal statement and still have traded its commission for a constituency.

## The capture is reasonable at every step

Be fair to how it happened, because nobody walked into apostasy on purpose. Every step was defensible.

A pastor watches the culture move against things his people hold dear — the unborn, the family, the freedom to believe out loud. These are not invented grievances. The pressure is real, the losses are real, and a shepherd who said nothing while his flock was mocked and legislated against would be a coward, not a saint. So the pastor speaks. Good. Then a movement forms that promises to defend exactly the things he was defending from the pulpit, and the movement is organized, and it wins, and it brings results his prayers had not produced. So he aligns with it. Reasonable. Then the alignment starts paying — the church grows, the giving rises, the people are energized in a way the Bible study never managed — and the pastor notices that politics does something to a room that the gospel had been struggling to do for years. So he gives the room more of what wakes it up.

That is the whole mechanism. There is no villain in it. Each step is the kind of decision a faithful, frightened, overworked pastor makes because the alternative looked like surrender. The capture does not require corruption. It only requires a series of sensible men solving real problems with the tool that happened to be working.

But James Davison Hunter saw where the tool leads. In *To Change the World* (2010) he argued that the church's century-long obsession with seizing cultural and political power had not made the culture more Christian — it had made the church more political, and more bitter, and more defined by what it was against. The will to power, Hunter wrote, leaves a residue, and the residue is *ressentiment* — a settled posture of grievance and resentment that becomes the church's actual mood, no matter what its statement of faith still says. The tool was working. It was just working on the church.

## What it does to the people in the pew

Walk the room the capture builds and count who is missing.

There is the lifelong member who fits the tribe perfectly, and for him church has never felt better — affirming, energized, full of people who see the world exactly as he does. And there is the woman three pews back who believes every line of the creed, loves Jesus with everything she has, and votes differently, and who has learned to keep her mouth shut in the lobby because the last time she said something true and out of step the temperature dropped ten degrees. She is not in a doctrinal crisis. She believes what the church believes about God. She has simply discovered that the church has a second, unwritten statement of faith, and on that one she is a heretic, and there is no membership class that will fix it because nobody will admit the second statement exists.

So she goes quiet. Then she goes occasional. Then she finds a reason that has nothing to do with politics — the music, the schedule, a hard season — and she goes. And the church marks her down as someone who drifted, who was not committed, who could not handle the truth, when what she actually could not handle was being treated as a stranger inside the body that baptized her. **A church that makes the tribe feel at home and the outsider feel watched has not grown stronger. It has just stopped being able to count the people its strength drove out.**

This is what Paul saw coming in the very first generation. The Corinthians had begun to organize themselves around their favorite leaders — *I follow Paul, I follow Apollos, I follow Cephas* — and Paul met it with three of the most violent questions in his letters: *Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Were you baptized in the name of Paul?* (1 Corinthians 1:12–13). He treats the partisan spirit, the sorting of Christians into camps under banners, as a direct assault on the cross itself. Not a personality difference. Not a healthy diversity of emphasis. A tearing of the one body for whom one Lord died. The Corinthian factions were not even political. They were just tribal. Paul still called it a wound in the body of Christ.

And consider what the capture does to discipleship itself, because this is the cost nobody puts on the books. The disciple is supposed to be formed by the slow pressure of the gospel against everything in him that resists it — his greed, his pride, his contempt for the people not in his camp. But in a captured church the gospel and the tribe say the same things, which means the gospel never gets to contradict him. It only ever confirms him. He comes on Sunday and hears that the people he already despised are the problem, that the country he already loved is the city of God, that the convictions he already held are the courageous ones. He leaves more certain and less changed. A church that only ever tells the tribe what the tribe already believes has not discipled anyone. It has flattered them in the name of Jesus, which is the one form of preaching the New Testament never once commends.

## How the pastor got captured

The harder question is what happened to the man at the front, and the answer is older than the present moment.

Robert Bellah named the underlying machinery in 1967, in an essay called "Civil Religion in America." There exists, Bellah argued, a real American religion — with its own scriptures, saints, holy days, and a god generic enough to bless any party — running quietly alongside the Christian faith and constantly available to be confused with it. The danger Bellah saw was not that Americans would abandon Christianity for this civil religion. It was that they would never notice the difference, because the two share so much furniture — the same flag in the sanctuary, the same songs near the Fourth of July, the same word *God* meaning two entirely different things in the same sentence. A pastor can serve the civil religion for years, sincerely, and believe the whole time he is serving Christ, because the civil religion was designed to feel exactly like faith.

There is a dated inflection point where this stopped being a drift and became a strategy. In 1979 the Moral Majority formed, and a generation of pastors were offered something genuinely new: a way to convert the spiritual authority of the pulpit into political weight, at scale, and to be told it was their prophetic duty. The offer was not cynical to the men who took it. It felt like courage. But it established a template that has governed a great deal of American ministry ever since — the pastor as a node in a political machine, the congregation as a turnout operation, the sermon as a tool with a measurable downstream effect on a Tuesday in November. Once a pastor has tasted that kind of consequence, the ordinary work of discipleship — slow, unglamorous, unquantifiable — starts to feel small. Politics offered pastors relevance and reach, and relevance is the most addictive drug the ministry sells.

Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon saw the whole arrangement clearly in *Resident Aliens* (1989), and their diagnosis still cuts. The church's mistake, they argued, was believing its job was to make America work — to be the moral underwriter of the nation, the chaplain to the empire, the institution that keeps the society decent. The church is not called to run the world or to fix the country, they insisted. The church is called to *be the church* — a colony of a different kingdom, a people whose common life is itself the witness. The moment a congregation accepts the assignment of making the nation Christian, it has already accepted the nation's terms, the nation's tools, and the nation's measures of success. And it will win elections and lose its soul, and the two will happen at the same meeting, and the minutes will record only the win.

## What it does to the witness

Now stand outside the church and look back at it, because this is where the real cost lands.

Jesus prayed, the night before he died, for one thing on behalf of every Christian who would ever live: *that they may all be one, so that the world may believe* (John 17:21). He tied the credibility of the entire gospel to the visible unity of his people. The world's ability to believe that the Father sent the Son was staked, by Jesus himself, on whether his followers could be one across the lines that divide everyone else. Read that against the captured church and feel the size of the loss. We took the one thing whose unity was supposed to prove the gospel true to a watching world, and we sorted it by party, and now the watching world looks at the church and sees the same red and blue map it sees everywhere else, with a cross drawn on top. We did not just divide the body. We falsified the evidence Jesus offered for his own resurrection.

Paul put the church's actual job in one image: *we are ambassadors for Christ, God making his appeal through us* (2 Corinthians 5:20). An ambassador represents a kingdom not his own. The instant an ambassador starts representing the host country's politics instead of his own sovereign's, he has stopped being an ambassador and become a defector who kept the title. A church captured by a party is an embassy that took down its own flag and ran up the local one, and still expects to be treated as foreign soil. The world is not fooled. It can read the flag.

And Paul had already abolished the very sorting the captured church runs on. *There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is neither male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus* (Galatians 3:28). The most stubborn human divisions of the ancient world — ethnic, economic, sexual — Paul declared dissolved at the foot of the cross. He did not mean the differences vanished. He meant they lost the power to determine who belonged. A church that lets political tribe do exactly what Paul said the cross had ended — decide who is inside and who is suspect — has not merely added a flaw. It has reinstalled the dividing wall Christ died to tear down, and hung a banner on it.

This is the part the captured church cannot see about itself, and it is the part Hauerwas and Willimon pressed hardest. The church that sets out to win the culture ends up shaped by the culture's logic — the logic of majorities, of winning, of treating the people across the line as opponents to be defeated rather than image-bearers to be loved. You become what you fight by fighting it on its terms. The congregation organized for victory slowly takes on the soul of a campaign: the suspicion, the loyalty tests, the conviction that the other side is not merely wrong but wicked. And then it reads those instincts back into the Bible and calls them discernment. The world did not have to defeat the church. It only had to get the church to adopt its weapons, and the weapons did the rest.

## The verdict, and the question for the man at the front

I have felt the pull myself. There is a version of preaching where you let the room's shared politics carry the emotional weight, where you reach for the applause line you know lands because it flatters what the people already believe about the world, where you let the tribe's enemies stand in for sin so you never have to name the sin in the room. It is easier. It works. And it is a substitution so smooth you can perform it for years and call it boldness. The captured pastor does not feel like a sellout. He feels like a prophet, because the room treats him like one, and the room is the tribe, and the tribe is pleased.

Here is the test for any church, and it is brutal, and most of ours would fail it. Could the person who votes the other way and believes every word of the creed feel fully at home in your church — not tolerated, not endured, but at home? Could she speak in the lobby without the temperature dropping? If the honest answer is no, then your church has a second statement of faith it has never written down, and on that statement Christ is not the only Lord. The precinct is.

A church can keep its doctrine intact and lose everything Jesus prayed it would keep. It can win every election it sets out to win and forfeit the one thing — its unity across the lines that divide the world — that was supposed to prove the gospel true. We did not lose the witness to the secular culture or to the academy or to the people who hate us. We handed it to a party that never asked for our souls, only our turnout, and got both, and we called the trade faithfulness right up until the pews on one side went quiet.

The room is warm. The tribe is pleased. And somewhere in the back, the one Jesus prayed would be made one with us is putting on her coat, and the door is closing behind her, and we are not going to notice she is gone until we stand before the One who counted her, and could not find her, in the body that bears his name.
