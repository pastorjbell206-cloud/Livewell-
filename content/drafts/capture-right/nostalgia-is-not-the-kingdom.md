There is a sound a certain kind of sermon makes when it reaches for the past, and once you have heard it you cannot stop hearing it. It is the sound of grief dressed up as conviction. *We used to pray in schools. We used to honor the Lord. We used to be a Christian nation, and look at us now.* The voice drops. The room leans in. Something holy seems to be at stake, and something holy is at stake — but not the thing the voice thinks. What is at stake is whether the people of God can tell the difference between the kingdom and a memory.

I have made that sound. I have stood in front of a congregation and traded on a past I never lived in, and I have watched it work, and the working of it should have frightened me more than it did.

The claim of this essay is blunt, and the rest of it is me earning the claim. **Nostalgia for a Christian America is not faith. It is idolatry — the same idolatry the progressive commits when he worships the future, only turned to face the other direction.** One man bows to a golden age that is coming. The other bows to a golden age that has passed. Neither is bowing to the God who stands outside the calendar entirely, and both have confused a date with a King.

## The golden age is a myth, and the honest history says so

Begin where it costs my own side the most, because the version of an argument worth refuting is the strong one, and the strong version deserves a fair hearing before it gets answered.

The longing is not stupid. It is not even entirely wrong. There was a time when more Americans went to church, when the Ten Commandments hung in courthouses, when the cultural air carried a residue of Scripture that a person could breathe without trying. A man who remembers a town that closed on Sunday and a school that opened in prayer is not lying about what he saw. The decline he feels is real. The numbers are real. Something has gone out of the public square, and the people who insist nothing has been lost are not telling the truth either.

So grant all of it. And then ask the question the nostalgia cannot survive: *Christian for whom.*

The 1950s that the golden-age sermon mourns — the decade of full pews and family altars and a President who told the nation that faith was the bedrock of freedom — was the same decade in which a Black family could be turned away from that full church at the door. The same courthouse that hung the commandments enforced the color line in front of it. The prayer that opened the school opened a school a Black child could not enter. This is not a hard fact to find. It is the most documented fact in American life. The golden age was golden for some and iron for others, and the people for whom it was iron were sitting in the same country, often in the same pew-less balcony, while the hymns went up.

Mark Noll spent a career forcing evangelicals to look at this. In *The Scandal of the Evangelical Mind* in 1994 he wrote the sentence that named the whole problem — that the scandal of the evangelical mind is that there is not much of an evangelical mind — and what he meant was partly this: that a people formed by sentiment instead of by serious thought will always prefer the flattering memory to the documented record. In *America's God*, his history of the years from Jonathan Edwards to Abraham Lincoln, Noll shows something that should end the golden-age sermon for good. The same biblical literacy that filled those churches was deployed, with total confidence and excellent proof-texts, on both sides of slavery. The defenders of human bondage were not biblical illiterates. They were biblically fluent. They knew the verses. The Christian America we are told to mourn could read its Bible and own its neighbor in the same afternoon and feel no contradiction.

A nation that could not see the image of God in the man it enslaved was not a Christian nation that has since fallen. It was a nation that had not yet begun to reckon with what Christianity says. The golden age was not lost. It was a thing we agreed not to look at too closely.

## Tom Holland's harder gift

Now the turn that complicates my own argument, because honesty cuts both ways and the secularist's version of this story is no truer than the reactionary's.

Tom Holland, who is not writing as a believer, set out in *Dominion* in 2019 to tell the story of how the Christian revolution remade the Western mind, and he arrived somewhere he did not expect. The very instinct that lets a modern person condemn the slaveholding, segregating, exclusionary past — the instinct that says the weak have dignity, that the victim has a claim, that power must answer to mercy — that instinct is not natural to the human animal and is nowhere to be found in the pagan world Christianity displaced. It is Christian to the root. The man who tears down the Christian America of 1955 in the name of justice is swinging a hammer the church forged.

This is the gift Holland hands both sides, and both sides hate it. To the progressive he says: your moral confidence is borrowed Christianity wearing a secular coat. To the reactionary he says: the standard by which you can finally see how badly the old order failed the enslaved and the excluded is itself the working of the gospel inside history, and the gospel was never housed inside your golden age in the first place. The kingdom was at work in 1955, yes — but it was at work in the people pushing against the iron, not in the people defending it. The leaven was rising in the balcony, not in the pulpit that consigned them there.

So when you mourn the lost Christian nation, be careful which part you are mourning. If you mourn the residue of Scripture in the public air, that is a fair grief. If you mourn the *order* — the arrangement, the hierarchy, the world where everyone knew their place — then the thing you are calling Christian is the precise thing the gospel was busy dissolving.

## Scripture has a name for this longing, and it is not flattering

Here is where it stops being history and starts being the Bible, which is the only court that finally matters in this room.

The Preacher of Ecclesiastes saw the golden-age sermon coming three thousand years out and shut it down in a single line. *Do not say, "Why were the former days better than these?" For it is not from wisdom that you ask this* (Ecclesiastes 7:10). Read that slowly, because it is more severe than it sounds. He does not say the question is mistaken. He says it is *unwise* — that the very asking reveals something has gone wrong in the asker. The longing for the former days is not a neutral feeling the Bible is content to leave alone. It is a symptom. It is the heart looking backward because it has lost its nerve to trust God forward.

And there is an older, darker name for it, because Israel had this disease before America was a thought. Three days out of Egypt — three days past the sea splitting open, past the chains falling off, past the most spectacular deliverance in their history — the people of God stood in the wilderness and grieved. Not for the future. For Egypt. *Would that we had died by the hand of the LORD in the land of Egypt, when we sat by the pots of meat and ate bread to the full* (Exodus 16:3). Look at what nostalgia did to memory. Egypt was the house of slavery, the place of the whip and the dead Hebrew babies in the river, and standing in their freedom they remembered it as a place with good food. **Nostalgia is the lie a frightened heart tells about the past in order to refuse the future God is calling it into.** The pots of meat were always attached to chains. The golden age always had a balcony. The longing edits the photograph.

This is why the New Testament keeps setting its face forward and refusing to let the saints look back. Paul, who had more religious past to be nostalgic about than anyone — the right tribe, the right training, the spotless record — counted all of it as loss and said the one thing he did was this: *forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead* (Philippians 3:13). And the Lord himself made it a law of the kingdom. *No one who puts his hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of God* (Luke 9:62). He is describing a man trying to plow a straight furrow while staring over his shoulder. You cannot do it. You will not cut a straight line into the future of God while your eyes are fixed on a past that is half-invented anyway. The reactionary is plowing with his head turned all the way around, and he wonders why the row is crooked.

## The two cities, and the man who wept for Rome

The church has already had this exact argument, at the highest possible volume, with an empire collapsing around it, and we have forgotten that we won.

When Rome fell in 410, the educated pagan world blamed the Christians. You abandoned the old gods, they said, and the old gods abandoned the city — for the better part of a thousand years Rome stood, and within a century of bending its knee to Christ, the barbarians were in the streets. It was the original golden-age argument. *We used to be great. We used to honor the gods. Look at us now.* And many Christians felt the pull of it, because Rome had become, for them too, the order they were born into and loved. The Christian who wept for Rome as though the kingdom itself had fallen with it was the first Christian nationalist, and he was making the mistake we are still making.

Augustine answered him with twenty-two books. *The City of God*, finished around 426, is the longest refusal in Christian literature of the idea that any earthly order — even the best one, even Rome, even the one with crosses on its standards — can carry the weight of the kingdom of God. There are two cities, Augustine said, built by two loves: the earthly city built by love of self reaching to contempt of God, and the City of God built by love of God reaching to contempt of self. They are tangled together in every nation and every human heart, and they will not be sorted out by an empire or an election or a return to a better decade. Rome was never the kingdom. And the Christian who grieved Rome as the kingdom had simply mistaken his two cities — loving the wrong one with the love the other one was owed.

Augustine refused both directions of the idol at once, which is the thing I keep failing to do. He would not let the pagan worship the lost golden age of Rome. And he would not let the triumphalist Christian pretend the new Christian empire was the kingdom arriving. He stood outside the calendar. He pointed the city of man, in every age, to the city it was not, and never could be, and was never meant to be.

## The mirror, and the man holding it

Now the cost, and I am inside it.

I have spent essays warning the Christian left that "the right side of history" is a religion — that to worship the future, to read the trend lines for a vindication that scripture reserves for God, is to install a tribunal where the throne should be. I stand by every word. But the reactionary holding this essay needs to hear the same verdict turned around, because the idol does not care which direction you face when you bow to it.

The progressive worships a future that has not arrived. The reactionary worships a past that never was. **Nostalgia and progressivism are the same idolatry, back to back, each pointing at the other and calling the other one godless.** Both have taken a moment on the calendar and given it the weight that belongs only to the kingdom of God. Both will defend their chosen moment with a devotion they no longer spend on the cross. And both, when you press them, are afraid of the same thing — of a present in which God has not yet vindicated his people, a present in which faith means trusting a hand you cannot see to bend an arc you cannot read.

Robert Bellah saw the machinery underneath all of it. In his work on civil religion he described the way Americans fuse the nation and the faith into a single object of devotion, until the flag and the cross blur and a man can no longer tell which one he is actually kneeling to. The golden-age sermon is civil religion in its grieving mode. It does not ask whether the old order was faithful to Christ. It assumes that because the old order was *ours*, and because it carried the residue of Scripture, it must have been the kingdom — and so its passing must be apostasy rather than mercy. Bellah's point cuts to the bone: the nation became the sacred thing, and the gospel became its chaplain.

I have let the gospel be my nation's chaplain. I have, in weak moments, preached a sermon that mourned a country more than it called for repentance, that grieved a lost cultural Christianity without once asking whether that Christianity had ever been worth the name for the man in the balcony. That is the sin, and it does not feel like sin. It feels like patriotism. It feels like fidelity. It feels, from the inside, exactly like faith — which is how you know it is so dangerous, because the most effective idols are the ones that wear the face of the God they replace.

## The kingdom has never once been behind you

Here is the test, and it is brutal, and I have failed it.

Take the past you are proudest of — the decade, the order, the Christian nation you are sure we have lost. Now go and find the person for whom that past was iron and not gold, and ask him to mourn it with you. Ask the man who sat in the balcony to grieve the church that built the balcony. If your golden age cannot survive the gaze of the people it excluded, then it was never the kingdom. It was your comfort, and you have been calling your comfort holy.

The kingdom of God has never once, in the whole of Scripture, been located behind the people of God. It is always ahead. It comes down out of heaven, John says — it does not get restored out of the archive. The new Jerusalem descends from the future God holds; it is not a renovation of a past we lost. Every backward glance in the Bible is rebuked, and every forward strain is blessed, because the God of the Bible is not the curator of a museum. He is the one who makes all things new, and *new* is a word that points in only one direction.

So mourn what is worth mourning. Grieve the Scripture going out of the public air; that grief is honest. But do not build an altar to a decade. Do not plow with your head turned around. Do not stand in your freedom and weep for the pots of meat, forgetting they were always chained to a wall.

The former days were not better. That is not despair. It is the beginning of wisdom, and the Preacher said so, and the plow is still in your hands, and the field is still in front of you, and the One who walked out of his own grave is not waiting for you in the past.

He has gone on ahead. He always does.
