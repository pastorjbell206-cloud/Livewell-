We have domesticated the prophets. We read them as if they were addressing spiritual conditions — pride, idolatry, faithlessness — with economic descriptions as illustrative background. But that reading inverts the texts. The prophets were addressing economic conditions directly. The spiritual and the economic were not separate categories to them, because the God of Israel was not the God of private religious experience. He was the God of the covenant, and the covenant had economic requirements.

Amos is the sharpest example. He was a farmer — a dresser of sycamore figs, he calls himself in Amos 7:14 — and God called him to go north to Israel during the reign of Jeroboam II, a period of considerable prosperity. From the outside, the prosperity looked like divine blessing. From inside the economy, Amos saw something else: the prosperous class was expanding its holdings by absorbing the land of those who had fallen into debt, selling the poor for the price of a pair of sandals (Amos 2:6), using fraudulent weights in the marketplace (Amos 8:5), and conducting their religious obligations with such efficiency that they were impatient for the Sabbath to end so they could get back to selling (Amos 8:5). The religion and the exploitation were operating simultaneously. That is what made Amos angry — not that Israel had abandoned the temple, but that the temple had been separated from the treatment of the poor.

Amos 5:21-24 is the indictment: *I hate, I despise your religious festivals; your assemblies are a stench to me. Even though you bring me burnt offerings and grain offerings, I will not accept them... But let justice roll on like a river, righteousness like a never-failing stream.* This is not a call to balance worship with social concern. It is a declaration that worship disconnected from economic justice is offensive to God. The festivals continue, the sacrifices continue, and God says he hates them. The condemnation falls on a religious community that has maintained its ritual life while participating in the systematic impoverishment of the vulnerable.

Isaiah 3 and 5 make the same point in different register. The leaders of Jerusalem are indicted for grinding the faces of the poor (Isaiah 3:15). The wealthy who accumulate property — *who add house to house and join field to field till no space is left* (Isaiah 5:8) — are pronounced cursed. This is not a minor concern on the edges of Isaiah's message. It is central to his indictment of the community that calls itself God's people.

Micah 2:1-2 identifies the specific mechanism: those who lie awake at night planning injustice, who covet fields and seize them, who take houses and defraud families of their inheritance. The covetousness and the action are directly connected. The prophetic tradition does not treat economic exploitation as the unfortunate byproduct of individual character failures. It treats it as a systemic pattern, sustained by the cooperation of legal systems, religious institutions, and political arrangements — all of which are subject to the judgment of the God who owns the land.

I have preached through these texts and felt the discomfort they produce in congregations where some members are comfortable and others are not. The discomfort is not a problem to manage. It is the texts doing what they were written to do. The prophets were not polite about this material. Jeremiah 22:13-17 contrasts the king who builds his palace with forced labor against his father Josiah, who — the text says — *defended the cause of the poor and needy, and so all went well.* The summary is explicit: *Is that not what it means to know me? declares the Lord.* To know God is to defend the poor. The economic and the theological are not adjacent concerns. They are the same concern.

The church in America has access to this tradition. The prophets are in the canon. The texts have not changed. What has changed is our willingness to read them for what they actually say.

They say quite a lot. They are waiting to be read.
