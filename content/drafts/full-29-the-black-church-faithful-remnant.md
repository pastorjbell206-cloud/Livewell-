I write this essay as a white pastor who has spent a long time learning what I should have learned earlier. The Black church in America is, by every honest historical measure, the more faithful church. While white American evangelicalism was building Christendom, the Black church was being the church under conditions that should have destroyed it and did not. I am writing this essay because the white American Christians I know need to read what the Black church has been writing all along.

I want to begin with an acknowledgment of where I am writing from, because the acknowledgment matters for what follows. I am a white pastor of a predominantly white congregation in a predominantly white denomination. My theological formation was almost entirely in white evangelical institutions. The Black theological tradition was almost completely absent from my seminary education. The voices I read in the late twentieth century during my first decade of pastoral ministry were almost entirely white. This is not a confession of guilt; this is a confession of incompleteness. The incompleteness was the condition of most white evangelical theological formation in my generation, and it produced pastors and laypeople who could not see what should have been obvious. The seeing has been a slow recovery for me. The recovery is incomplete. The essay is what I can write at this point in the recovery.

## What the Black church held while we lost it

The history is widely available now, in scholarship that has been increasingly accessible over the past three decades. I am summarizing what others have documented in much greater detail. The summary cannot do the work of the careful reading. The summary can point you toward the reading.

The Black church in America emerged under conditions of slavery, in hush harbors where slaves gathered after their plantation work to worship in ways their owners did not control. The earliest theological formation of the Black church was the formation of a community that held the gospel in conditions in which the gospel itself was being weaponized against them by the white masters who preached a curated version of scripture designed to support their economic interests. The Black church read the same scripture and found something different in it — the Exodus, the prophets, the Christ who was killed by the political authorities and raised against them. The early Black church was theologically literate in ways white evangelical institutions of the time were not, because the early Black church was reading scripture under conditions that exposed the costs of mis-reading in ways that the surrounding culture's reading did not have to confront.

The Black church under Jim Crow continued the theological work. Howard Thurman's *Jesus and the Disinherited* (1949) is the foundational text — Thurman's reading of Jesus as a member of an oppressed minority under Roman occupation, addressing the questions that occupied minorities have to ask, in ways that white American theology had been refusing to recognize for two centuries. Thurman's work shaped Martin Luther King Jr. and shaped the civil rights movement and shaped most of what is now called Black liberation theology. The work has been available since 1949. White evangelical institutions have, on the whole, only begun to engage it seriously in the past two decades.

The civil rights movement was the most explicitly Christian political movement in American history. It was led by Black pastors. It was theologically grounded in Black ecclesial tradition. It was funded by Black churches. It was sustained by Black congregations under conditions of explicit persecution, including church bombings and assassinations of leaders. The white evangelical tradition, on the whole, stood at a distance from the movement, with some honorable exceptions, and in many cases stood actively against it. The Southern Baptist Convention, the largest evangelical denomination in America, did not formally apologize for its role in supporting slavery and segregation until 1995. The apology was overdue by roughly a century and a half, and was not, by the most thorough analyses, complete.

The contemporary Black church continues the theological work. The current generation of Black theologians and pastors — Esau McCaulley, Jemar Tisby, Kelly Brown Douglas, Brian Bantum, Christena Cleveland, Charlie Dates — are continuing the tradition that Cone and Thurman and the earlier generations built. The work is rigorous, deeply biblical, prophetically engaged, and largely unknown in white evangelical institutions. The unknowing is the problem.

## What the Black church has held that white evangelicalism has not

I want to name specifically what the Black church has held theologically that the white evangelical tradition has, on the whole, lost or never had.

The Black church has held an honest theology of suffering. Suffering is treated as real, as costly, as worthy of lament, as the condition in which the gospel actually has to be lived. The lament tradition of the Psalms is present in Black worship in ways that have largely been suppressed in white evangelical worship. The white evangelical move toward triumphalist worship — the worship that asserts victory without acknowledging the cost — has not been the move the Black church made. The Black church's worship has held space for both the agony and the hope, in ways that white evangelical worship has often refused.

The Black church has held a prophetic critique of empire. The understanding that the political powers of any given moment are accountable to a higher standard, and can be addressed prophetically rather than capitulated to politically, is structural to the Black tradition. The white evangelical tradition, by contrast, has on the whole accommodated itself to whatever political power was available, including in ways that produced the Christian nationalist absorption I have written about elsewhere in this writing project. The Black tradition has not made this move at the same scale, because the political powers of America have generally not been available to the Black church on the terms the white church has had access to them, and the Black tradition has been forced to develop a more theologically rigorous posture toward political power than the white tradition has needed to.

The Black church has held a worship tradition that took the embodied dignity of all people seriously. The Black ecclesial tradition is more deeply embodied, more deeply communal, and more deeply attentive to the historical and bodily realities of its members than the white evangelical tradition has generally been. The result is a worship that addresses the whole person, not just the cognitive components, and that holds the community together across decades in ways the more individualistic white evangelical tradition has often failed to.

The Black church has held a politics that refused to baptize either Caesar. The Black church has historically refused the conservative move of identifying Christianity with the existing political order, and has also refused the progressive move of subordinating Christian commitments to a secular political project. The Black church has held a third position — a politics shaped by the gospel, attentive to the conditions of the marginalized, rigorously theological, and finally not identified with any political coalition. The third position is what white evangelicalism has been failing to find for the past forty years. The Black church has been holding it the whole time.

## What this means for the white evangelical reader

I am writing this for the white evangelical reader who has been formed in the tradition I have been formed in. The reader will probably feel some resistance to what I am saying. The resistance is part of the dynamic. The resistance is part of what has kept the white evangelical tradition from learning what the Black tradition has been writing.

I want to ask three things of the reader.

First, read the work. Howard Thurman's *Jesus and the Disinherited* (1949) is the foundational text. James Cone's *God of the Oppressed* (1975) is the systematic theological articulation. *The Cross and the Lynching Tree* (2011) is the integration of the cross of Christ with the lynching tradition of American history, and it is one of the more important theological works of the past half century. Esau McCaulley's *Reading While Black* (2020) is the contemporary articulation, written by an evangelical theologian who is in conversation with both traditions. Jemar Tisby's *The Color of Compromise* (2019) is the historical reckoning. Read these books. Read them with the same seriousness you would read any other major theological work. Let them do the work in your understanding that they have been doing in the understanding of Christians who have been engaging them for decades.

Second, sit under Black preaching. If there is a Black church in your area whose theology you find compatible with your own, attend services regularly for a year. Sit under the preaching. Sing the hymns. Notice what is being held theologically that you have not encountered before. The attendance is not a one-time field trip. The attendance is a posture of learning, sustained over time, that white evangelical institutions have rarely modeled. The modeling has to begin somewhere.

Third, repent of the institutional history. The institutional history of white evangelicalism in America includes substantial complicity in slavery, in segregation, in the backlash against the civil rights movement, in the contemporary refusals to engage racial questions honestly. The repentance is not abstract. The repentance is the willingness to read the history honestly, to grant what is true about the failures, and to take responsibility for the institutional inheritance you are part of. The repentance is not paralysis or self-flagellation. The repentance is the simple acknowledgment that the institutions you have been formed by have failed in particular ways, and that those failures are part of why the witness of white evangelicalism is the diminished thing it is now.

## The verdict

The Black church has been doing what we are about to need to learn how to do — be the faithful church under an empire that is not Christian, and may have never been. The Black church figured this out under conditions worse than anything most of us will ever face. We do not need to invent the post-Christendom future. We need to read the people who have already been living it. The reading is overdue.

I want to be honest about my position in this conversation. I am a white pastor writing about the Black tradition for a primarily white audience. The position has obvious limits, and I have tried to acknowledge them throughout. The essay is not a complete account of the Black church; the complete account is in the work of Black theologians and historians, whose work I have pointed you toward. The essay is an invitation to read what the Black tradition has been writing, to recognize the witness it has been giving, and to learn from a tradition that has held the gospel under conditions that have, in significant respects, exposed the white evangelical tradition's accommodations to power and culture.

The post-Christendom church in America will be, in significant respects, multiethnic in its leadership and its theology, drawing on the global church's traditions and on the African American tradition that has been holding the gospel here for four hundred years. The white evangelical institutions that figure out how to participate in this future will be smaller and more humble than the institutions of the past. The white evangelical institutions that refuse to participate will continue to shrink. The choice is being made now, and the choice is being made by particular pastors and lay leaders, in particular institutions, with particular decisions about what they will read, who they will sit under, and what they will repent of.

The Black tradition has been doing the work. The white tradition has been doing different work, much of it less faithful. The learning is what the next generation owes the previous one. The reading is the beginning of the learning. The next essay in this writing project takes up the question of why Gen Z has been leaving the church, and what would bring them back — a question whose answer overlaps with the work the Black tradition has been modeling for four hundred years.
