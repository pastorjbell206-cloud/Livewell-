Open the Bible at random and you can come away with two flatly opposite pictures of God. In one, He is the fixed point, the one thing that does not move while everything else does — "I the LORD do not change" (Malachi 3:6). In the other, He seems as movable as a parent worn down by a long argument: He sees Nineveh repent and "relented of the disaster that he had said he would do to them" (Jonah 3:10); He hears Moses plead and "the LORD relented from the disaster that he had spoken of bringing on his people" (Exodus 32:14). So which is it? Does God change His mind or not? Skeptics treat this as a contradiction caught red-handed. Believers often just look away from it. Both responses miss something worth having.

I missed it for years. As someone who came to faith out of unbelief, I had been trained to hunt for exactly this kind of seam in a text — the place where the story trips over itself — and the God who "repents" looked like an obvious trip. It took me a long time to see that I was reading the seam the wrong way around.

Here is the claim, and I want to make it gently because it is easy to make it coldly. **God does not change His mind the way you and I do — not because He is rigid, but because He is complete. He has no new information, no second thoughts, no improved plan He wishes He had thought of sooner. And precisely because He does not change, the verses where He "relents" mean something far more reassuring than they appear to.** The unchanging God is not the frozen God. He is the only God you could ever actually lean your whole weight on.

## Why an unchanging God sounds cold — and isn't

Let me name the resistance first, because I felt it and you may too.

"God does not change" sounds, on first hearing, like bad news. We want a God who responds — who is moved by our prayers, who can be reached, who is not a marble statue staring past us. An "unchanging" God sounds like an indifferent one, sealed off in His perfection, untouchable. If nothing I do can move Him, why pray? Why love a being who is the same whether I show up or not? The doctrine the theologians call *immutability* — God's unchangeableness — has a public-relations problem, and the problem is that we hear it as *unresponsiveness.*

But run the opposite all the way out and see if you actually want it. A God who changes is a God who can change *for the worse.* A God who has new thoughts is a God who can be surprised — which means He did not see this coming, which means tomorrow may bring something He has not accounted for. A God who can be talked into things can be talked out of them; the love He has for you today is, in principle, revisable. Every quality you love about God — His mercy, His faithfulness, His goodness — is only as reliable as His changelessness. If He can change, then His promise to you is a mood, and moods pass. I wanted a God who would not move. Not because I wanted Him distant, but because I had spent enough time around people whose love was weather, and I could not bet my life on weather again.

So the question is not whether you want a changeable God. The question is what kind of unchangeableness we are talking about — and the tradition is far more careful here than its critics assume.

## Aquinas, Boethius, and the God who is not in time the way we are

Two old thinkers worked this out with a precision worth recovering, and you do not need Latin to follow them.

Thomas Aquinas, in the thirteenth century, reasoned about it this way. To change is to gain something you lacked or lose something you had — to move from potential to actual. A seed changes into a tree; it had the capacity for treeness and now realizes it. But God lacks nothing. He is not on His way to becoming more fully Himself; He is already, completely, all that He is — "pure act," Aquinas called it, with no unrealized potential waiting to be filled in. A being like that cannot change, not because He is stuck, but because there is no improvement available to Him and no diminishment possible. He does not grow wiser, because He was never less than perfectly wise. He does not cool toward you, because His love was never a temperature. Immutability, for Aquinas, is not God's limitation. It is the overflow of His fullness.

Boethius, seven centuries earlier, writing in prison while awaiting his own execution, added the piece that makes the puzzle verses snap into place. We experience our lives as a moving line — one moment, then the next, the past gone and the future not yet here. We are inside time, carried along it. But God, Boethius argued in *The Consolation of Philosophy* (around 524), is not on the timeline. God's relation to time is not "before" and "after" but a single, complete present — what Boethius called the possession of life "all at once and complete," *tota simul.* God does not wait to find out what Nineveh will do and then react. He holds the whole of Nineveh's history — the warning, the repentance, the sparing — in one undivided sight. The change is real, but the change is in Nineveh, not in God. The city moves. The sun does not move because the shadows do.

This is what the New Testament means when it says of God that "with [Him] there is no variation or shadow due to change" (James 1:17). The image is astronomical: stars and planets shift, cast shadows, rise and set. God does not. He is the fixed light by which everything else is seen to move.

## So what is happening when God "relents"?

Now we can return to the troublesome verses, and they stop being an embarrassment and start being a comfort.

When Jonah preaches doom and God "relents," nothing changed in God. What changed was Nineveh. God's character — His settled disposition to judge unrepentant evil and to spare the repentant — is exactly the same before and after. A city walking toward judgment turns around, and the same unchanging God who opposes the unrepentant now, by the very same fixed principle, spares the repentant. From inside time it looks like God changed His mind. From God's vantage it is the steadiest thing imaginable: His response to repentance is utterly reliable, which is the whole reason Jonah was sent to warn them in the first place. The warning was never a prediction of what would certainly happen. It was an invitation that assumed God's unchanging mercy toward anyone who turns.

The theologians have a useful phrase: God is *immutable in His character and His purposes, not static in His relationships.* He genuinely relates to us as the story unfolds — He warns, He responds, He answers prayer — but His relating flows from a center that never wobbles. Think of it less like a man changing his mind and more like the way the unchanging law of gravity produces wildly different outcomes depending on whether you jump or stay on the ground. The law did not change when you jumped. Your relation to it did. Scripture describes God in the language of relationship — "relented," "was moved," "remembered" — because that is the only honest way to describe what it is like to deal with Him from inside time. The language is true. It is just true the way a sunrise is true: real, vivid, exactly what it looks like from here, and not a statement about the sun moving.

This is why prayer is not pointless before an unchanging God. God does not change in response to prayer, but He has, unchangingly and from before the world began, ordained that He will act through the prayers of His people. Your prayer is not new information that updates His plan. Your prayer is part of the plan, woven in from the start. You are not changing God's mind. You are taking the place He always meant you to have in what He was always going to do.

There is a serious objection here, and it deserves a serious hearing rather than a brush-off. Some thoughtful Christians have argued in the last few decades that all of this is too tidy — that the God who "relents" really does adjust, really does respond to a future He left genuinely open, and that the older view turns Him into a statue who only pretends to interact with us. They are reaching for something true: that God's relationship with us is real, not theater. But notice the cost of the trade. If God is genuinely surprised by Nineveh — if He did not know until the cloth and ashes whether the city would turn — then He is, in the most literal sense, learning. And a God who is still learning is a God who does not yet know how your story ends, which means the comfort He offers is only as good as His current guess. The older view is not colder than this. It is warmer. It says God's interaction with you is real *and* that He already knows, holds, and has accounted for everything you are walking toward, including the parts you cannot see. He is not improvising His love as new facts arrive. He committed to you with His eyes fully open, having already seen the whole of you, the worst of you, the end of you — and loved you anyway.

## The mountain you can actually stand on

Let me put the whole thing where it lands, which is not in the seminar room but in the worst night of your life.

The reason this doctrine matters has nothing to do with winning arguments and everything to do with what you reach for when the ground goes. Malachi gives the verse a context we usually drop: "For I the LORD do not change; therefore you, O children of Jacob, are not consumed" (Malachi 3:6). Read the "therefore." The reason a faithless people has not been wiped out is that God's commitment to them does not rise and fall with their performance. His covenant love is not responsive to their merit; it is anchored in His own unchanging character. They are spared not because they are lovable but because He is constant. That is the only kind of love that can hold a person who knows what they actually are.

I have sat with enough people in collapse to know that what fails them is rarely their theology of God's existence. What fails them is their suspicion that His care is conditional and revisable — that He loved them last year, when they were doing better, and may have cooled since. The doctrine of immutability is the sledgehammer that breaks that suspicion. The God who does not change cannot love you more on your good days or less on your worst, because His love was never a reaction to you in the first place. It is older than you. It does not update. It will be exactly what it is today on the day you die and the day after.

The skeptic asks the question as a "gotcha." It turns out to be the most stabilizing thing a frightened person can be told.

## The hardest case — and the deepest comfort

There is one event that seems to break everything I have said, and a newcomer deserves to have it raised rather than hidden, because it is the place where immutability becomes almost unbearably good.

If God does not change, what do we do with the central claim of Christianity — that God became a man? That the eternal Son took on flesh, grew up, got tired, wept at a grave, and died? That looks like the largest change imaginable: from infinite to infant, from beyond time to bleeding inside it. The church wrestled with this for centuries and arrived at something careful and strange. In the incarnation, God did not stop being God or trade His unchanging nature for a changeable one. The eternal Son took *to* Himself a full human nature — added it, entered it, lived a real and limited human life within it — without subtracting anything from who He eternally is. The fixed point did not move. The fixed point came near. And here is what that means for you. The unchanging God did not stay safely above our changing, suffering world, observing it from a distance the way Boethius's watchman observes the road. He came down onto the road. He took the changing, the tiring, the grieving, the dying into Himself, while remaining the One who does not change — which is the only way the steadiness could reach us where we actually are. An immutable God who stayed remote would be solid but useless to a drowning man. An immutable God who came all the way down, without ceasing to be the rock, is solid ground walking out onto the water to where you are going under. That is not a God who changed His mind about us. That is the unchanging mind, made flesh.

## What remains

So I will leave the contradiction standing where it belongs — resolved, but not flattened.

God does not change His mind, and God relents. Both are true, and holding them together is not a clever trick but a clearer sight: the steadiness is in Him, the movement is in us, and the verses where He "turns" are simply what it looks like, from the inside of time, when an unmoving mercy meets a moving world. The doctrine does not make God colder. It makes Him the one warm thing that will still be warm when everything else has gone out.

Paul, after eleven dense chapters wrestling with the inscrutable ways of God, finally gives up trying to map Him and just worships: "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Romans 11:29). Irrevocable. Not subject to revision. Not a mood He might be talked out of. The same yesterday, and today, and on the day you most need Him to be.

Everything in your life is going to move. The question has only ever been whether anything underneath it does not. The oldest answer is also the steadiest, and it is the only thing I have found that holds.
