> [PERSONAL STORY — James to supply: a time the "God is in control" line was said to you, or by you, and rang hollow against what was actually happening. Drop it in after "I have heard that sentence land wrong."]

Somebody says it at the funeral. *God is in control.* Somebody says it after the diagnosis, after the layoff, after the marriage finally comes apart in the kitchen at eleven at night. It is meant as comfort and it often lands as an insult, because the person hearing it is looking at a piece of reality that does not look controlled by anyone, least of all by a good God. The sentence and the situation will not sit in the same room. And so a lot of people file the doctrine of God's sovereignty under things Christians say that they do not really mean — a verbal tic for hard moments, a way of filling a silence that should have been left alone.

I have heard that sentence land wrong. I have probably said it when I should have said nothing. And I came to faith late, out of unbelief, partly through wrestling with exactly this — because the version of "God is in control" I had been handed as a younger man was so glib, so untouched by how the world actually goes, that rejecting it felt like the only intellectually honest thing to do.

So let me try to say what the doctrine actually claims, because I think we have been defending a caricature of it. **To say God is sovereign — that He is in control — is not to say that everything that happens is good, or that He authored the evil, or that your pain is part of some cheerful plan you would applaud if you could see the spreadsheet. It is to say something both smaller and far larger: that there is no event, including the worst one in your life, that has escaped Him, fallen outside His reach, or defeated His purpose.** That is a harder claim and a stranger comfort than the bumper sticker. It does not ask you to call the wreckage good. It asks you to believe the wreckage is not the end of the sentence.

## Two pictures, both wrong

Begin by clearing away the two cartoons, because most people are choosing between them and both are unworthy of belief.

The first cartoon is the micromanaging puppeteer — a God who personally pulls every string, so that the drunk driver, the tumor, and the collapsing bridge are all His direct, intended handiwork, scripted in advance for reasons He is not sharing. People who hold this picture end up either blaming God for everything or contorting themselves to praise Him for things no decent person would praise. It makes God the author of evil, and Scripture will not allow that: "God cannot be tempted with evil, and he himself tempts no one" (James 1:13).

The second cartoon is the absent landlord — a God who got the universe going, set the rules, and stepped back, so that what happens now happens on its own, indifferent, mechanical, unwatched. This is the God of the deists, and frankly the God a lot of practicing Christians actually believe in without admitting it: present in theory, absent in fact, a being who would not notice if you stopped praying. This picture spares God the blame but at the cost of His care. He is in control the way a retired founder is in control of the company — on the letterhead, not in the building.

It is worth seeing what each cartoon does to a real person in a real crisis, because the choice is not academic. The puppeteer God leaves the grieving mother believing that God personally selected her child's death off a menu of options and willed it for reasons she is forbidden to question — a belief that produces either a crushed silence or a slow, corrosive resentment she is too frightened to name. The absent-landlord God leaves her believing her child died into a void, unwatched, unheld, a random outcome in a machine that would have ground on identically whether she prayed or not. One God she cannot trust because He seems cruel. The other she cannot reach because He is not there. Most people who say they have lost faith in God's sovereignty have actually only lost faith in one of these two cartoons — and they were right to. The tragedy is that no one ever offered them the third thing.

The classical doctrine of providence threads between these, and the threading is the whole point. It claims that God governs all things — actively, continuously, down to the sparrow and the hair — and yet does so without being the author of the evil within them, and without cancelling the real freedom and real causes of the creatures He governs. How both can be true is genuinely hard, and the tradition has never pretended it is easy. But it has insisted that the alternatives — a God who does evil, or a God who does nothing — are both worse, and both false.

## Boethius and the watchman on the hill

An old image helps here, and it comes, again, from a man writing under a death sentence.

Boethius, awaiting execution, was tormented by exactly our question: if God foresees and governs everything, are we just puppets, and is He responsible for the injustice that put me in this cell? In *The Consolation of Philosophy* he reached for a picture. Imagine a man standing on a high hill, watching travelers on the road below. He sees, in a single glance, the one who has already passed and the one still coming. His seeing does not *force* either traveler to walk where they walk — they go freely — but nothing on the road is hidden from him, and nothing surprises him. God, Boethius argued, sees the whole road of history from a height we cannot reach, all of it present to Him at once. His governing it does not turn the travelers into puppets, any more than your watching a man walk makes him walk. Real freedom, real second causes, real human choices — and over all of it, a comprehensive sight and a settled purpose that nothing on the road can derail.

Thomas Aquinas sharpened this into a distinction the church has leaned on ever since: the difference between primary and secondary causes. God is the primary cause — the One who upholds and governs all of existence — but He ordinarily works through secondary causes, the real activity of real creatures. The farmer plants, the rain falls, the wound heals, the criminal chooses; these are genuine causes, genuinely doing the causing. God's sovereignty does not replace them. It works *through* them, without overriding their nature. Which means God can be utterly in control of a world in which human beings make free and even wicked choices, because His control operates at a level beneath and around theirs, not in competition with it. This is why Scripture can say in one breath, "The heart of man plans his way, but the LORD establishes his steps" (Proverbs 16:9). Both are true. You really plan. He really establishes. Neither cancels the other.

## What "in control" does not mean

Now the part that the funeral sentence gets so badly wrong, and that needs to be said clearly.

Sovereignty does not mean every event is good. The Bible is full of events God flatly hates — and over which He is nonetheless sovereign. The clearest case is the one at the center of the whole faith. The crucifixion of Jesus was, by any moral measure, the single worst thing that ever happened: the torture and judicial murder of the only innocent man who ever lived, carried out by cowardice, envy, and imperial cruelty. And Peter, preaching about it, says both things at once without flinching: Jesus was "delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God," *and*, in the same sentence, "you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men" (Acts 2:23). The murder was real wickedness, fully chargeable to the men who did it. And it was, at the same time, the hinge of God's plan to save the world. God did not do the evil. He was not absent from it either. He was sovereign over it — bending the worst act in human history toward the rescue of everyone who would ever trust Him.

That is the pattern, and Joseph names it at the end of Genesis after his brothers had sold him into slavery and ruined a decade of his life: "As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive" (Genesis 50:20). Look at the precision of it. He does not say the evil was secretly good. He does not let his brothers off the hook — *you meant evil.* He says two distinct intentions ran through the same event: their evil intention, and God's good one, and God's was the one that finally governed the outcome. Sovereignty does not sand the evil down into something acceptable. It refuses to let the evil have the only say.

This is what the most quoted and most abused verse in the Bible actually means. "We know that for those who love God all things work together for good" (Romans 8:28) does not say all things *are* good — Paul, who wrote it, was beaten, imprisoned, and eventually executed. It does not say you will see the good, or like the road to it, or that the suffering was a blessing in a clever disguise. It says God is at work, in and through and around even the worst things, weaving them toward a good He has set His heart on and will not abandon. The promise is not that the cancer is good. The promise is that the cancer has not slipped God's grip.

## "Then why do anything?"

There is a fear that always rides in behind this doctrine, and it is worth meeting head on, because it has quietly hollowed out a lot of believers. If God is in control of everything, the reasoning goes, then nothing I do matters — my effort, my prayer, my choices are decorations on a script already written, and the honest response is to go limp and let the machine run. People call this fatalism, and they usually blame the doctrine of providence for it. The blame is misplaced. Fatalism says the end is fixed *regardless* of what you do — you will arrive at the same place whether you act or not, so why act. Providence says something almost opposite: God ordains the ends *and the means to them*, and your action is one of the means. The farmer who knows God gives the harvest does not therefore refuse to plant; he plants precisely because that is how God ordinarily gives harvests. Paul, who believed in God's sovereignty as fiercely as anyone who ever wrote, is the same man who said he worked harder than all the others — "though it was not I, but the grace of God that is with me" (1 Corinthians 15:10). Both clauses. He worked. The grace did the working through him. Your striving is not a futile gesture against a fixed wall. It is the appointed channel through which a sovereign God has chosen to do part of what He is doing. To go limp is not to trust providence. It is to opt out of the very means by which providence works.

## Calvin and the difference it makes at three in the morning

John Calvin, whatever you have heard about him, wrote about this not as a cold logician but as a pastor trying to get terrified people through the night.

In the *Institutes* (1559) Calvin argued that the doctrine of providence is, of all doctrines, the most *practical* — and that its purpose is the relief of fear. A person without it, he said, lives at the mercy of a thousand random terrors: every illness, every accident, every turn of fortune is a loose stone that might fall on them from a meaningless sky. They are, in Calvin's phrase, "miserably disquieted." But the person who knows that nothing reaches them except through the hand of a Father — that the same God who counts the sparrows has not stopped counting them where you are concerned — that person can walk through the same dangerous world with a steadiness the first person cannot. Not because the dangers are smaller. Because the dangers are not unsupervised. Calvin's God is not the puppeteer and not the absent landlord. He is the Father into whose governance even the falling stone must come, and who has already shown, at a cross, exactly what He is willing to do with the worst that the world can throw.

This is why Paul can pile the language as high as he does — that God "works all things according to the counsel of his will" (Ephesians 1:11) — and mean it not as a cold sovereignty but as a warm one. *All things.* Not some things, the nice ones. All of it, including the part you are in right now, the part that does not look governed by anyone who loves you. The doctrine does not ask you to feel that it is controlled. It asks you to believe that it is, on the evidence of a God who proved at Calvary that He does His best work in exactly the places that look most abandoned.

## What remains

So I will not tell you it feels like God is in control, because often it does not, and a faith that needs the feeling will not survive the first hospital waiting room.

The honest thing is this. The doctrine of providence was never a promise that the world would look governed. It was a promise that it is — that beneath a reality which often looks like chaos, and sometimes looks like cruelty, there runs a purpose that the chaos has not interrupted and the cruelty cannot finally win. It does not require you to call your suffering good. It requires only that you consider the possibility that your suffering, real and hateful and uninvited, is nonetheless held — that it has not escaped Him, that He has not looked away, and that the same hands that let the worst thing happen at Golgotha were, the whole time, doing the deepest thing.

You will not always feel the control. You were never promised the feeling. You were shown a cross, and told that the sentence your life seems to be is not finished — that the One who turned that Friday into the rescue of the world is still writing, and that the part that looks like the end is not where He puts the period.

It does not feel like it. It rarely does. And the empty tomb did not feel like it either, the morning before they found it open.
