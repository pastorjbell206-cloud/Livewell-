The word has gone soft on us. "Holy" now means something close to "extra nice." A holy person is a kind person with good habits. A holy place is a quiet one with nice light. We have flattened the word into a moral compliment, the top rung of the ladder we are all climbing — God is good, and very good, and then so good He is holy, the best version of the things we already approve of. On that reading, holiness is just goodness with the volume up.

That reading is wrong, and I think it is one of the reasons God has become hard for modern people to take seriously. We have made Him a slightly improved member of our own category — like us, only better. And a being who is merely a better version of us is not worth falling on your face for. He is worth admiring, maybe. Not worshiping.

So here is the claim I want to make, and it is a strange one. **To say God is holy is not first to say He is morally excellent. It is to say He is *other* — of a different kind, a different order, not the top of our scale but off the scale entirely. Holiness is the attribute that makes God God, and it is the one we have most thoroughly misunderstood.** When you recover it, everything else about Him — His love, His wrath, His mercy — stops being sentimental and starts being real.

## The word means "cut off," "set apart"

Begin with the bare meaning of the word, because it points away from where we expect.

The Hebrew root behind "holy," *qadosh*, carries the sense of being separate, set apart, cut off from the common. It is not primarily a moral word. It is a category word. To call something holy is to say it does not belong to the ordinary run of things — it has been removed from common use and reserved to God. The Sabbath is holy because it is cut out of the week and given to Him. The ground around the burning bush is holy not because the dirt is virtuous but because God is there, and His presence changes the nature of the place. Moses is told to take off his sandals not for hygiene but because the territory has been transferred to a different jurisdiction.

When the word is applied to God Himself, it means He is in a category with no other members. He is not the greatest being among beings. He is the source and ground of being, related to everything but comparable to nothing. The old theologians had a phrase for this: God is *sui generis*, of His own kind. Leviticus puts it as a command rooted in His nature — "You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" (Leviticus 11:44) — and the logic is not "be as nice as I am" but "be set apart, because I am set apart." Holiness in us is derivative; it is participation in something that originates wholly in Him.

This is why the encounters with God's holiness in Scripture are never cozy. You do not meet the holy and feel affirmed. You meet it and feel the floor give way.

## The thing Isaiah could not survive

The clearest window into this is the vision that broke a prophet, and it repays slowing down.

In the year King Uzziah died — and note that Isaiah dates it, an actual year, a real death, a nation in political grief — the prophet sees the LORD seated on a throne, high and lifted up, the train of His robe filling the temple. Around Him are seraphim, burning ones, and even they cannot look. With two wings they cover their faces. With two they cover their feet. And they call to one another the only thing there is to say: "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory" (Isaiah 6:1–3). Hebrew has no comparative or superlative the way English does; you do not say "holier, holiest." You repeat the word for emphasis. To say something twice is intense. The seraphim say it three times. It is the only attribute of God lifted to that pitch anywhere in Scripture. We are never told God is "love, love, love" or "mercy, mercy, mercy." We are told, three times over, by creatures who cannot bear to look, that He is holy.

And Isaiah's response is not delight. It is collapse. "Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts" (Isaiah 6:5). This is a good man. A prophet. By every social measure the most religious person in the room. And in the presence of the holy he does not feel righteous. He feels *undone* — the word means to come apart, to disintegrate. Proximity to holiness did not flatter his goodness. It dissolved his confidence in it. The habit Jesus would later name — measuring yourself against the people around you and coming out fine — works only until the real standard walks into the room.

We have all felt the edge of this, even those of us with no religion at all. There are experiences that overrun our categories — that leave us small and silent and faintly afraid, not because anything threatened us but because we brushed against something vast. We reach for words like "awesome" and they fail. The reaching is the point.

## Rudolf Otto and the feeling you cannot place

A German scholar gave that feeling a name in 1917, and it remains the most useful thing written on the subject.

Rudolf Otto, in *The Idea of the Holy* (Das Heilige), argued that at the root of all real religion is an encounter that is not moral and not rational but something prior to both. He coined a word for it — the *numinous*, from the Latin *numen*, divine presence. The numinous, Otto said, presents itself to us as the *mysterium tremendum et fascinans*: a mystery that makes us tremble and yet draws us in. Tremendum — it produces awe, dread, the sense of being utterly small before something utterly great. And fascinans — at the same time it fascinates, magnetizes, makes us unable to look away. The holy repels and attracts in the same instant. You want to flee and you cannot leave.

Otto's insight was that this experience cannot be reduced to ethics. We treat holiness as super-goodness because goodness is a category we can handle. But the trembling that the seraphim feel, that Isaiah feels, that Moses felt before the bush, is not a reaction to God's good behavior. It is a reaction to His sheer *otherness* — what Otto called the "wholly other," that which is not us, not nature, not anything in the system of things we can manage. Modern people, he thought, had lost contact with this almost entirely. We kept the moral residue of religion — be kind, be good — and lost the dread that gave it weight. We kept the ethics and fired the numen. And a religion that can no longer make you tremble has nothing left to offer the part of you that already, in your better moments, knows you are not the largest thing in the universe.

## Why we stopped trembling

Otto thought modern people had lost contact with the numinous, and it is worth tracing how, because the loss did not happen all at once or by decision.

For most of human history the world was assumed to be charged with presence — a place where the holy could break through at any point, where rivers and mountains and thresholds carried weight, where you took your shoes off because you never knew whose ground you were on. The philosopher Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), described the long process by which the modern West emptied that world out — what he called "disenchantment." We came to experience reality as flat, natural, fully explainable, a closed system with nothing pressing in from beyond it. The rivers became water and the mountains became geology and the thresholds became doors. And a flattened world produces a flattened God: not the wholly other before whom the seraphim hide their faces, but a manageable presence inside our system, available on request, useful, safe. We did not argue ourselves out of the numinous. We slowly built a world with no room in it for the kind of encounter Isaiah had, and then we were surprised that our worship felt thin. You cannot tremble before a God you have already fit inside your categories. The trembling requires a God who exceeds them — and we had spent three centuries making sure ours did not. I came to faith out of exactly that flattened world, assuming any God worth the name would be one I could examine and assess and approve, never imagining the One actually there might be the kind that undoes the examiner.

## Why holiness is the bad news before it is the good news

Now the move that the soft version of the word can never make, and it is the necessary one.

If God is holy in this sense — pure, set apart, of an order in which evil simply cannot coexist — then the problem is not that we are imperfect. The problem is that we are incompatible. Habakkuk, wrestling with God over injustice, says it flatly: "You who are of purer eyes than to see evil and cannot look at wrong" (Habakkuk 1:13). That is not a God we can stroll up to and shake hands with. The reason the encounters in Scripture begin with terror is that holiness, meeting unholiness, is combustible. The seraphim cover their faces. Isaiah comes apart. This is the bad news the cartoon God never delivers, because the cartoon God is just us with better PR — of course we can approach him; he is one of us.

R. C. Sproul built his whole life's teaching around this point. In *The Holiness of God* (1985) he argued that the central modern error about God is precisely the loss of His holiness — that we have made Him "a celestial bellhop," available, manageable, there to meet our needs. We are scandalized, Sproul observed, by stories where God strikes someone down for mishandling holy things, and our scandal is itself the evidence of the problem: we assume God's holiness is no big deal, that of course He should overlook our casual approach, because we have quietly assumed we belong in His presence as we are. We do not. The wonder of the Bible, Sproul said, is never that God judges sin. The wonder is that He lets any of us live in His presence at all. Holiness reframes the whole question. We spend our lives asking why God allows suffering. The deeper question, once you have seen the throne, is why He allows *us.*

And that is exactly where Isaiah's story turns — not by Isaiah cleaning himself up, which he cannot do, but by holiness coming down to make contact possible.

## The coal from the altar

Watch what God does, because it is the pattern of everything that comes later.

Isaiah, undone, makes no move to fix himself — he cannot; that is the whole crisis. Instead, one of the burning ones flies to him with a coal taken from the altar and touches it to his lips, the very lips he had confessed were unclean. "Behold, this has touched your lips; your guilt is taken away, and your sin atoned for" (Isaiah 6:7). The holiness that undid him is the same holiness that now cleanses him — but it had to descend to do it. Isaiah did not climb up to the throne. The coal came down from the altar. The initiative is entirely God's, and it costs something, and it makes the unclean fit to stand where he could not stand a moment before.

The book of Revelation shows us the same throne room Isaiah saw, the same ceaseless cry — "Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord God Almighty, who was and is and is to come" (Revelation 4:8) — and the living creatures still cannot stop. But by then we know what stands at the center of that throne: a Lamb, looking as though it had been slain. The coal from the altar has become a cross. The holiness that no unclean thing could approach has, at appalling cost to itself, made a way for the unclean to come home. This is why holiness is the good news after it is the bad news. A God who was merely nice could never have undone Isaiah. But a God who was merely terrifying could never have sent the coal.

## What remains

So I will not try to make this comfortable, because the discomfort is the gift.

We have spent a long time domesticating God — trimming Him down to a size we can fit in our lives, a supportive presence, a better version of our own values. It has not made Him more believable. It has made Him forgettable. A God you can fully understand and easily approach is a God you made, and you will eventually notice you made Him and put Him away with the other things you outgrew. That is, I think, exactly what has happened to a great many people who left. They did not reject the holy God. They rejected the bellhop and called it disbelief.

The God who is actually there is holy — other, set apart, of a kind with no second member, before whom the burning ones hide their faces and a righteous man comes apart at the seams. To meet Him is the most disorienting thing that can happen to a person, and the soul was built for exactly that disorientation. We were not made to admire God. We were made to be undone by Him, and then, against all expectation, to be touched by the coal and sent back out able to stand.

The seraphim are still saying it. Three times, because twice is not enough. And it is the one thing about God we most need to hear and least want to.
