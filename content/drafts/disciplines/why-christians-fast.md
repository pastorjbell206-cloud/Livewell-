There is a question worth sitting with before anything else. When was the last time you wanted something and did not get it — not because you couldn't, but because you chose not to? For most of us the honest answer is that we cannot remember. We live inside an arrangement no previous generation of human beings ever knew, in which nearly every appetite can be satisfied in minutes. Hungry: the food arrives. Bored: the screen lights up. Lonely, restless, vaguely dissatisfied: there is a tab for that, a feed for that, a purchase for that, a notification already waiting. We have built a world with the friction sanded off every desire, and we have come to experience the gap between wanting and having as a kind of malfunction, a problem to be solved as fast as possible.

I had never been hungry on purpose. It had genuinely never occurred to me that I might choose to want something and sit inside the wanting without reaching for the cure. When I finally tried it, the first thing I learned was not about God. It was about myself, and it was not flattering. I discovered how much of my ordinary day was a low, constant management of small appetites — how often I reached for food I did not need, the phone I did not need, the small hit of relief I did not need — and how rarely, in all my praying, I had ever let one of those appetites go unanswered long enough to ask what was underneath it.

That is what fasting is for, and it is why the church practiced it for two thousand years before we quietly let it slip away. **Fasting is not primarily about food. It is voluntary hunger, deliberately chosen, that exposes how thoroughly our desires have come to run us — and that re-orders those desires around the only hunger that was ever meant to be ultimate.** The church fasted because the saints understood something we have forgotten: that a soul which has never been told *no* by its owner will be told *yes* by everything else.

## Jesus assumed you would do it

Begin with the words of Jesus, because they settle the question of whether this is optional faster than any argument could.

In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus teaches his followers how to fast, and notice exactly how he frames it: "When you fast, do not look gloomy like the hypocrites... But when you fast, anoint your head and wash your face, that your fasting may not be seen by others but by your Father who is in secret" (Matthew 6:16–18). *When* you fast. Not *if*. He does not argue for the practice. He assumes it, the way he assumes you will pray and you will give, placing all three side by side as the ordinary furniture of a life with God. Somewhere between his assumption and our reality, an entire spiritual discipline went missing, and most modern Christians have never once been taught to do the thing Jesus simply took for granted his disciples would do.

He had done it himself. Before his public ministry began, the Spirit drove Jesus into the wilderness, where he fasted forty days, and at the end of it, starving, he met the tempter. The first temptation was the obvious one: turn these stones into bread. And Jesus answered, "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God" (Matthew 4:1–4). That sentence is the whole theology of fasting in a single line. He is not despising bread. He is locating it. Bread is real and good and necessary, and it is not the deepest thing, and the only way to learn that in your body rather than merely agreeing to it in your head is to go without bread long enough to feel which hunger is actually running the show.

The prophet Joel called a whole nation to it. "Yet even now," declares the Lord through Joel, "return to me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning" (Joel 2:12). Fasting in Scripture is the body's way of telling the truth about the soul's condition — a grief too deep for words, a repentance too serious for a casual prayer, a need for God too urgent to keep eating as though everything were fine. The empty stomach becomes a prayer the whole person prays.

## What hunger does that comfort cannot

Richard Foster reopened this door for a generation of Protestants who had forgotten the room was even there.

When Foster published *Celebration of Discipline* in 1978, fasting had become so rare in much of the Western church that he had to argue, to readers who considered themselves serious Christians, that it was a normal part of the faith at all. His case was not built on grim asceticism. It was built on what fasting reveals. Foster wrote that more than any other discipline, fasting brings to the surface the things that control us — that when we deny the body its routine comforts, what comes up is not hunger so much as everything we had been using food and ease to keep buried. The anger we suppress with a snack. The anxiety we mute with a meal. The restlessness we feed with one more thing in the mouth or the hand. **Fasting does not create those disorders. It uncovers them — and you cannot heal what you will not let yourself feel.** A man who has never been hungry has never met the part of himself that food was managing.

Dallas Willard, who thought as carefully about the formation of the soul as anyone in the last century, pressed the point further. Willard argued that the spiritual disciplines, fasting chief among them, work by training — not by trying. You cannot decide, in the moment of temptation, to suddenly become a person whose desires are rightly ordered, any more than you can decide, in the moment, to suddenly run a marathon. The capacity has to be built ahead of time, in practice, when nothing is on the line. Willard's image was the athlete: nobody admires the runner for "trying hard" during the race; the race is won in the months of training nobody saw. Fasting is training. It is the discipline by which a person learns, in the low-stakes laboratory of a skipped meal, that an unmet desire is survivable — so that when a high-stakes desire comes calling, a desire that could wreck a marriage or a vocation or a soul, the muscle of refusal is already there.

The desert fathers knew this in their bones. Those strange, severe Christians who fled to the Egyptian wilderness in the third and fourth centuries fasted not because they hated the body but because they had watched what the unrestrained body did to the soul. They understood appetite as a thing that grows by feeding and shrinks by discipline, and they treated the stomach as a kind of training ground for the whole inner life. Master the smallest appetite, they taught, and you gain a foothold against the largest. Feed every appetite the instant it speaks, and you raise a household of tyrants. We have spent fifty years feeding every appetite the instant it speaks. We should not be surprised at the household we have raised.

What the fathers grasped, and what we have lost, is that the appetites are connected. The man who cannot say no to a second helping is not, in the end, a different man from the one who cannot say no to the affair, or the spending, or the rage, or the screen at two in the morning. The capacity for self-governance is one capacity, exercised across a thousand small surrenders or refusals, and it is built or eroded in the places no one is watching — at the refrigerator, at the table, in the ordinary moments when a desire speaks and a person decides whether he or his appetite is in charge. The fathers fasted the small appetite precisely because they knew the large ones could not be fought head-on in the heat of temptation. You do not learn to resist a wildfire by waiting for the wildfire. You learn it by tending the small flames, daily, until refusal is no longer a heroic act but a settled habit of the soul. This is why fasting was never a freestanding spiritual stunt for them. It was the foundational training on which the rest of the moral life was built.

## The fast God actually wants

Here is the warning Scripture itself sets right in the middle of the practice, and it is aimed straight at people like me.

Isaiah 58 is one of the most uncomfortable chapters in the prophets, because it is God's rejection of fasting — fasting done by people who were technically doing it right. They fasted, and they complained that God had not noticed. And God's answer, through Isaiah, is withering: you fast only to quarrel and to fight, you humble yourselves for a day and call it righteousness, while the people who work for you go unpaid and the hungry go unfed at your gate. "Is not this the fast that I choose," God asks, "to loose the bonds of wickedness... to share your bread with the hungry and bring the homeless poor into your house?" (Isaiah 58:6–7). The fast God wants is not a private spiritual technique that leaves the world exactly as it was. The hunger you take on is meant to make you feel the hunger of people who did not choose theirs.

This is the correction the discipline needs, because a fast can become just another self-improvement project — a spiritual diet, a way to feel disciplined and impressive and a little superior to the people still eating. Jesus saw that coming, which is why he told us to wash our faces and tell no one. **A fast that makes you more focused on yourself has failed, no matter how many meals you skipped. A fast that opens your hands to the hungry has begun to work.** The emptiness in your own stomach is supposed to be a door, and on the other side of it is your neighbor.

There is a logic to that connection worth sitting with, because it is not arbitrary. The reason a culture of total satisfaction breeds indifference to the poor is that satisfied people lose the capacity to imagine want. When you have never been truly hungry, hunger becomes an abstraction, a statistic, a thing that happens to other people in other places, easily acknowledged and just as easily forgotten over dinner. The fast collapses that distance. For a day, the gnawing in your own gut is the same gnawing the hungry man knows every day with no choice in the matter and no certainty of relief at sundown. You cannot fast seriously and remain comfortable in your indifference, because the body will not let you. The discipline that empties the stomach also, if you let it, enlarges the heart, and Isaiah's God knew it, which is why he tied the fast he chooses to the loosing of chains and the feeding of the poor. The two were never separate practices. They were the same compassion, learned first in your own body and then carried out the door to someone else's.

## Why we stopped, and what it cost

So why did the practice nearly vanish from ordinary Christian life? The reasons are worth naming, because each one is still operating on us.

Part of it was the same Protestant recoil that emptied so many rooms in the house — a fear that fasting was a way of earning God's favor, of climbing toward heaven on the strength of our own self-denial. That fear is not groundless; fasting can curdle into exactly that. But the cure for a discipline that can be abused is not to abolish it. We do not stop praying because prayer can become showing off. We learn to do it rightly. Somewhere the church forgot the difference between rejecting a distortion and rejecting the thing itself, and a whole tradition of formative practice went out with the dishwater.

And part of it, the deeper part, is simply that we no longer live in a world that makes fasting legible. To a culture that treats the satisfaction of every desire as the definition of the good life, voluntary hunger looks not holy but pathological — a problem, a disorder, something to be talked out of. We have so thoroughly accepted the premise that wanting-and-having should be instant and frictionless that a person choosing the gap on purpose seems to be malfunctioning. But the gap is where the formation happens. The gap is where you find out what you are made of and what you have been feeding. A people who have eliminated every gap have eliminated, without noticing, the very space in which the soul learns to want the right things.

There is one more reason, and it is the hardest to admit, because it implicates me as much as anyone. We stopped fasting because we did not, in our bones, believe we needed it. Fasting is for people who know their appetites are out of order and want them set right, and the quiet assumption of comfortable Christianity is that our appetites are basically fine — a little indulgent, maybe, but nothing a fast is required to address. That assumption is the disease itself. The reason the saints fasted with such seriousness is that they took the disorder of the human heart with a seriousness we have largely abandoned. They did not think a soul drifted toward God on its own; they thought it had to be trained, disciplined, sometimes starved into surrender, because left to itself it would organize its whole life around the satisfaction of appetites that could never finally satisfy. We have softened that conviction into something gentler and more flattering, and the softening is precisely why the practice died. You do not take medicine for a sickness you have decided you do not have.

You probably stopped fasting, if you ever started, for one of these reasons or for the most ordinary reason of all: nobody ever taught you, and the culture made the practice unthinkable, and so the muscle simply atrophied from disuse. The recovery is not complicated. Begin small — a single meal, an afternoon, a day, with water and with prayer in the hours you would have spent eating. Pay attention to what comes up, because something will, and it will tell you the truth about yourself. And aim the hunger outward, toward the God who is the only thing that finally fills and toward the neighbor whose hunger is not a choice. Do that, and you will rediscover what the saints never forgot: that a little chosen emptiness is one of the few places left where a person can hear, under the roar of all his satisfied appetites, the quieter and deeper hunger he was made for, and the One who alone can meet it.
