There is a pastoral failure we do not discuss, because discussing it is uncomfortable, and because the person most likely to leave when it becomes uncomfortable is also the person most likely to have funded the building.

I have committed this failure. I am still capable of it.

The wealthy congregant is not a problem to manage. He is a soul for whom Christ died, carrying the particular burden that Jesus named directly and without softening in Mark 10:23 — "How hard it is for the rich to enter the kingdom of God." The disciples were astonished. They had assumed the opposite. Wealth looked like favor. It looked like evidence of righteousness. Jesus was not gentle in correcting them. He pressed: harder than a camel through the eye of a needle. The disciples asked each other who could possibly be saved. That is the right response. That is the response that takes the text seriously.

Most of us do not take it seriously. We take the wealthy member seriously — as a donor, as a board member, as a person whose opinion carries weight in elder meetings and capital campaigns — and we call that pastoral care. It is not pastoral care. It is institutional management.

## What care actually requires

The steel-man case for the approach we take is this: wealthy congregants are humans with real griefs, real marriages, real anxieties, real fear of death. They deserve pastoral presence in those moments. The CEO sitting across from me at the hospital bed of his dying mother is not his balance sheet. He is a son who is about to lose his mother, and he needs the same word of the Lord that anyone needs. That is not wrong. That is right.

But pastoral care is not only crisis response. It is formation. It is the long, patient work of shaping a soul toward the kingdom. And the kingdom, as Jesus described it, makes specific demands on the accumulation of wealth that pastoral care must eventually name. Not once. Not as a disclaimer. As a recurring, patient, self-implicating conversation.

Amos did not comfort the wealthy of Samaria. He addressed them: "Woe to those who lie on beds of ivory and stretch out on their couches, and eat lambs from the flock... who drink wine in bowls and anoint themselves with the finest oils, but are not grieved over the ruin of Joseph" (Amos 6:4–6). The ruin of Joseph was the suffering of the poor. The comfortable had ceased to feel it. The prophet's job was to make them feel it again. Not to expel them. To awaken them. That is a pastoral act.

## The thing we refuse to say

The wealthy in our congregations generally know that Jesus said hard things about money. They have read Luke 12:15 — "one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions." They have heard it preached around the edges. What they have rarely experienced is a pastor willing to sit with them and ask: What is this doing to your soul? What does your wealth cost the people downstream from the decisions your wealth makes possible? Is the kingdom of God actually the organizing center of your financial life, or is your financial life a separate sovereignty that coexists peacefully alongside your religious life?

Those questions are not comfortable. They are also not optional if you believe the Sermon on the Mount is binding.

I know a pastor — a good man, a careful man — who has spent fifteen years serving a congregation with several families of significant wealth. He has never once had a direct conversation with any of them about money and discipleship. He tells himself they know. They do not know. They have never been told. They have been prayed over, counseled in marriage difficulty, eulogized when they lost parents, and handed the offering plate on stewardship Sunday. They have not been pastored in the place where the hardest work of discipleship is required.

He is afraid. I understand the fear. I have lived it.

## What complicity looks like

Pastoral care that does not include prophetic challenge is not pastoral care. It is what the prophets called false peace — speaking smooth words to those who have not been reached by grief (Jeremiah 6:14). The prophet who assures the wealthy that their wealth is a sign of God's blessing while the poor of the congregation struggle with medical debt has spoken a false word. He has not pastored. He has performed.

The word of God for the wealthy congregant is not "give more to the building campaign." It is the word Jesus gave to the rich young ruler: something specific, something costly, something that goes to the center of the question that wealth always raises — what does it mean to hold loosely what the world says you have earned?

Some will leave. The rich young ruler left. He "went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions" (Matthew 19:22). Jesus did not chase him. He did not soften the word. He let the man go.

That is the hardest sentence in the text. And it is the pastoral model.

The wealthy congregant needs to be loved enough to be told the truth. The congregation that does not produce that love has made a trade: the comfort of the wealthy for the formation of their souls. It is a very bad trade. And most of us have made it.
