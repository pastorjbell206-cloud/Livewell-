Every Christian believes the whole Bible and reads about a fifth of it. We have our passages — the ones underlined to the point of softening the page, the ones we return to in the dark, the ones we quote without looking up — and we have the vast remainder we have never sat in, the genealogies and the imprecatory psalms and the harder sayings of Jesus and the parts of Paul we wish he had phrased more gently. We do not deny the rest. We simply do not eat it. And a man who affirms the whole loaf while only ever eating the heel has a relationship with the bread that his words do not describe. **Cherry-picking is the American hermeneutic, and it works precisely because it never announces itself as picking. It feels like devotion to the parts we love.**

The phrase I want to recover is older than the problem. When Paul said goodbye to the Ephesian elders, weeping, knowing he would not see them again, he summed up his ministry among them in one defense: I did not shrink from declaring to you *the whole counsel of God* (Acts 20:27). The whole counsel. Not the useful counsel, not the popular counsel, not the counsel that grew the church or pleased the donors or fit the moment. The whole of it — including, he says elsewhere in the same speech, the parts that made men weep and the warnings that made them angry. Paul measured his faithfulness not by what he preached but by what he refused to leave out.

## The American edit

There is a reason cherry-picking flourishes here in particular, and it is worth naming because it is not flattering. The American religious mind is consumer-shaped, and a consumer assembles. He builds his preferences from a catalog, taking the features he wants and declining the ones he does not, and he carries that habit into the sanctuary without ever deciding to. So he assembles a Bible. He takes the grace and leaves the judgment, or the justice and leaves the holiness, or the comfort and leaves the cost, and the resulting collection feels like his faith because he chose every piece of it. That is the tell. A faith you assembled from the parts you preferred is a faith made in your own image, and an image made in your own image is the oldest idol there is.

Walter Brueggemann spent his career insisting that the Bible will not hold still for this. Scripture, he argued, is not a settled monologue but a sustained and unresolved conversation — the structure-legitimating texts and the pain-embracing texts, the voice of order and the voice of protest, held together in a tension the text never relaxes. The God of the priestly material and the God of the lament psalms are the same God, and the reader who wants only one of those voices has not found the real one. Brueggemann's point cuts at both tribes. The Christian who wants only a God of justice and the Christian who wants only a God of order are each silencing half of the witness and calling the silence "the biblical view." The whole counsel includes the texts that argue with your favorite texts.

This is why cherry-picking is not merely incomplete. It is distorting. A half-truth held as a whole truth does not function as a smaller truth. It functions as a falsehood, because it claims a completeness it does not have. The man who knows only the verses about God's love and none about God's holiness does not believe a little about God. He believes something false about God, assembled honestly out of true verses.

The danger is sharpest precisely because the materials are genuine. A heresy built from outright lies is easy to spot and easy to reject. The heresies that have actually wounded the church were built from real verses, faithfully quoted, arranged to leave out the verses that would have balanced them. Every serious distortion in Christian history has been a true emphasis grown cancerous by isolation — grace cut off from holiness becoming license, holiness cut off from grace becoming cruelty, sovereignty cut off from love becoming fatalism, love cut off from judgment becoming sentiment. None of these errors required a single false verse. They required only that certain true verses never be allowed in the room. That is the whole method, and it is why the whole counsel is not a nice ideal but a structural necessity. The verses you leave out do not merely shrink your picture of God. They warp the verses you keep.

## The canon you actually carry

Here is a test you can run on yourself, and it stings. List the passages you have heard preached or have preached in the last year. Now list the books of the Bible you have not opened in five. The gap between those two lists is your real canon — the functioning Bible inside the affirmed Bible, the one you actually live from. For most of us it is startlingly small. We affirm sixty-six books and operate on a few dozen passages, and the few dozen are not randomly distributed. They cluster around our comforts and our convictions and avoid, with a precision we never planned, the texts that would cost us something.

And the clustering follows our politics, which is why this matters for reading Scripture past our politics. The conservative's functioning canon and the progressive's functioning canon are different canons cut from the same book. Each has kept the verses that arm his side and let the others fall out of use, not by tearing them out but simply by never going there. Nobody decided to remove Amos from the conservative Bible or the household codes from the progressive one. They just stopped getting read, and a verse that never gets read is, functionally, a verse that has been edited out, however firmly it remains affirmed in principle. The whole counsel is the cure precisely because it forces you to sit under the texts your tribe has quietly retired.

This is a subtler corruption than open denial, and far more common, because it never requires anyone to say a single disloyal word about the Bible. The man who edits by avoidance keeps his doctrine of Scripture perfectly intact. He affirms every word inspired, every page authoritative, sixty-six books without exception — and he reads thirty of them, and the thirty are the ones that never make him uncomfortable. Ask him and he will tell you, sincerely, that he submits to the whole Bible. He believes it about himself. But belief about the Bible and submission to the Bible are not the same act, and a man can hold the highest possible view of Scripture's authority while arranging his reading so that authority never once falls on the parts of his life he has decided not to surrender. The doctrine guards the gate. The avoidance empties the house. He has a high view of a book he has quietly stopped letting speak to him.

The discipline that recovers this is ancient and unglamorous: read the whole thing, in course, including the parts that bore you and the parts that offend you, on the assumption that the boredom and the offense are diagnostic. The passages that bore you may be the ones you have not yet been taught to hear. The passages that offend you may be the ones aimed precisely at the part of you that needs the offense. **The verse you most want to skip is, with unnerving reliability, the verse most likely to be addressed to you.**

## What the whole counsel is for

Christopher Wright, in *The Mission of God* (2006), made a case that reframes the entire question of why we read the canon whole. The Bible, he argued, is not a collection of doctrines to be sorted and a collection of rules to be obeyed, with some inspiring stories attached. It is one story — God's mission to redeem a broken world and gather a people through whom to bless all peoples — running from the garden to the city, from Abraham to the new creation, with every part serving the whole. Read that way, the canon is not a quarry you mine for the stones you need. It is a single building, and a stone pulled out of the building and admired on its own has lost the thing that made it load-bearing.

This is the deep reason cherry-picking fails. A verse means what it means partly because of where it sits in the story. The law means one thing inside the story that runs through the cross and another thing wrenched out and held as freestanding code. The wrath of God in the prophets means one thing inside the story that arrives at a Father running to meet a prodigal and another thing isolated and waved as a club. You cannot understand a sentence of Scripture rightly while ignoring the story it is a sentence in, and you cannot know the story while reading only your favorite chapters of it. The whole counsel is not a quota of coverage. It is the only context in which the parts can mean what they actually mean.

Wright's framing exposes something about the American habit in particular. We treat the Bible as a reference work — a book you consult, the way you consult a dictionary or a legal code, opening to the relevant entry and closing it again. But you do not read a story by consulting it. You read a story by living through it, beginning to end, letting the early chapters set up the later ones and the later ones reinterpret the early ones. A man who only ever consults the Bible for rulings on his current questions will never be formed by it, because formation comes from the arc, not the entry. He will know what the Bible says about a hundred topics and remain a stranger to what the Bible is about. The cherry-picker is, finally, a man reading a story as though it were an index, and then wondering why it never moves him the way it moved the saints who read it as the story of their lives.

There is a further loss the cherry-picker never accounts for, and it is the loss of the parts that point. The genealogies he skips are not filler; they are the spine of a covenant promise running across centuries, the record that God keeps his word to a thousand generations through names no one can pronounce. The lament psalms he avoids are not the Bible having a bad day; they are the permission, given by God himself, to bring rage and despair into the presence of God without losing your place at the table — a permission the church desperately needs and rarely preaches. The hard sayings of Jesus he reads past are not the parts to apologize for; they are the parts where the cost of following becomes visible, and a discipleship that has edited them out has edited out the cross. The whole counsel is not a longer reading list. It is the difference between a faith that can hold a funeral and a faith that can only hold a celebration, between a gospel that costs something and a gospel that merely consoles.

Jesus himself modeled this on the Emmaus road, where, beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to the two disciples the things concerning himself *in all the Scriptures* (Luke 24:27). Not in the messianic proof-texts only. In all the Scriptures — the whole counsel read as one testimony pointing to one Person. The risen Christ did not hand his disciples a list of favorite verses. He handed them the entire library and showed them it had been about him the whole time. A canon read whole is a canon that can do that. A canon read in fragments cannot, because the fragments do not point anywhere together.

## All of it, including the parts that breathe on you

Paul gave Timothy the sentence the whole church has memorized and the whole church has narrowed. *All* Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable — for teaching, for reproof, for correction, for training in righteousness (2 Timothy 3:16). All of it. Not the parts that teach you what you already wanted to learn, but the parts that reprove, the parts that correct, the parts that train you in a righteousness you did not come looking for. The verse most often quoted to defend the authority of the whole Bible is itself a verse about reading the whole Bible — and we have managed to quote it while practicing the opposite, holding up its claim to inspiration while skipping the reproof and correction it promises the inspired text will deliver.

There is a discipline that exposes all of this, and it is almost embarrassingly simple. Read the whole Bible, in order, on a schedule you do not get to edit by mood. Not because coverage is holiness — it is not — but because a reading you cannot edit is the only kind that can correct you. The moment you are allowed to choose each day's passage, the lenses choose for you, steering you reliably toward comfort and away from the texts that would reprove. A fixed reading through the whole counsel takes the steering wheel out of your hands and drives you, week after week, straight through the passages you would have avoided forever if the choice had stayed with you. That is not legalism. That is a man who knows his own appetites well enough not to trust them with the menu.

So the question is not whether you believe the whole counsel. You will say you do. The question is whether you read it — whether the genealogies and the laments and the warnings and the hard sayings get any time, or whether your real Bible is the comfortable anthology inside the affirmed library. The first is a man under the whole word of God. The second is a man who has made the word of God agree with him by the simple method of never reading the parts that wouldn't.

Paul could say he was innocent of the blood of all because he had withheld nothing profitable. Few of us could say it. We have withheld plenty — not from others, but from ourselves, skipping the counsel that would have corrected us and calling the skipping a preference. The whole counsel is still there, unread, breathing, profitable, waiting. And the parts we have most carefully avoided are sitting exactly where Paul said the profit would be hardest and best: in the reproof, in the correction, in the training we did not ask for and most need.
