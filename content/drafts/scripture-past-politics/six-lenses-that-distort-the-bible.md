Ask a roomful of Christians whether they read the Bible objectively and most hands go up. Ask the same room to define a good life, a good marriage, a good death, and the answers will sound less like Galilee and more like the country they happen to live in. The two facts do not sit easily together. We believe we come to the text empty-handed, and we arrive carrying a worldview we did not pack and cannot see. **You do not read the Bible. You read the Bible through a lens — and the lens was ground by your culture long before you ever opened the book.**

This is the claim under everything I write about Scripture, so let me be exact about it. The distortion does not begin at interpretation. It begins before interpretation, in the equipment. By the time you reach a verse and ask what it means, six lenses have already decided what kinds of meaning are even available to you. They have filtered the text before your conscious mind got a vote. The work of reading Scripture past our politics is not, first, the work of choosing better answers. It is the work of seeing the glass.

## The glass you cannot feel

Charles Taylor spent eight hundred pages in *A Secular Age* (2007) making one point that should unsettle every confident reader: we are shaped most by the things we never argue about. He called them the background — the set of assumptions so deep we mistake them for reality itself. You do not believe your background the way you believe a doctrine. You believe it the way a fish believes in water. It is not a position you hold. It is the medium you swim in, and a medium is invisible precisely because it is everywhere.

James K. A. Smith pressed this further in *Desiring the Kingdom* (2009), and his turn is the one that should frighten us most. We are not, Smith argued, primarily thinking beings who occasionally feel. We are primarily loving beings, formed less by what we are taught than by what we practice, and our deepest practices are liturgies we never recognize as liturgies. The mall forms us. The screen forms us. The thousand small repetitions of a consumer economy form us — and they form not our opinions but our wants, the prior layer where opinions get their orders. By the time a man sits down with his Bible on Sunday, he has been catechized all week by liturgies he never noticed attending. He thinks he is bringing a blank mind to the text. He is bringing a trained heart.

This is what the older theologians meant by presupposition, and what N. T. Wright means when he insists that everyone reads from inside a worldview — a story about what the world is, who we are, what has gone wrong, and what would set it right. There is no view from nowhere. There is only the question of whether you know where you are standing. The reader who believes he has no lens is not the reader without distortion. **He is the reader whose distortion runs deepest, because it has never once been examined.**

There is a familiar image for this, the monster in the mirror — the thing we are afraid of turning out to be the thing we have brought with us, reflected back. We read the Bible looking for the truth about God and the world, and what comes back at us, more often than we know, is the truth about ourselves: our wants, our fears, our nation, our party, dressed in the language of revelation. The lens does not show us what is there. It shows us what we are, magnified and sanctified, and the sanctifying is the dangerous part, because once a prejudice has been read back to you in the voice of God you will defend it as faith. The monster in the mirror is not in the text. It is in the glass we hold between us and the text, and it has our face.

## The first three lenses: how the self got large

Name the glass and it loses some of its power. So name it.

The first lens is *individualism*, and it is the most American of the six. We read the Bible as though it were addressed to me — my salvation, my walk, my best life, my personal relationship — when the overwhelming grammar of Scripture is plural. The "you" of the New Testament is almost always a "you all." The promises are given to a people, the commands to a community, the warnings to a church. When Paul writes that you are God's temple in 1 Corinthians, the "you" is corporate and the temple is singular: together you are one dwelling place. Read individualistically, that becomes a verse about my body and my private holiness, which is not nothing, but it is not the sentence Paul wrote. The lens did not change a word. It changed who the word was for.

The second lens is *consumerism*, and it does to Scripture what it does to everything: it turns a summons into a product. The consumer approaches the text the way he approaches a menu, scanning for what meets a felt need, skipping what does not appeal, evaluating the encounter by whether it delivered. Did the passage feed me. Did the sermon move me. Did I get something out of it. These are the questions of a buyer, and the Bible was not written to be bought. It was written to confront, to claim, to put a demand on the reader that the reader did not come shopping for. A consumer cannot hear a summons. He can only hear an offer.

The third lens is *nationalism*, and it is the lens most likely to make a Christian angry when you name it, which is how you know it is load-bearing. The nationalist reads the Bible as though his country were a character in it — as though the promises to Israel transferred to his flag, the warnings to other nations, the blessing to his side. He reads Scripture for confirmation that God is on his nation's side, and he never asks Joshua's terrifying question to the commander of the Lord's army, whether you are for us or for our enemies, and receives the answer that breaks every nationalist reading: *Neither.* The lens does not let that answer through. It filters for an allegiance the text refuses to give. And the lens is not partisan in the way we usually mean. The Christian who reads America into the promised land and the Christian who reads America into Babylon are wearing the same lens facing opposite directions — both have made a modern nation the secret subject of an ancient text, and both have stopped reading the text to read their country in it.

## The second three lenses: how the cross got comfortable

The fourth lens is *the therapeutic*, and it may be the most pervasive lens in the contemporary church. It reads the Bible as a resource for emotional well-being, a kit for feeling settled, a path to my flourishing as I have already defined flourishing. Under this lens, sin becomes brokenness, repentance becomes growth, and the God who judges becomes a God who only affirms. The therapeutic lens cannot read Gethsemane, where the Son of God asks for the cup to pass and then drinks it anyway, because the lens cannot hold a love that wounds before it heals. It wants comfort without cost. The cross was cost before it was anything.

The fifth lens is *prosperity*, the lens that reads the covenant as a contract — faith in, blessing out, with material increase as the proof of divine favor. It is easy to mock in its televised forms and easy to miss in its respectable ones, where it survives as the quiet assumption that a faithful life will go well, that God owes the obedient a soft landing. Then the reader meets Job, who lost everything while righteous, and Paul, who learned contentment in hunger and want, and the lens cracks against the text. The prosperity lens cannot read a Bible in which the most faithful man who ever lived died young, abandoned, and convicted. It needs the story to pay out. The story does not pay out. It redeems, which is a different and far costlier thing.

The sixth lens is *partisanship*, and it is the one this whole project exists to expose. The partisan reads the Bible as ammunition — mining it for verses that arm his side and explaining away the verses that arm the other. Both tribes do it. The progressive Christian and the conservative Christian read the same text and each comes away with a Jesus who voted the way they vote, and neither notices that a Jesus who only ever agrees with you is a Jesus you have made in your own image. **A Bible that confirms everything you already believed politically is not a Bible you are reading. It is a mirror you are praying to.**

I list six, but the number is not the point and the list is not closed. There are more — class, generation, the particular wounds each of us carries into the text — and they do not operate one at a time. They stack. A man reads a single verse through individualism and consumerism and partisanship at once, three lenses ground into one, and the combined distortion is invisible because it is total. This is what makes the work hard. You cannot remove the lenses by listing them, the way you cannot stop seeing the world through your eyes by learning the anatomy of the eye. Naming them does one thing only, and it is the thing that matters: it converts an invisible distortion into a visible one, and a distortion you can see is a distortion you can pray against. That is the whole ambition of this essay. Not to clean the glass. To make you aware you are wearing it.

## Why the lenses are invisible — and what makes them visible

Here is the difficulty that makes all of this hard. A lens, by definition, is not the thing you look at. It is the thing you look through. You do not see your glasses; you see the world through them, and if the prescription is wrong you do not conclude that your glasses are wrong. You conclude that the world is blurry. This is why arguing about interpretation so rarely changes anyone. Two readers stare at the same verse through different lenses and each is certain the other is the one distorting the plain meaning. Neither suspects the glass.

Scripture itself names this condition, and names it precisely. Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 3 of a veil that lies over the reading of the old covenant, a veil that remains because the heart is turned away, a veil lifted only in Christ. The next chapter widens it: the god of this age has blinded minds so they cannot see (2 Corinthians 4:4). The biblical word for our condition is not bias. It is *veil*. We are not slightly off. We are reading through a covering we did not hang and cannot remove by effort. Richard Hays spent a career showing how even the New Testament writers read their own Scriptures through a lens — but theirs was a lens they confessed, the lens of the crucified and risen Christ, deliberately chosen and named, which is the opposite of a lens you do not know you wear.

What lifts the veil is never sophistication. It is encounter. On the Emmaus road in Luke 24 two disciples walk beside the risen Jesus for miles and do not recognize him, their eyes held shut, their reading of their own Scriptures locked in a grief that filtered out resurrection. He opens the Scriptures to them, and still they do not see. Then he breaks bread, and their eyes are opened, and he vanishes, and they realize their hearts had been burning the whole time. The text was the same text it had always been. The lens was lifted by a Person. We do not argue our way out of our distortions. We are met our way out of them.

That detail is worth sitting in, because it cuts against everything our moment believes about how minds change. The two on the road had the facts. They had the Scriptures, they had the reports of the empty tomb, they had the risen Christ himself walking beside them explaining the texts in order — and none of it pierced the veil. Information did not lift it. The expert exposition of the Messiah himself did not lift it. What lifted it was a shared meal, the breaking of bread, an act of communion before it was an act of comprehension. We keep trying to think our way past our lenses, and Scripture keeps insisting that the lenses come off in the presence of Christ and in the company of his table, which is to say they come off in worship and in the church before they come off in the study. The proud reader who thinks better commentaries will fix his sight has misunderstood the disease. The disease is in the heart that is turned away, and only the encounter that turns the heart back can lift the veil the heart hung.

## Reading with the glass in hand

None of this means the lenses can be discarded. You will not achieve the view from nowhere, and the reader who claims he has shed every cultural assumption has merely added a seventh lens called pride. The goal is not glasslessness. The goal is to hold your glasses in your hand often enough to remember you are wearing them.

So the discipline is plain, and it is humbling. Read the passages you most enjoy with suspicion, because enjoyment is often the sound of a lens working smoothly. Read the passages that offend you with attention, because offense is often the sound of a lens grinding against something true. When the Bible agrees with your politics, your appetites, your nation, and your sense of yourself all at once, stop and check the glass, because a text that only ever flatters you is not being read. Sit under teachers from centuries and continents not your own, because they wear different lenses and can see your blur the way you cannot. And confess, before you open the book, that you come to it veiled and need the veil lifted by Someone who is not you.

Paul ends the great chapter on love with the sentence that should be carved over every reader's desk. Now we see through a glass, darkly; then we shall see face to face (1 Corinthians 13:12). He is not describing a problem we will solve this side of glory. He is describing the human condition until the One who lifts veils returns to lift the last one. Until then we read in the dark, through glass, distorted.

The honest reader is not the one who claims clear sight. He is the one who keeps his hand near the glass, knowing he cannot see without it and cannot fully trust it either — and who reads, every morning, as a man waiting to have his eyes opened.
