On a given Sunday morning in the congregation I serve, the same sermon lands in a room containing a retired schoolteacher who has carried orthodox faith for fifty years and finds abstract theology comforting, a thirty-year-old man attending for the first time who would not identify himself as a Christian but came with his wife because their marriage is in trouble, a recent immigrant for whom the English language is still an obstacle course, and a teenager who is secretly losing her grip on everything her parents believe. The sermon is one sermon. These are four completely different situations. They are not the exception — they are the room.

The preacher who has not reckoned with the diversity of the room will do one of two things. He will aim for the center and hope the edges receive something. Or he will aim for one audience — usually the audience most like himself — and let everyone else work out how to translate it. Neither strategy is preaching. They are, at best, one variety of public address delivered to a roomful of people who deserved more than a variety.

The gospel is not culturally neutral. It arrived at a specific moment in history, in a specific language, in a specific body, to specific people living under specific conditions. The Incarnation itself is God's answer to the question of how an eternal truth reaches particular people — not by hovering above the particularity of human experience but by entering it. Jesus did not preach the Sermon on the Mount to an abstracted generic audience. He preached it to the poor, the mourning, the persecuted, people for whom the promise of a kingdom that worked differently was not an interesting philosophical proposition but a claim about whether their life could be otherwise.

The preacher's work is to do what the Incarnation models: to speak the unchanging word into the specific situation. Not to change the gospel. To speak it with the kind of specificity that makes it arrive — that makes the man whose marriage is breaking apart hear not a general religious communication but a word addressed to him in the actual circumstances of his life. This is not manipulation. It is the ancient pastoral practice of preaching as a form of care.

What does it require? It requires knowing the room. Not abstractly — not the demographic report — but knowing the specific people in it, their specific weights, what they carry when they walk in and what they need to carry out. This is the argument for pastoral visiting, for genuine congregational community, for the practice of listening throughout the week so that the Sunday sermon is already in dialogue with the actual lives of actual people before the first sentence is spoken.

It also requires theological generativity — the ability to ask, with genuine curiosity, what the same text yields when it is read from a different situation. The parable of the Prodigal Son (Luke 15) does not say the same thing to the waiting father as it says to the younger son as it says to the older brother who never left. All three of those characters are in the room on Sunday morning. The preacher who only addresses the returning prodigal — who knows only one way the text can land — has preached one third of the sermon.

The steel-man for simplicity deserves hearing. There is a version of this argument that produces a preacher who is so attentive to the diversity of his audience that he loses the nerve to say anything clear. The attempt to address everyone equally can produce sermons that say nothing sharply. Clarity requires commitment to what the text actually says, and that commitment must not be dissolved by the desire to offend no one.

Clarity and particularity are not opposites. The clear sentence that lands in a specific life is more powerful than the vague sentence that is inoffensive to everyone. The preacher's task is to say the clear thing with enough particularity that multiple different people recognize themselves in it — the way the text of Scripture itself does, which is why a first-century letter to a Roman congregation still names something recognizable in twenty-first century Michigan.

The gospel does not require translation. It requires incarnation. Every week, in every room, again.

That is what preaching is for.
