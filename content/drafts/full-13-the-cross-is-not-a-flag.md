I have stood under that image. The image is a cross and a flag on the same stage, photographed from the right angle, sold as patriotism. The image is not patriotism. The image is idolatry, and the idolatry has been so normalized in the American sanctuary that most of us no longer see it. I want us to see it.

I want to say at the outset that I love the United States. I am writing this from a town I have served as pastor for fifteen years, in a country whose freedoms have let me build the work my life has been. The patriotism is real. The patriotism is also subordinate. The patriotism does not belong inside the worship space. Patriotism belongs to one order. Worship belongs to another. The two orders have been blurred in the American sanctuary for a hundred years, and the blurring has cost more than most of us have noticed.

This essay is about the cost.

## What iconography does

Iconography is not decoration. Iconography is theology. What you put in your worship space, where you put it, and what you put it next to are theological claims, whether you intended them to be or not. The Eastern Orthodox tradition has been clear about this for fifteen centuries. The Reformed tradition, in its iconoclastic moments, has been clear about it from the other direction — the historic Reformed concern about religious imagery was precisely the recognition that images make claims, and that some claims should not be made. Both traditions, opposite as they look, agree on the underlying point. Iconography is doing theological work. The question is what work it is doing.

The cross does specific theological work. The cross is the place where the eternal Son of God, taking on human flesh, suffered the consequences of human sin and the violence of the political authorities of his day, and by his suffering and resurrection inaugurated the kingdom of God in which the principalities and powers of the present age are judged. The cross is the central Christian symbol because the cross is the central Christian event. The cross is not a generic religious image. The cross is the specific judgment on every other claimant to ultimacy in human history — including, and especially, every political claimant.

The flag also does specific theological work, whether we have noticed it or not. The flag is the symbol of a particular political community. It marks territory. It claims allegiance. The American flag has its own theological history — it has been used in invocations, in funerals, in courtroom oaths, in pledges of allegiance recited by children. The American flag is not a neutral object. The American flag is a sacred object in the American civil religion, and the civil religion is one of the most successful religious projects in modern history.

When you put the cross and the American flag on the same platform, in the same worship space, you have made an iconographic claim. The claim is that the two symbols belong together, that the things they represent are aligned, that the worship of Christ and the loyalty to America are mutually reinforcing in such a way that they can share the same sacred space without theological tension. The claim is precisely what Christian nationalism, in its more academic forms, has been arguing for. And the claim is iconographically present in every evangelical church I have visited that displays the flag in the sanctuary, whether the pastor and congregation realize they are making the claim or not.

I have been in churches where I knew the pastor would explicitly disavow Christian nationalism if asked, and yet the iconography of his sanctuary was a Christian nationalist iconography. The iconography was running underneath the explicit theology, doing its own theological work, forming the imagination of the congregation in directions the explicit theology was not. This is how iconography works. It works underneath. It works on the imagination. It works whether you intended it to or not. The cross-and-flag image is doing its work in your sanctuary every Sunday, regardless of what is said from the pulpit, regardless of what the explicit doctrine of your church holds. The image is the deeper theology.

## What the historic church knew

The historic church has been wary of this kind of iconographic conflation for fifteen centuries. The Eastern church, in the iconoclastic controversies of the eighth and ninth centuries, hashed out a careful theology of images that distinguished the icons of Christ and the saints from the imperial portraits — both being images, but only one being theologically legitimate. The Western church, in the medieval period and again in the Reformation, repeatedly insisted that no imperial or royal symbol could be placed in such a way as to share the visual authority of the cross or the altar. The Reformed tradition went further, stripping out almost all sacred imagery in some periods — partly because of the iconoclastic instinct, but also partly because the entanglement of religious imagery with political power had become so visible in late medieval Catholicism that the Reformers preferred bare walls to the risk.

What none of these traditions did, until the rise of modern nationalism in the nineteenth century, was place national flags inside worship spaces as part of the regular iconographic furniture. This is a new development. It begins, in the American case, in the period after the Civil War. It accelerates during World War I and World War II, when American Christianity was thoroughly enlisted in the war effort. It becomes, by the postwar period, the default. By 1955, the American flag in the sanctuary is so common that almost no one notices it. It has become invisible in the way that powerful theology often does — by being so omnipresent that it ceases to be remarkable.

The historic church would have been horrified. The Reichskirche of the 1930s did exactly this — placed national imagery inside worship spaces, conflated the cross with the national symbol, made the iconography itself a theological claim. The Confessing Church responded by, among other things, refusing the iconography. The Barmen Declaration is a textual document, but it has an iconographic correlate — the Confessing Church congregations took the swastikas out of their sanctuaries. They knew what was at stake. We have not done the work of knowing what is at stake in our own iconographic situation.

## What it forms

The cross-and-flag iconography forms a specific kind of Christian. The Christian formed by this iconography learns, in his body, that loyalty to America and loyalty to Christ are versions of the same loyalty. The lesson is not taught explicitly; the lesson is taught by repetition, by visual reinforcement, by the unconscious absorption of what is on the platform every Sunday. The Christian who has been in this iconography for twenty years has been formed by it whether he has noticed or not. The iconography has done its work.

What the iconography produces is a Christian whose patriotism feels like worship and whose worship feels like patriotism. The two have become emotionally indistinguishable. When this Christian sings *God Bless America* at a political rally, he is experiencing what feels like a religious moment. When he prays in church, he is sometimes experiencing what feels like patriotic feeling. The categories have been blurred in his affections. The blurring has been produced, in part, by the iconography he has been inside for two decades.

This is not a small effect. The affections shape the politics. The Christian whose worship and patriotism have become emotionally indistinguishable will find it almost impossible to imagine a Christianity that is in tension with his patriotism. He will read prophetic critiques of the nation as attacks on the faith. He will read calls for Christian universalism — the recognition that the church belongs to every nation, not to one — as forms of disloyalty. He will, in some cases, become the foot soldier of a political project that has co-opted his religious affections. The political project will use him. The political project will not love him back. The political project will, when the project no longer needs him, discard him. The pattern is the pattern I named in the strongman essay. The iconography has prepared the ground.

## What to do

Take the flag out of the sanctuary.

I want to say this plainly, because the plain statement is what most pastors will not yet make from their own pulpits. The flag does not belong in the worship space. The flag has its place — in the public square, at the courthouse, at the school, at the cemetery. The flag does not belong on the worship platform, in the chancel, behind the pulpit, anywhere the eye of the worshipper will rest on it during the act of worship.

I have done this in my own sanctuary. The doing cost me. Several families left. A handful of older members were wounded in ways I had to spend a year repairing. The elder board had a hard meeting. The local newspaper ran an unflattering item. The doing was the right thing to do, and the cost was the cost of the right thing, and the church on the other side of the doing is a church that has, in some real ways, recovered an iconographic clarity that we had lost. The pastor reading this who has not yet done this can do it. The doing will cost. The cost is worth it.

If you cannot take the flag out — because the elder board will not allow it, because the political conditions of your congregation make it impossible right now, because the conversation has not been prepared — then move it. Move it to the lobby. Move it to the fellowship hall. Move it anywhere outside the worship space. The iconographic claim diminishes when the visual conjunction is broken. The worship recovers some clarity. The compromise is not ideal. The compromise is better than the iconography that is currently doing its work.

If you cannot move it, at least preach about it. Name what the iconography is doing. Tell the congregation what they have been absorbing. Refuse to let the iconography do its work invisibly. The naming itself is part of the work. The congregation that has heard the iconography named is a congregation that has, in some real sense, begun to think about it. The thinking is the beginning of the recovery.

## The verdict

The cross does not need the flag. The cross has never needed any flag. The cross is the thing that judges every flag, and the church that places a flag beside the cross has misunderstood what is doing the judging.

Take the flag out. Let the cross stand alone. See what happens to your church when the cross is the only object on the platform. You will discover what your worship was about. Some of what you will discover will be painful. Some of what you will discover will be merciful. All of it will be honest, in a way the current iconography has not let you be.

The faithful church in America in 2026 has, among other things, the work of recovering iconographic clarity. The work is small. The work is consequential. The work is, in many cases, what the pastor reading this can begin to do this week. The next essay in this arc takes up the related question of partisan identity — the deeper confusion that the iconography has been the visible sign of. Left and right are both mistakes. The next essay says so. The third option is not centrism. The third option is the gospel. The gospel does not allow you to belong to either team in the way the iconography has been training you to belong. That is the next essay. This one ends with the cross. Take the flag down. Let the cross do its work.
