There is a particular dread that arrives on Thursday afternoon. The text is open. The commentary stack is there. And nothing comes — not the electricity that used to arrive, not the sense that this passage is hunting you, that it needs to be preached. Just words. Just obligation. Just the crushing awareness that Sunday is three days away and you have nothing that will cost you anything to say.

I have sat in that chair. I know what that silence feels like.

Stale preaching is not primarily a craft problem. It is a soul problem — and most preachers try to fix the craft because the soul examination is harder to bear. They buy a new commentary. They switch series. They try a different structure. They discover a preacher they envy and borrow his moves. None of it works for long, because the real problem is not what is in the books. The real problem is that somewhere in the accumulated weight of ministry — the funerals, the board meetings, the pastoral emergencies at ten o'clock on a Saturday night, the decade of Sunday mornings in the same room — the preacher stopped being a person before God and became a professional before a congregation.

The Psalms model something the modern pulpit has mostly forgotten. The poets of Israel did not write from composure. They wrote from the middle — from inside the confusion, the grief, the bewilderment that God was silent. Psalm 22 opens not with a sermon point but with a cry: *My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?* (Psalm 22:1). The preacher who has not spent time in that territory — not as a rhetorical exercise but as a genuine description of his own inner life — has nothing to offer the congregation member who sits there in exactly that condition every week.

Steel-manning the drift is important here: there are legitimate explanations for a season of staleness that have nothing to do with spiritual failure. Grief does this. Protracted conflict does this. The kind of exhaustion that accumulates across fifteen years of sustained giving without adequate replenishment — that does this too. Sometimes the well is not polluted. It is simply empty. And an empty well needs to be filled, not blamed.

But even that diagnosis points to the same answer. The filling does not happen through ministry productivity. It happens through the kind of sustained, unhurried encounter with Scripture as a reader rather than as a preacher — where the text is not material to be processed for Sunday but a place where God might actually say something to you, specifically, today. Eugene Peterson called this *lectio divina* — the slow, prayerful reading that treats the Bible as a living document rather than a data set. Most preachers know the concept. Very few practice it consistently in the season when it would matter most.

There is also something to be said about community — or the chronic lack of it. Elijah, exhausted and suicidal after Carmel, did not need a vision or a new strategy. He needed sleep, bread, water, and someone who said *get up, the journey is too great for you* (1 Kings 19:7). What he received was presence. The preacher who preaches to community without living inside community of his own will eventually run out of the kind of deep knowing that makes preaching land somewhere other than the surface. Preaching becomes stale when it stops coming from a life that is actually being lived.

This is the verdict: you cannot give what you have stopped receiving. And the fix is not more preparation. The fix is more exposure — to text you are reading for yourself, to people who know you past your title, to the God who called you into this in the first place.

The sermon that matters next Sunday is not built in the study on Thursday. It is built in the accumulated faithfulness of a life before God across the whole week. When that faithfulness is missing, no amount of technique will compensate. When it is present, even a preacher who is tired and uncertain will find that what he says carries weight — not because he performed it well, but because it is true, and truth has mass.

The fire is not gone. But you may need to stop running long enough to find where it went.
