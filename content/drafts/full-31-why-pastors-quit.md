I have spent five years inside the Pastors Connection Network watching pastors quit. Some of them quit because they should have. Most of them quit because no one had built them a way to stay. The leadership writing I read in seminary did not name what is actually breaking them. I want to name it. And I want to name what staying actually requires.

This essay is for the pastor reading it at one in the morning, considering whether he can do this another year. I have been him. I have been him more than once. The work is hard. The work is worth it. The work is also being made unnecessarily harder by the structures most of us are operating inside, and the structures can be changed. The changing is what this essay is about.

## What the data shows

Barna's *State of Pastors* reports over the past five years have documented a sustained crisis. The percentage of pastors who have considered leaving full-time ministry in the past year has remained around forty percent across multiple surveys. The percentage who have considered leaving in the past month has been climbing. The percentage of pastors who report being lonely most of the time has been climbing. The percentage who report being unable to find adequate mental health support has been climbing. The percentage who report financial stress that affects their ability to function pastorally has been climbing.

These numbers describe a profession in serious trouble. The trouble is not a perception problem. The trouble is the actual condition of the pastoral workforce in American Christianity. The condition has been getting worse rather than better.

The standard leadership writing has named the visible features of the crisis — workload, congregational politics, lack of self-care. The standard writing has, on the whole, addressed the wrong level of the problem. The actual reasons pastors are leaving are deeper, and they have not been named at the level they need to be named.

## What is actually breaking pastors

I want to name the reasons that most leadership writing has not been willing to name, because the naming is the necessary work, and the naming has been delayed for too long.

Pastors are breaking because of theological loneliness. The pastor is, in most congregations, the only person whose vocation requires the sustained engagement with hard theological questions. The pastor cannot share these questions with most members of his congregation, because most members are not equipped to engage them and would be alarmed by some of them. The pastor cannot, in many cases, share them with his elder board, because the elder board is supposed to be confident in his theological soundness rather than working through his theological wrestling. The pastor cannot share them with most other pastors he knows, because the other pastors are inside the same institutional dynamic and have learned to keep their own wrestling private. The pastor is alone with the questions, often for years, often with no spiritual director, no confessor, no real interlocutor. The loneliness is structural. The structural loneliness is corrosive.

Pastors are breaking because of spiritual abuse from elder boards. This is more common than the conversation about pastoral burnout admits, and the elder boards inflicting the abuse are often unaware that they are inflicting it. The dynamics are recognizable. The pastor is held responsible for outcomes he does not control. The pastor is criticized in ways that would not be acceptable in any other professional context. The pastor's spouse is treated as part of the church's volunteer corps. The pastor's children are subjected to scrutiny no other family in the congregation receives. The pastor's compensation is held below what the work requires, with the assumption that ministry should not be primarily about money. The pastor is told, when he raises any of this, that he is being unspiritual or ungrateful or worldly. The abuse is real. The abuse is widespread. The abuse is not being named at the level it needs to be named, because the elder boards inflicting it are also the elder boards that would have to acknowledge their role in it.

Pastors are breaking because there is no pastor for the pastor. The historic church recognized that pastors need pastoral care, and built structures for it — spiritual directors, confessors, denominational oversight that included pastoral support rather than just administrative oversight, monastic communities for retreat and renewal. Most contemporary American evangelical pastors do not have any of these resources. They have congregations they pastor; they do not have anyone who pastors them. The asymmetry is unsustainable. The pastor giving and not receiving will, eventually, run out of what to give. The running-out is what most of the burnout literature describes without naming the structural cause.

Pastors are breaking because of the cumulative weight of holding other people's confidences. This is a feature I addressed in a separate essay in this writing project, the one on the loneliness of knowing what you know. The pastor accumulates, over the course of his ministry, an enormous burden of confidential knowledge about the people he serves. The burden is carried alone, by professional necessity. The carrying alone is exhausting in ways that no one outside the work understands. The exhaustion contributes to the breaking.

Pastors are breaking because the financial structures of pastoral life are precarious. The average American pastor is paid below his education level, in jobs that do not include overtime, with insurance gaps in many denominations, with housing situations that the IRS treats in ways that produce both opportunities and complications, and with no real path to retirement security in many cases. The financial precariousness compounds with the other stresses, and the compounding accelerates the breaking.

Pastors are breaking because they have stopped having a personal devotional life. The professional engagement with scripture — sermon preparation, pastoral counseling, theological writing — has replaced the personal engagement for many pastors. The replacement is not equivalent. The professional reading of scripture is necessary work, and the work is exhausting in its own ways. The work does not nourish the soul in the way the unprofessional reading of scripture does. The pastor who has not been reading scripture for himself for several years is a pastor whose interior is being depleted faster than it is being filled. The depletion is part of the breaking.

These are the reasons. They are not the reasons the books name. They are the reasons that, in my five years of working with pastors through PCN, have been the most consistent across the pastors I have walked with.

## What staying actually requires

The path to stay is hard, and most pastors I know who have made it are doing several things that the leadership culture of their generation did not equip them to do. Let me name what staying actually requires.

It requires a spiritual director who is not part of your congregation. A spiritual director is a person trained in the historic Christian practice of accompanying the soul, who meets with the pastor on a regular basis to listen, to ask questions, to offer scripture and prayer, to hold the questions the pastor cannot bring to anyone else. The spiritual director should not be a member of the congregation, should not be in any reporting relationship to the elder board, and should be operating under explicit professional confidentiality. The historic church has had this role for centuries. American evangelicalism has largely lost it. The recovery is part of what makes pastoral life sustainable.

It requires a group of other pastors who tell you the truth. The PCN exists in part to be this group; the principle generalizes. The pastor who is not in regular conversation with three to five other pastors, in conditions of mutual trust and accountability, is operating in conditions that almost no one can sustain across decades. The group has to be small enough for real intimacy and stable enough for actual trust. The group has to be willing to ask the hard questions and to receive the hard answers. The group has to operate outside the professional networks that have other interests in your performance. The group is rare. The group is necessary.

It requires a marriage that is honored as a marriage and not as part of your ministry. The spouse of the pastor is not the unpaid associate pastor of your church. The spouse is your spouse. The marriage has to be protected from the demands of the congregation, has to be given the time and energy it requires, has to be the relationship in which you are most fully known, has to be cultivated as the primary human relationship of your life rather than as the support structure for your professional work. Pastors who are working seventy-hour weeks and have not had a real conversation with their spouse in three months are pastors whose marriages will eventually fail, with consequences for the church and the children and the surrounding community that the seventy-hour weeks did not anticipate.

It requires the recovery of a personal devotional practice that is not used in the sermon. The pastor needs to be reading scripture for himself, regularly, slowly, with attention to what God is saying to him in the reading rather than what he can extract for his Sunday morning preaching. The recovery is hard. The recovery requires the discipline of refusing to use the personal reading for professional purposes. The recovery is what produces the interior depth that the professional work depletes, and without the recovery the depletion eventually wins.

It requires structural changes that the elder board has to support. Real sabbatical — not vacation, but extended time away from ministry for rest, study, and renewal. Adequate compensation that does not require the pastor's spouse to subsidize the ministry through unpaid labor or through her own underpaid work. Adequate health insurance and retirement structures. Reasonable expectations about working hours and on-call availability. The elder board that does not provide these things is not pastoring the pastor. The elder boards that have learned to provide these things have lower pastoral turnover and healthier pastors. The provision is not luxury; the provision is what makes the work sustainable across decades.

## What it looks like to leave well

I want to say something about leaving, because not every pastor who is considering leaving should stay. Some pastors should leave. The calling that brought them in is not the calling they have now, or the conditions of their current congregation are not conditions in which they can be faithful, or the structural changes their context would require are not changes the context will make. The leaving is sometimes the right call.

Leaving well looks different from leaving badly. Leaving well involves an honest assessment with a spiritual director and a small group of trusted pastors. Leaving well involves a season of preparation rather than an abrupt exit. Leaving well involves honoring the congregation through the transition rather than disappearing. Leaving well involves the willingness to take responsibility for what was your part of what did not work, including the parts the congregation cannot see. Leaving well involves the integration of the pastoral identity into whatever vocation comes next, rather than the rejection of the pastoral formation as a mistake.

The pastor who leaves badly leaves with bitterness, often with a public statement that scorches the congregation he is leaving, often into a new vocation that does not honor what he has been. The pastor who leaves badly carries the unresolved pain into the next chapter of his life, and the unresolved pain shapes the next chapter in ways that are usually painful for everyone involved.

Most of the pastors I have walked through leaving have leaned, in their own assessment, toward the badly side. The badly side is what most of us do when we have not had the spiritual director and the small group and the time and the structures to leave well. The structures are part of what makes either staying or leaving well possible.

## The verdict

Pastoring is the only calling I would still recommend to a young person who asked. It is also the calling I would warn that young person about more honestly than I was warned. The staying is worth it. The leaving is sometimes right. The path between them is the path the PCN exists to walk with you. You do not have to walk it alone. Do not walk it alone. The aloneness is what makes most of the leavers leave.

I am writing this for the pastor reading at one in the morning, considering whether he can do this another year. He can. He probably should. He cannot do it the way he has been doing it. The path forward requires the structural and personal changes I have named, and the changes are available, and the changes are what most of the pastors who have made it long-term have made.

Find a spiritual director. Find a group of three to five pastors who will tell you the truth. Protect your marriage. Recover a personal devotional practice. Have the hard conversation with your elder board. If your elder board will not move, the question becomes whether you stay long enough to change them or whether you leave to find a context where the structures are healthier. Either answer is possible. Neither answer is taken without doing the prior work.
