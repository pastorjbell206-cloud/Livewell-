The global church is not somewhere else. We tend to picture it that way — believers in distant countries, with unfamiliar languages and harder lives, the subject of a missions slideshow and a line in our giving. Real, but remote. Theirs, not ours. That picture is a quiet heresy, and it impoverishes us more than it impoverishes them.

The New Testament will not allow the distance. Paul tells the Corinthians that they are members of a single body, and that "if one member suffers, all suffer together" (1 Corinthians 12:26). He does not mean only the people in your building. He means the body of Christ, which has no nationality, which spans every tribe and tongue, which is one organism with one Head. The believer in a house church in a hostile country is not a stranger you support. He is your brother, closer kin in the only family that finally matters than your own neighbors who do not share the faith.

This reframes everything. The persecuted church is not a charity. It is your family under fire. While you read this in safety, members of your own body are paying for the name of Christ with their freedom and sometimes their lives — and Hebrews tells you to "remember those who are in prison, as though in prison with them" (Hebrews 13:3). *As though with them.* Not as distant beneficiaries of your concern, but as your own people, whose chains you are meant to feel.

It also corrects the strange provincialism that creeps into comfortable Western Christianity — the unspoken assumption that we are the center, the mature ones, the senders, and the rest of the church is the mission field. The numbers alone should humble us. The church is growing fastest, and often suffering most, in the very places our slideshows pity. The vitality of global Christianity today is not in the comfortable West. It is in the church that has little, risks much, and prays with an urgency that ought to embarrass us. We imagined ourselves the teachers. Increasingly, we are the ones who need to sit down and learn.

I have carried the provincial picture. I have prayed for the global church the way you pray for a cause rather than a family — at a distance, briefly, without really believing that their joys were mine to share and their wounds mine to feel. The repentance is to let the doctrine become real: to grasp that I am bound, by something thicker than blood, to people I will not meet until glory, and that their faithfulness is part of my inheritance and their suffering part of my own body's pain.

What does it change, practically? It changes how you pray — by name, by place, with the specificity you would use for family. It changes how you give — not as patron to project, but as one part of a body that shares its resources because it is one organism. It changes how you hold your own comfort — more lightly, knowing that the same Lord is being served at terrible cost by people who would consider your hardest week a gift. And it changes how you understand the church itself — not as your congregation with some distant affiliates, but as a single, global, blood-bought people of whom your church is one small, beloved, accountable part.

The believers across the world are not other Christians. They are *the* church, and you are in it with them. Their faith is your family's faith. Their suffering is your body's suffering. Their joy, when the kingdom comes in fullness, will be the joy you share at the same table.

They are not somewhere else. They are your church. Start praying as though you believed it.
