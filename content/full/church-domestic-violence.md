She has been sitting in your pews for years. She tithes. She serves in the children's ministry. Her husband is a deacon, or a worship leader, or a reliable presence in the men's small group who is known as the one who always seems to have a word of encouragement. She is suffering in private. She may never tell you. And if she does tell you, everything about the theological training you received, the pastoral culture you absorbed, and the institutional pressures of your role has prepared you, however unintentionally, to make it worse.

This is not a marginal scenario. Domestic violence affects approximately one in four women in the United States. [cite — confirm] The research on whether religious involvement reduces that rate is at best mixed. Some studies suggest that religious couples who practice their faith together have lower rates of intimate partner violence. Other studies suggest that women in conservative religious contexts report higher rates of staying in abusive relationships due to religious pressure. [cite — confirm] What the data does not suggest is that the church is consistently a safe place for women disclosing abuse. The data suggests the opposite: that women in many church contexts face significant barriers to disclosure, face theological frameworks that can be weaponized to keep them in danger, and face pastoral responses that prioritize marital preservation over survivor safety.

The church has much to answer for here. It is time to name what we have enabled.

**The Theology That Does the Harm**

No serious theological tradition teaches that husbands have the right to physically or psychologically abuse their wives. The harm is not located in an explicit endorsement of violence. It is located in a set of theological structures, selectively applied and frequently distorted, that create conditions in which abuse is sustained and victims are silenced.

The first structure is a distorted theology of submission. Ephesians 5:22 — "Wives, submit yourselves to your own husbands as you do to the Lord" — has been taught in countless evangelical contexts as a unilateral obligation of the wife that exists regardless of the husband's behavior, character, or treatment of her. This teaching severs the verse from its context. The sentence before it, Ephesians 5:21, is "Submit to one another out of reverence for Christ." The mutuality of submission is the opening premise of the entire section. The husband's love for his wife is immediately modeled on the self-giving love of Christ for the church — a love that gave itself up, that nourished and cherished, that sought the flourishing of the beloved (Ephesians 5:25-29). The passage is not a power structure. It is a description of mutual self-giving between two people who image the relationship between Christ and the church.

When submission is taught without mutuality, when the wife's obligation is foregrounded and the husband's costly love is treated as aspirational rather than binding, when the authority structure is emphasized while the servant-leadership expectation is softened — the theological result is a framework that can be used by an abusive husband to require compliance and by pastoral counsel to enforce it. That result is not accidental. It is the predictable outcome of a selective reading that serves the interests of those with power and disciplines those without it.

The second structure is a theology of covenant permanence that has been applied in ways that treat the wife's suffering as the acceptable price of marital integrity. Divorce is sin. Marriage is forever. The covenant must be preserved at whatever cost. These convictions, held in their proper scope, have real theological substance. Covenant matters. Permanence in marriage witnesses to the faithfulness of God. But the theology of covenant was never designed to serve as a theological cage for a woman being beaten in her own home. When covenant language is invoked to persuade an abused wife to return to the man who has harmed her, it has been separated from the God whose covenant with Israel was always, simultaneously, a covenant with the vulnerable and a judgment on those who harmed them.

The third structure is the church's institutional interest in protecting its own — in avoiding the scandal of a leader's abuse becoming public, in managing the reputational cost of a visible divorce in a prominent family, in maintaining the appearance of a congregation populated by intact marriages. This interest is not theological. It is self-protective. But it has been dressed in theological language often enough to have done sustained damage to the women who trusted the church enough to speak.

**What Actually Happens When She Tells**

The research on pastoral responses to domestic violence disclosure is sobering. Women who disclose to pastors often report being counseled toward reconciliation before their safety has been assessed, being encouraged to examine their own contribution to the marital conflict, being advised to pray more or submit more or trust God to change their husband, being warned about the spiritual consequences of divorce, and being encouraged to keep the matter private within the church leadership rather than involving external authorities. [cite — confirm]

Some of these responses come from pastoral ignorance rather than malice — from men who received no training in recognizing or responding to abuse, who have no framework for the dynamics of coercive control, who genuinely believe that the right application of biblical counsel will resolve a situation they do not fully understand. The ignorance is real. It does not reduce the harm.

Domestic violence is not a marital conflict that requires better communication skills. Coercive control — the pattern of behavior in which an abuser uses fear, isolation, financial control, and psychological manipulation to maintain dominance over a partner — does not respond to marital counseling. Joint counseling with an abusive partner is, in fact, contraindicated by most experts in intimate partner violence, because it provides the abuser with information about the survivor's inner life that can be used against her. [cite — confirm] The pastor who refers an abused woman and her husband to marital counseling is not being pastoral. He is unwittingly providing the abuser with a new venue for control.

**What the Bible Actually Demands**

The biblical witness on the protection of the vulnerable is not ambiguous. The repeated insistence in the Torah, the prophets, and the New Testament that the widow, the orphan, and the stranger — the people without social protection — are the specific objects of God's care and the test of the community's faithfulness is not incidental decoration. It is the spine of the biblical account of justice. The woman being harmed in her own home is precisely the vulnerable person the biblical tradition exists to protect.

Proverbs 31:8-9 commands: "Speak up for those who cannot speak for themselves, for the rights of all who are destitute. Speak up and judge fairly; defend the rights of the poor and needy." The woman who cannot speak for herself because the theology she has been taught requires her silence, because the institutional culture of the church has trained her to absorb harm rather than disclose it, is the person this text was written for.

The church that claims to honor Scripture while being passive toward the abuse of women within its own walls has not been faithful to Scripture. It has been selective in a way that protects the perpetrator and burdens the victim.

**What the Church Must Do**

The first obligation is to be a safe place for disclosure — which requires explicit, regular, unapologetic communication that the church takes abuse seriously, that no theological conviction about marriage will be used to pressure a victim to remain in danger, and that the pastor is a mandatory reporter of child abuse and has an obligation to help survivors connect to safety resources. This communication must happen from the pulpit, not just in a policy document filed with the church secretary.

The second obligation is training. Pastors need to understand the dynamics of coercive control, the cycle of abuse, the reasons survivors often return to abusive relationships, and why that return is not evidence that the situation is not serious. This is not specialized knowledge reserved for counselors. It is the minimum competence required to pastor any congregation of significant size without causing harm.

The third obligation is referral — not as a way of removing responsibility, but as the application of the biblical wisdom that recognizes different forms of expertise. The pastor who insists on handling every situation himself because he is the spiritual authority is often the most dangerous responder in the room. Domestic violence advocates, counselors trained in trauma and coercive control, legal resources for protection orders — these are the resources a pastor must be prepared to offer quickly, clearly, and without theological conditions.

The fourth obligation is the hardest. It is the willingness to name abusive men in the church's leadership as disqualified from their roles. Not to excommunicate them before a process of accountability, but to recognize that the man who is controlling and harming his wife in private is not fit to lead the public community, and that the church's silence about his role sends a message to every survivor in the building about whether this is a safe place.

The man in the worship team who beats his wife at home is not leading the congregation in worship. He is managing his public reputation while she carries the cost of his private behavior. The church's continued platformizing of him is not grace. It is the ongoing sacrifice of the vulnerable to the comfort of the institution.

**The Weight of the Verdict**

I have sat with women who stayed in abusive marriages for years longer than they should have because the men they told at church told them to stay. I cannot undo what I have not done rightly. I can name it for what it is and commit to doing differently.

The church is not the only institution that has failed in this. But it carries a specific responsibility because it claims to speak for the God who defends the vulnerable. Every time it does the opposite — every time it deploys theological language to keep a woman in danger, every time it protects the reputation of a leader at the cost of his victim's safety, every time it treats marital preservation as the higher value than human dignity — it is not being faithful to that God.

It is betraying him.
