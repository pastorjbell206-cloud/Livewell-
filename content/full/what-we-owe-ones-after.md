My sons will inherit the world I am making. That sentence has the power to stop a man, or it should. Not the abstract world — the specific consequences of specific choices made in specific decades. The soil that will grow or not grow their food. The atmosphere that will be warmer or cooler by the degree we have now determined. The debt that will arrive on their doorstep in the form of public fiscal obligation. The institutions that will be weakened or strengthened depending on what this generation chose to do with them. The social trust that will be available or depleted depending on what we modeled about what human beings owe each other.

I have five sons. That is not an abstraction. Their futures are downstream from decisions I am making now, decisions my congregation is making now, decisions the church as an institution is either making or refusing to make. The question of what we owe the generations that follow us is therefore not merely philosophical. It is personal in the most specific sense of the word.

---

The biblical tradition carries an explicit intergenerational conscience that the modern church has largely misplaced. When Moses delivers the law in Deuteronomy, he frames it repeatedly in terms of what it will mean for children not yet born. *You shall teach them diligently to your children* (Deuteronomy 6:7). *When your son asks you in time to come...* (Deuteronomy 6:20). The covenant at Sinai was structured to create an intergenerational community — not a contract between living parties who would renegotiate it when circumstances changed, but a commitment that the present generation would carry forward and transmit, intact and alive, to those who came after.

Psalm 78 is among the most sustained expressions of this intergenerational responsibility in the entire canon. It opens with this:

*We will not hide them from their children, but tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the Lord, and his might, and the wonders that he has done. He established a testimony in Jacob and appointed a law in Israel, which he commanded our fathers to teach to their children, that the next generation might know them, the children yet unborn, and arise and tell them to their children, so that they should set their hope in God* (Psalm 78:4-7).

The chain is explicit: each generation responsible not only to receive what it has been given but to pass it on to those who cannot yet speak for themselves. The children not yet born have a claim on the living. That claim is not sentimental. It is structural — built into the covenant community's self-understanding as a people who exist across time, not merely in the present moment.

The prophetic tradition extends this beyond spiritual inheritance. When God addresses Israel's land use practices, the language is consistently intergenerational. The jubilee system in Leviticus 25 was designed, among other things, to prevent the permanent concentration of land in the hands of the wealthy — because the next generation had a claim on the land as inheritance, not only the present generation as property. You did not own the land in the modern sense; you were its steward for a season, and what you did with it in that season would either preserve or destroy the next generation's inheritance. *The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine. For you are strangers and sojourners with me* (Leviticus 25:23). This is an explicit limit on present-generation rights in the name of intergenerational obligation.

---

The environmental question is where this theology has the most urgent contemporary application, and it is also the place where the American church has been most resistant to speaking clearly. Climate change is real, it is largely human-caused, and the consequences are being distributed unjustly — falling hardest on the poorest communities, in the Global South and in poor communities domestically, communities that have contributed least to the problem and have least capacity to adapt to it [cite — confirm]. By the projections of current science [cite — confirm], the most severe consequences will be borne primarily by people not yet born, and by those born into circumstances that provide the least protection from what is coming.

This is the precise category the biblical tradition identifies as requiring prophetic response: the vulnerable, the voiceless, those who have no representative in the room where the decisions are being made. The unborn generations are the most voiceless constituency in the history of political discourse. They cannot vote. They cannot lobby. They cannot picket. They cannot even know, yet, what is being decided on their behalf. If the church has any calling to speak for those who cannot speak for themselves — and the tradition is unanimous that it does (Proverbs 31:8-9) — then the generation not yet born has the most comprehensive claim on that voice.

The counter-position from within evangelical Christianity tends to take one of two forms. The first questions the scientific consensus, arguing that the church should not build its prophetic program on contested empirical claims. The second argues that environmental concern is a distraction from the more urgent spiritual mission of the church, and that the emphasis on creation care comes at the expense of proclamation of the gospel.

Both deserve honest engagement. The first ignores the overwhelming scientific consensus that has developed over three decades of increasingly sophisticated research [cite — confirm] — a consensus that is essentially unanimous among the relevant scientific communities. The church's commitment to truth requires following evidence where it leads, and the evidence here is not genuinely contested among the people equipped to evaluate it.

The second argument poses a false choice. The proclamation of the gospel is not undermined by the church's engagement with creation care — it is amplified by it. The credibility of the church's witness to the coming kingdom is directly tied to the seriousness with which the church takes the world God made and pronounced good. A gospel that saves souls while being indifferent to the soil those souls will be buried in is not the gospel of the God who created the heavens and the earth, who called creation good before humanity was part of it, and who declares in the final vision of Scripture not the escape from earth but its renewal: *Behold, I am making all things new* (Revelation 21:5).

---

The debt question is the economic equivalent of the environmental one. The practice of continuous deficit spending — at every level of government, and in most households — is a mechanism by which the present generation consumes resources that will have to be repaid by the next. This is not a partisan claim; it is an accounting identity. What we borrow, they will repay. The political arrangements that make intergenerational wealth transfer acceptable — that allow the present to tax the future to fund its own consumption — are a structural form of the injustice the prophets condemned: the powerful extracting from the powerless.

The present generation is powerful relative to the unborn. The unborn have no voice in the fiscal decisions being made on their behalf. This is exactly the configuration the prophetic tradition was designed to address. *Woe to those who add house to house and join field to field, until there is no room for anyone else, and you live alone in the midst of the land* (Isaiah 5:8). The logic of endless accumulation at the expense of future space, future possibility, future inheritance — this is what Isaiah was condemning, and its modern equivalents are not hard to find.

---

There is a form of short-termism built into democratic systems and market economies that the church is not required to replicate. The elected official thinks in four-year cycles. The publicly traded company thinks in quarterly earnings reports. The consumer thinks about this month's budget. None of these time horizons extends to the generation that will be born in 2050 or 2075 or 2100. None of the incentive structures built into these systems rewards decisions that sacrifice present gain for future flourishing.

The church's time horizon is explicitly longer. We serve the God who covenanted with Abraham about a people not yet born (Genesis 12:1-3), who made promises to David about a kingdom that would outlast every human dynasty (2 Samuel 7:16), who holds human history within a purpose that extends from creation to new creation. That extended vision is not a pious addition to the church's agenda. It is the fundamental form of its witness to the world: we are accountable to someone who sees all the consequences, including the ones that will not arrive for fifty years.

This should make the church uniquely equipped to speak for the future — to be the institution in public life that consistently asks the question no one else is structurally incentivized to ask: *What are we bequeathing?*

I think of my sons. I think of what they will face, not abstractly but specifically — the particular shape of the world they will inherit depending on whether my generation made the choices this moment required, or whether we managed our discomfort and called the management wisdom. The men who planted trees they would never sit under understood something the present moment resists: you owe a debt to the future that no present comfort can discharge. The debt is real. The ones who will arrive to collect it do not exist yet, which is precisely why the people who do exist bear the responsibility.

This is the weight of being human beings in time — made, as the theologian Miroslav Volf has argued, for the long arc of history, not merely the brief present tense we inhabit [cite — confirm]. The children not yet born are waiting for us to act like it.

That is not a comfortable thing to sit with. It is not meant to be.
