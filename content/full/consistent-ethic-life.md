The consistent ethic of life is a phrase associated with Cardinal Joseph Bernardin, who argued in the 1980s that a genuinely pro-life position would require opposition not just to abortion but to capital punishment, unjust war, poverty, and anything else that diminishes the dignity of human beings. He called it a seamless garment [cite — confirm].

The phrase never fully caught on, for an obvious reason: it made everyone uncomfortable. Those on the right did not want their pro-life credentials to require opposition to capital punishment or military intervention. Those on the left did not want their social justice commitments to require opposition to abortion. The seamless garment was inconvenient to both coalitions, so both coalitions declined to wear it.

But the logic of the argument is sound. And the failure of two political coalitions to find it useful is not a theological argument against it.

## The Logic That Does Not Stop

If the Imago Dei is the basis for protecting the unborn — and it should be — then it is the basis for protecting the prisoner on death row, the immigrant at the border, the elderly person whose care has become too expensive, the child in the underfunded school, the veteran abandoned after his service is no longer needed. The same argument that makes abortion morally serious makes all of these morally serious. You cannot run the argument selectively without conceding that what you are defending is not the Imago Dei but a political position that uses the Imago Dei for its purposes and sets it aside when it generates inconvenient obligations.

This is not a new argument. It runs through the whole of Scripture with a consistency that is almost embarrassing when set against the selective applications that American Christianity has made of it.

Ezekiel 16:49 records God's indictment of Sodom — and it is not the sin most people expect. "She and her daughters were arrogant, overfed and unconcerned; they did not help the poor and needy." The famous failure of Sodom, in God's own accounting, was not merely sexual sin. It was the failure of the prosperous to care for those who had nothing. The passage does not replace the sexual ethics; it refuses to let the sexual ethics stand alone as the full account of what God requires.

Proverbs 31:8-9: "Speak up for those who cannot speak for themselves, for the rights of all who are destitute." The word "destitute" here is not restricted to the unborn. It describes the full range of persons without structural protection — which, in the ancient world and in this one, is a large population.

Micah 6:8: "What does the Lord require of you? To act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God." Not to act justly in the categories that are politically convenient. To act justly — full stop, without the conditions that would make it more comfortable.

## The Specific Applications the Consistent Ethic Generates

The consistent ethic of life produces obligations that cut across every political coalition, and that is precisely what makes it theologically reliable. When a theological position is perfectly aligned with a political party, that is a warning sign, not a comfort. The Gospel has never been comfortable with any human political arrangement.

On capital punishment: if the Imago Dei is borne by every human person as a permanent feature of their humanity — not something they can forfeit by their conduct — then the argument that the state can remove a person's life as a punishment requires an additional premise that many Christians have not clearly established. This is not an argument for simple abolition. It is an argument that the question cannot be avoided by people who take the Imago Dei seriously as the foundation of their ethics.

On war: the church has a long and contested tradition of just war theory, stretching from Augustine through Aquinas and continuing through the twentieth century [cite — confirm]. The consistent ethic does not require pacifism. It requires that the people who are most enthusiastic about military force apply the same scrutiny to it that they apply to abortion — and that the people who are most enthusiastic about anti-war positions apply the same care for the victims of violence in the womb that they apply to the victims of violence in other contexts.

On poverty: Deuteronomy 15:11 commands care for the poor not as a suggestion but as a command: "There will always be poor people in the land. Therefore I command you to be openhanded toward your fellow Israelites who are poor and needy." Matthew 25:40: "Whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me." The consistent ethic requires that the people most passionate about protecting the unborn be equally passionate about the conditions into which those unborn children will be born — the schools, the healthcare, the housing, the poverty programs that will determine whether those children actually flourish.

On immigration: the most repeated command in the Pentateuch, appearing in various forms more than thirty times [cite — confirm], is the command to care for the alien, the stranger, the foreigner in your midst. "You shall not oppress a resident alien; you know the heart of an alien, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt" (Exodus 23:9). A consistent ethic of life that extends dignity to the unborn and denies it to the immigrant has not actually grounded its argument in the Imago Dei. It has grounded it in something else and borrowed the theological vocabulary.

## The Steel-Manned Counter-Position

The honest counter-argument to the consistent ethic is this: moral clarity requires focus. The church that tries to be equally passionate about everything accomplishes nothing in particular. Different moral questions have different levels of urgency — abortion involves the direct taking of life and therefore has a different moral weight than poverty, which involves the failure to provide support. Bernardin's seamless garment was well-intentioned but strategically and theologically naive; it confused a broad ethical orientation with a specific moral argument, and the result was a position so comprehensive it was impossible to advocate coherently.

This argument is serious and deserves a serious answer.

The first response is that the consistent ethic does not require equal tactical emphasis on every application. A pastor can believe that all of these questions fall under the logic of the Imago Dei without believing that he must devote identical airtime to each in every sermon. Consistency of theological principle is not the same as uniformity of pastoral emphasis. What the consistent ethic prohibits is the selective deployment of the Imago Dei as a support for one's preferred political causes while ignoring the obligations it generates in other directions.

The second response is that the argument for focus is too easily applied to justify permanent inaction. Every political coalition uses "strategic focus" to avoid the obligations that fall outside its comfort zone. The consistent ethic asks why a particular coalition has focused on these obligations and not those — and that question is a theological one, not only a strategic one.

## The Cost of Wearing the Seamless Garment

The consistent ethic of life is not politically convenient. It aligns fully with no existing party platform. In the American context, it produces a person who is pro-life on abortion and pro-poor on economic policy, who takes capital punishment seriously as a life issue and takes immigration seriously as a dignity issue, who asks hard questions about military force and hard questions about abortion access, who refuses to let either party absorb his theological convictions entirely.

That person is politically homeless in the current arrangement. He will be accused by the right of being soft on abortion and by the left of being soft on choice. He will be seen by one coalition as a traitor and by another as insufficiently committed. He will lose something in every room he is in.

I am inside this description. I have found it more comfortable to speak on the issues that align with my congregation's existing political positions than on the ones that don't. That comfort is a form of pastoral malpractice I am still working through. The consistent ethic does not let me separate my prophetic voice from my pastoral caution — it asks me to apply both in all the directions the logic leads, not just the ones that cost me nothing with the people in the room.

## The Whole of Scripture

The consistent ethic is not Bernardin's invention. It is the shape of the prophetic tradition in Scripture — a tradition that refused to let Israel's internal piety substitute for its external justice, that indicted Israel for the condition of the widow and orphan with the same passion it applied to idolatry, that understood that the God who demanded clean worship also demanded a society ordered toward the flourishing of its most vulnerable members.

Isaiah, Amos, Micah, Jeremiah — the prophets did not have the luxury of focusing on one application of covenant faithfulness while leaving the others to other advocates. They were called to the whole account. And the whole account is what the church is called to as well.

The consistent ethic of life is not politically convenient. It aligns fully with no party platform, comforts no demographic, and generates obligations in every direction. That is probably the truest sign that it is right.

The seamless garment is uncomfortable to wear precisely because it covers everything.

That is what a garment is for.
