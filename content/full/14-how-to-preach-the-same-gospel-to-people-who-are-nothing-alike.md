Walk into almost any established congregation in America and look at the range of people sitting in the pews. There is the retired teacher who has been attending for forty years and whose faith is formed by a lifetime of Scripture and tradition. There is the millennial couple who came for the first time six weeks ago, who have no church background and came because their neighbor invited them during a hard season in their marriage. There is the college student home for the summer who is somewhere between faith and genuine doubt and has not decided yet whether this Sunday will be their last. There is the single mother who is exhausted and needs something more than information. There is the businessman who came to evaluate whether the pastor is worth his time.

The preacher stands before all of them with one message to give. The gospel is singular. The audience is not. And the tension between those two realities is the central homiletical challenge of every Sunday.

## Why This Is Harder Than It Used to Be

Preaching to diverse congregations has always been demanding, but the complexity has increased significantly in recent decades. The cultural range in a typical congregation has widened. The gap between churched and unchurched backgrounds is larger than it has ever been. The attention span and the information environment in which congregants live have changed the way people receive and process communication. And the political and cultural divisions that have fractured public life have not stopped at the church door.

A sermon that lands powerfully for a fifty-five-year-old elder who grew up in the tradition may be functionally inaccessible to the thirty-year-old who came for the first time. A preaching style that resonates with people who value intellectual engagement may leave the grieving mother cold. The preacher who optimizes for one part of the congregation consistently tends to lose the others — not all at once, but gradually, as they sense that the sermon is not for them.

This is not a new problem. It is Paul's problem in 1 Corinthians 9: "I have become all things to all people, that by all means I might save some" (1 Corinthians 9:22). Paul is not describing shapeshifting or the abandonment of the gospel's content. He is describing a homiletical posture — the willingness to consider the particular hearer and to let that consideration shape the form, though never the substance, of the message. The gospel does not change. The angle of approach does.

## The Historical Arc of the Problem

The early church faced a version of this challenge that makes our contemporary diversity look manageable. The Jewish Christian communities of Jerusalem, the Gentile communities of Antioch and Corinth and Ephesus, the Greek philosophical milieu of Athens — each required a different entry point into the same news about Jesus of Nazareth. Paul's sermon on Mars Hill in Acts 17 begins with the altar to an unknown God. His letter to the Romans begins with natural revelation. His sermon in the synagogue at Antioch of Pisidia in Acts 13 begins with the Exodus. Three different openings. One proclamation.

What Paul understood, and what the homiletical tradition has periodically recovered and periodically lost, is that the point of contact is not the same as the point of arrival. The point of contact must be where the hearer actually is. The point of arrival is always the same: the crucified and risen Christ, the forgiveness of sins, the possibility of genuine reconciliation with God and each other.

Tim Keller spent three decades at Redeemer Presbyterian Church in New York building a congregation of people who were explicitly drawn from the skeptical, educated, urban professional class — people who had left the tradition or never been in it — without abandoning the theological substance of Reformed Christianity [cite — confirm]. His model was not to simplify the gospel for the secular audience but to do the patient cultural translation work that made the gospel's claims legible to people who thought they had already heard and dismissed it. That is the homiletical challenge in its most demanding form.

## Preaching to the Text, Not the Type

One of the most reliable approaches to diverse-congregation preaching is a thorough, text-centered method that allows the Scripture itself to make its claims before the preacher shapes those claims for the audience. When the sermon begins with honest, careful attention to what the text is actually saying — in its original context, with its original force — it has a chance to do what sermons optimized for specific audiences often cannot: surprise everyone.

The person who came expecting to be confirmed in what they already believed gets disrupted by what the text actually says. The person who came expecting to be dismissed by religious language encounters something that speaks to their actual condition. The text, taken seriously on its own terms, has a range that no preacher's audience analysis can fully anticipate. Trust it.

This is not a license for historical or linguistic obscurity. The congregation does not need to know the Hebrew lexeme to encounter its force. But the preacher who has genuinely sat with the text — who has been surprised by it, troubled by it, met by it — preaches with a quality of attention that communicates itself across enormous differences in theological background and life circumstance. The congregation can feel the difference between a preacher who has done the exegetical work and one who is using the text as a launching pad for what they already wanted to say.

## The Art of the Layered Sermon

Skilled preachers who serve diverse congregations often learn to build layered sermons — messages that work at multiple levels of familiarity and engagement simultaneously. The surface is accessible: clear, concrete, illustrative, connected to ordinary human experience. Beneath the surface are implications that deepen for those who are prepared to go deeper. The theological substance is present and real, but it does not require prior theological formation to access the primary impact of the message.

This is not dumbing down. It is the recognition that every person in the room is a full human being who deserves the full truth of the gospel communicated in a way that genuinely reaches them. The preacher who uses inaccessible vocabulary is not being more faithful — they are being less attentive.

The layered sermon requires what Eugene Peterson called "the contemplative pastor" — the leader who has spent enough time in Scripture and prayer and honest attention to human beings that they can speak at the surface and the depth simultaneously without splitting the sermon into two different messages [cite — confirm]. The surface and the depth have to be the same thing, expressed differently. This is the work.

## The Counter-Position Worth Considering

There is a preaching philosophy — associated with certain strands of Reformed and confessional tradition — that holds that the congregation's diversity is not a homiletical problem to be solved but a reality to be accepted: the preacher's job is to preach the text faithfully, without audience optimization, and trust the Holy Spirit to do the particular work of application in each hearer. On this view, the attempt to tailor the message for diverse audiences is itself a form of unbelief — a failure to trust that the word of God is "living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword" (Hebrews 4:12) and therefore does not require the preacher's help to reach the skeptic or the grieving mother or the college student on the edge of leaving.

This position has genuine theological weight. The word does work that the preacher cannot. The sovereignty of the Holy Spirit in application is real. And there are preachers who have preached for decades in a single register, ignoring the diversity of their congregations, and have nonetheless seen genuine and lasting transformation.

But the position fails as a homiletical method when it is used to justify pastoral inattentiveness — when the refusal to consider the hearer is dressed as theological conviction rather than acknowledged as preference or limitation. The incarnation itself is a statement about context: the eternal Word became flesh in a particular time, a particular culture, a particular language. Not because flesh is superior to spirit, but because the hearer is embodied and the Word's reaching them required that attention to particularity. The preacher who ignores particularity is not being more faithful to the incarnation. They are being less.

## The Non-Negotiable of Genuine Humanity

Perhaps the single most universally connecting element in any sermon, regardless of the theological formation or cultural background of the congregation, is the preacher's genuine humanity. The sermon that begins or ends with a moment of honest vulnerability — not manufactured emotion, but real acknowledgment that the preacher is wrestling with the same human realities as the congregation — tends to build the kind of trust that makes the rest of the message receivable.

People from wildly different backgrounds and stages of faith share one thing: they are human beings who are struggling, hoping, wondering, and trying to make sense of their lives. Preach to that. Let the gospel address that. The gospel, after all, is not ultimately for any particular demographic. It is for people. And every room you preach in is full of them.

Self-implication is the technical name for what this requires of the preacher. Not confession as spectacle. Not vulnerability performed for effect. But the honest presence of a person who is inside the same struggle they are describing, who is not pointing at the congregation from a position of arrival but speaking from within the ongoing reality of trying to believe what the text claims. The retired teacher and the college student on the edge of leaving and the grieving mother and the skeptical businessman — none of them needs a preacher who has it figured out. They need a preacher who is still working it out, with enough honesty that they can see the work actually happening.

The Sermon on the Mount — the most universal and most demanding homiletical unit in the New Testament — begins not with doctrine but with condition: "Blessed are the poor in spirit" (Matthew 5:3). The entry point is human poverty. The room full of people who are nothing alike are united at the level of their poverty. Begin there. Trust the text to do the rest.

The gospel does not need a narrower audience. It needs a more honest preacher. Those two things — the breadth of the audience and the honesty of the preacher — are not in tension. They are the same solution.
