Every generation believes it is the exception to the rules of biblical interpretation. Every generation is convinced that they finally understand Scripture correctly — that the errors and distortions of previous readers were products of their time and their limitations, and that we, standing on the accumulated knowledge of history, reading with the tools of modern scholarship, informed by a more complete understanding of justice and human dignity, have at last arrived at what the text actually says.

Every generation is wrong about this in ways it cannot see from inside the generation.

The Pharisees were certain. They had memorized the text, elaborated its application across generations of commentary, built an entire tradition of interpretation that they believed faithfully preserved the intent of Moses and the prophets. They were wrong about the most important thing the text was doing — wrong not because they were ignorant but because they were certain in ways that foreclosed the recognition of what they were missing. Jesus told them: "You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me, yet you refuse to come to me that you may have life" (John 5:39-40).

The medieval church built a theological architecture of extraordinary sophistication — Thomas Aquinas, the development of doctrine, the integration of Aristotelian philosophy with Christian revelation. The church of 1300 was not intellectually primitive. It had tremendous resources. And it used those resources to defend practices — the sale of indulgences, the exclusion of the laity from Scripture, the authority of tradition as co-equal with the text — that the Reformers found catastrophically wrong. The Reformers were right about much of the critique and wrong about some of what they built in response.

American evangelicals are certain. I am certain. And we are wrong about more than we are right about, though I cannot tell you precisely which things — that is the nature of the problem.

---

The Bible does not change. Our interpretation of it does. And that fact should produce in every reader a specific kind of humility — not the humility that abandons conviction, but the humility that holds conviction without treating it as infallible, that distinguishes between what the text says and what my reading of it says, that remains genuinely open to correction.

The room you are in — your culture, your fears, your assumptions, your political moment, your economic interest, the community that shaped you — shapes how you read Scripture. This is not a problem to solve by reading more carefully. It is a condition of being a finite creature reading an infinite text. The question is not whether you will be conditioned. The question is whether you will be honest about it.

---

The history of biblical interpretation is not primarily a history of malicious distortion. It is primarily a history of sincere readings that were shaped — invisibly, often — by the interests and assumptions of the reader.

Slaveholders in the antebellum American South did not generally read the Bible cynically. They read it sincerely, and they found what they were looking for, because the text is large enough that a reader looking for permission can usually find a passage that provides it. The "curse of Ham" (Genesis 9:20-27) was interpreted for centuries to justify the subjugation of African peoples — an interpretation that required ignoring the plain context of the passage, the identity of the descendants in question, and the overwhelming trajectory of the canon toward the dignity of every human being as image-bearer. But the interpreters were not stupid. They were brilliant men, some of them, whose brilliant interpretation served their economic interest and their cultural position so completely that they could not see the service.

That is the pattern. The distortions of biblical interpretation are rarely produced by contempt for the text. They are produced by sincere readers whose formation — whose culture, their relationships, their position in the existing order — has already decided what the text must mean before the reading begins. The interpretation then confirms what was already assumed, and the assumption feels like discovery.

---

Consider the specific mechanisms.

We privilege the passages that address our concerns and read past the ones that address our blind spots. The preacher whose congregation is primarily wealthy will preach extensively on spiritual poverty and almost never on economic poverty, because the economic content of the gospel is uncomfortable to the people who fill the seats and fund the ministry. The passages are there — hundreds of them. They will be read on Sunday mornings as part of the lectionary or the reading plan. They will rarely become the weight-bearing structure of the sermon.

We interpret through our fears. The second-century church, terrified of Gnosticism's dissolution of the material world, read the resurrection accounts in ways that emphasized bodily physicality in response to Gnostic denial. The twentieth-century church, terrified of communism, read the passages on private property and individual responsibility with an intensity it did not bring to the passages on wealth and the poor. The fear is real. The text addresses real concerns. But fear is a lens, and lenses distort.

We read with our communities. The community of interpretation — the theological tradition, the denomination, the local congregation — shapes what is visible in the text and what is invisible. A person formed in the Reformed tradition and a person formed in the Pentecostal tradition will read the same chapter of Acts and notice different things, weight different emphases, find different centers of gravity. Neither is simply wrong. Both are reading from inside a tradition that has selected for certain things and against others.

We read against our enemies. The passages most weaponized in American culture-war Christianity are the ones that address behaviors associated with the opposing tribe. The passages that address the behaviors most characteristic of the tribe doing the reading receive considerably less attention. This is not unique to one side. It is the universal human tendency to read the prophets' indictment as aimed at someone else.

---

The honest counter-position deserves a full hearing.

The argument runs: if every reading is conditioned by the reader's context, then there is no authoritative reading — only competing interpretations, none more valid than another. This leads to interpretive relativism, in which the text means whatever the reader brings to it, and the church loses any capacity to say that some interpretations are wrong. The result is the kind of theological drift that has characterized the progressive churches — where Scripture gets reinterpreted until it endorses whatever the current cultural consensus holds, and the prophetic distance between the gospel and the culture collapses entirely.

This is a serious argument and it names a real danger. The solution to the problem of conditioned reading is not to abandon the claim that the text has a meaning that exists independent of the reader. It is to pursue that meaning with all available tools — careful exegesis, attention to historical context, the witness of the broader tradition, the correction of the community, the illumination of the Spirit — while holding the results with the honesty that any finite reader is still a finite reader.

The tradition is not useless. The creeds were not written by people who had stopped caring what the text actually said. The councils of the early church — wrestling with the nature of Christ, the Trinity, the relationship between the testaments — were doing exegetical work, even when they were also doing political work. The tradition is the cumulative reading of the church across time, and it has real authority — not as a substitute for the text but as a correction of the individual reader's inevitable distortions.

The Reformation's *sola scriptura* was not a claim that each individual reader could determine the text's meaning in isolation. It was a claim that the text stood in judgment over the tradition, that tradition could not add to Scripture what Scripture did not authorize. Even that claim required a community to apply it — the community of Reformed interpretation, accountable to the same text, correcting each other's readings over centuries.

The answer to conditioned reading is not epistemic humility that dissolves into relativism. It is the specific humility of a reader who knows they are conditioned and seeks, by every available means, to be corrected — by the text's own internal logic, by the witness of the historically marginalized whose reading of the same text looks different from the majority reading, by the accumulated wisdom of the tradition, by the willingness to be wrong.

---

The practical implication is not abstract.

Read the parts of the Bible that make you uncomfortable. The parts that are addressed to someone who looks like you in your position of relative comfort, who is doing something that people in your position typically do, and who is told by the prophet or the apostle that it is not acceptable. Those passages are not aimed at your enemy. They are aimed at you. Read them slowly. Read them with the assumption that they mean what they say before you begin finding the reasons they must mean something else.

Read with people who are unlike you. The tradition of reading that has been formed entirely within one culture, one class, one racial or national community is a tradition that has consistently filtered out the things that community preferred not to see. The Black church's reading of Exodus is not less valid than the white evangelical reading of Romans. It is, in many ways, more dangerous — more accurate to the raw experience of the text because it has been read by people for whom the experience of bondage and liberation is not metaphorical.

Read with the honest question: what would I need to give up if this passage means what it appears to mean? If the answer to that question is something you are not willing to give up, you have identified the lens.

---

The Pharisees were not bad people. They were people who had confused their reading of the text with the text itself — who had so thoroughly identified their interpretive tradition with divine authority that when the thing the text was pointing toward arrived, they could not see him.

That is not a first-century problem. It is the permanent temptation of every community of faith that has lived with the text long enough to feel at home in it. The familiarity is a gift. It is also a danger.

The Bible does not change. But our interpretation of it does. And the room you are reading it in — right now, today, in this particular moment of American history, inside whatever fears and hopes and cultural formations have shaped you — that room is shaping what you see.

You are not the exception to this. Neither am I. The question is whether we will be humble enough to know it — and honest enough to let that knowledge do something.
