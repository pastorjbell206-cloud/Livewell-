There is a version of the American story that goes like this: anyone who works hard enough can get an education, and education is the ladder out of poverty, and the ladder is available to everyone. We have told this story for so long and so loudly that many of us have stopped noticing what it requires us not to see.

What it requires us not to see is the funding structure. In most of the United States, public school funding is tied substantially to local property tax revenue. This means that a child who lives in a wealthy suburb attends a school with more resources per pupil than a child who lives in a low-income neighborhood. The structural outcome is that the children who most need educational investment receive the least of it, and the children who would succeed in almost any environment receive the most. The ladder is real. The rungs are not evenly spaced.

This is not a new observation. Jonathan Kozol documented it with shattering specificity in *Savage Inequalities*, published in 1991 [cite — confirm year], comparing per-pupil spending in wealthy and poor school districts in cities like Chicago, Camden, and East St. Louis. What he found was not merely difference but a difference that tracked almost perfectly with race and class — affluent white districts spending two to three times as much per student as poor Black and Latino districts in the same metropolitan area. The numbers have shifted over the decades, but the basic structure has not.

The church has, by and large, filed this under "policy question" and moved on. That is an error.

---

Begin with the biblical frame, because this is not primarily a policy question. It is a theological one.

Proverbs 22:6 is often quoted in parenting discussions: "Start children off on the way they should go, and even when they are old they will not turn from it." But the broader context of Proverbs is a vision of wisdom as the foundation of human flourishing — and wisdom, in the Hebrew tradition, is not purely spiritual. It is practical, material, cultivated through instruction and formation across years. The investment in children's learning is an investment in their capacity for tsedaqah — right-relatedness to God, neighbor, and community. To systematically under-invest in the formation of some children while over-investing in others is not just bad policy. It is a violation of the vision of shalom — the comprehensive flourishing of the whole community — that runs through the Hebrew prophets.

Isaiah 58:6–7 names what the fast God actually requires: "to loose the chains of injustice and untie the cords of the yoke, to set the oppressed free and break every yoke... to share your food with the hungry and to provide the poor wanderer with shelter." The "cords of the yoke" in the context of education inequality are the structural mechanisms that keep poor children tied to poor outcomes — not by accident, not by individual failure, but by system. The gleaning laws in Leviticus 19 built structural provision for the vulnerable into the economic order. The question is what the equivalent structural provision looks like in an educational economy.

The prophet Micah's indictment in Micah 2:1–2 is precise: "Woe to those who plan iniquity, to those who plot evil on their beds! At morning's light they carry it out because it is in their power to do it. They covet fields and seize them, and houses, and take them. They defraud people of their homes, they rob them of their inheritance." The inheritance seized is not always land. Sometimes it is the education that would have opened every subsequent door.

---

The property-tax funding structure has a history that cannot be separated from the history of race in America. The residential segregation of American cities — enforced through redlining, racially restrictive covenants, and discriminatory mortgage lending practices that persisted through the mid-twentieth century [cite — confirm timeline] — deliberately concentrated Black and Latino families in lower-value property. Since school funding followed property value, segregated housing produced segregated educational resources. The mechanism was not legally identified as racial discrimination. But the outcome was.

The Supreme Court took this question up in *San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez* in 1973 [cite — confirm year], in which a group of Mexican American families in a poor Texas school district challenged the property-tax funding system as a violation of equal protection. The Court ruled 5-4 that education was not a fundamental right under the federal Constitution and that wealth-based disparities in school funding did not constitute unconstitutional discrimination [cite — confirm]. The decision effectively left the funding structure intact and left the challenge to individual states.

Some states have done significant work on equalization since then. Many have not. The basic pattern — that where you are born substantially determines the quality of education you receive — has remained remarkably persistent.

---

What does this mean for children concretely? It means some children arrive at kindergarten already behind, attend schools with larger class sizes, less experienced teachers, fewer counselors, deteriorating facilities, and outdated materials — and then are told that their lower test scores reflect their effort and ability rather than their circumstances. The meritocracy narrative requires the pretense that the starting conditions are comparable. They are not.

James K.A. Smith has argued, following Bourdieu, that formation is always embodied and environmental — that we become what we habitually practice, and that the habits we are able to practice depend significantly on the environments in which we develop [cite — confirm specific work]. A child in a well-resourced school environment is being formed — in habits of inquiry, in confidence, in relational skills, in the experience of institutional care — differently than a child in an under-resourced one. This is not primarily about test scores. It is about who you become over twelve years of daily formation in an institution.

The church talks about discipleship as formation. It does not always apply the same logic to the question of what schools form in children, and in whose children, and at whose expense.

---

The steel-man position here is worth engaging honestly. It goes like this: the evidence on what produces educational outcomes is genuinely complicated. Per-pupil spending correlates with outcomes in some studies and less strongly in others [cite — confirm state of research]. Teacher quality matters enormously, and throwing money at under-resourced schools without addressing the conditions that make good teachers leave those schools is insufficient. The problem of education inequality is real, but the solution is not simply equalized funding — it requires more comprehensive thinking about poverty, family structure, community stability, and the conditions that support learning.

This is a serious argument. Funding alone is not the answer. Teacher retention, school leadership, curricular quality, family stability, community investment — all of these matter, and some of them are not solved by funding formulas. The complexity here is genuine.

But notice the move again: the genuine complexity of solutions is used to forestall prophetic clarity about the injustice itself. The fact that solutions are complicated does not mean the pattern is not unjust. The fact that equalized funding is insufficient does not mean current funding inequity is acceptable. The complexity of the answer does not dissolve the clarity of the problem.

Amos was not offering a twelve-point economic reform plan. He was naming what was wrong. "They trample on the heads of the poor as on the dust of the ground and deny justice to the oppressed" (Amos 2:7). The naming is the prophetic work. The policy development follows the naming. The church that waits for a perfectly worked-out policy answer before naming the injustice will wait forever and call the waiting careful stewardship.

---

There is a dimension of this that the church encounters directly in its own communities: the way educational inequality shapes who shows up in its pews and who does not, what assumptions its teaching makes about literacy and access and cultural competence, and what it communicates to families whose children are in failing schools about whether the church sees them and their circumstances.

The congregation that has no idea what the school funding situation is in its own neighborhood has not been paying attention to the people it claims to love. The pastor who knows the name of every member's adult child's career but does not know whether the neighborhood elementary school has current textbooks has his attention organized around the wrong center.

Paying attention to the conditions of children's lives in the community is not an optional add-on to pastoral care. It is part of what it means to love your neighbors concretely rather than abstractly.

---

The New Testament is insistent that faith without works is dead (James 2:17). The works James is describing in that letter are not generalized spiritual activities — they are the specific, material responses to specific, material needs. He has just given the example of a person who is cold and hungry and is told, warmly, to be warm and fed without receiving warm clothes or food. The theological point is not subtle. The difference between a living faith and a dead one is whether it produces the thing it claims to care about.

If the church claims to care about children — and it does, loudly, in many contexts — then it has to ask what it means to care about children whose educational circumstances are structured against them. Not just their children. All children. Including the ones in the ZIP codes that do not share a property tax base with the church's neighborhood.

The vision of the kingdom that Jesus announced in Luke 4:18–19 — good news to the poor, liberty to the captives, recovery of sight to the blind — is a vision that reaches into every system that binds people. Educational systems that concentrate disadvantage in poor communities of color bind people. The vision of liberty the church proclaims is not just spiritual. It has always had material dimensions. The prophets knew this. The church's amnesia about it is a recent development.

Children in underfunded schools are not failing. They are being failed. There is a difference, and every institution that has the standing to name it and does not has made a choice.

The ladder is real. The church's job is to say plainly that some of its rungs are missing — and to ask, with prophetic seriousness, who took them.
