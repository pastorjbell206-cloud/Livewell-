The commission is specific. It begins in Ezekiel 34.

God speaks to the shepherds of Israel who have fed themselves on the flock rather than feeding it. Who have not strengthened the weak. Who have not healed the sick or bound up the injured or brought back the strayed or sought the lost. The scattered sheep, God says, have become prey — and God holds the shepherds accountable. Not sympathetically. With fury. "I am against the shepherds, and I will require my sheep at their hand" (Ezekiel 34:10).

The scandal of abuse in Christian institutions is not a modern problem with a modern cause. It is the ancient failure of shepherds who served themselves, and the ancient pattern of structures that protected the shepherd's position over the sheep's life. What is modern is the scale of documentation, the courage of survivors who refused to stay quiet, and the public accounting that has followed. The reports on Sovereign Grace Ministries, the Southern Baptist Convention abuse investigation that surfaced hundreds of documented cases over twenty years, the investigations across multiple Catholic dioceses — [cite — confirm] these are not peripheral events in the history of the church. They are the same event: institutionalized protection of abusers and systematic silencing of those they harmed.

I have to say something before I continue arguing.

I have served on committees. I have participated in conversations where the primary concern, before anyone named the harm done to a person, was what this would mean for the church's reputation and the ministry's continuation. I knew, in those rooms, that something was wrong with the priorities. I did not name it with the directness the moment required. I know what it is to choose institution over person while telling yourself you are managing the situation wisely. That was not wisdom. That was complicity with a pattern I am here naming as sin.

The structural argument deserves to be made plainly. Institutions protect themselves. This is not a characteristic peculiar to religious institutions — every organization develops self-preservation instincts that can override its stated purpose. But the church faces a particular corruption when it operates this way, because its stated purpose is the care of the vulnerable, and the one against whom its institutional instincts are activated is precisely the person its purpose requires it to protect. The abuser is a threat to the institution's reputation. The victim is also a threat to the institution's reputation. The institution's response — historically, repeatedly, documentably — has been to manage both threats, which in practice means removing the abuser quietly and pressuring the victim to silence.

That management is theological error of the most serious kind. It treats reputation as a value higher than the truth. It treats the institution's survival as a value higher than the person's dignity. It treats the abuser's continuity as a pastoral concern more urgent than the survivor's justice.

The gospel cannot survive that logic. A community organized around a God who took on flesh and suffered and was vindicated against the powers that killed him cannot simultaneously be an institution that sacrifices the suffering to protect the powerful. Not without losing the capacity to proclaim what it claims to believe.

---

There is a particular theological distortion that enables this pattern, and it deserves to be named directly. It is the weaponization of grace.

The logic runs like this: we are all sinners. The abuser has sinned. He is also a brother in Christ. Grace requires that we forgive. Forgiveness requires that we not destroy a man's ministry. Therefore the institutional response to abuse is to offer private correction, require private repentance, and manage the situation in a way that allows ministry to continue while protecting the victim — through confidentiality agreements, through the subtle pressure of Christian community, through appeals to forgiveness as the measure of a survivor's spiritual health.

This is not grace. It is the counterfeit of grace, deployed in the service of institutional self-protection.

Paul's letter to the Corinthians addresses a case of sexual immorality within the congregation with a directness that should disturb any pastor who has been tempted toward managed transitions and quiet arrangements. "Purge the evil person from among you" (1 Corinthians 5:13). Paul is quoting Deuteronomy, and he knows it. The continuity between the testaments on this point is not accidental. Communities that harbor the predator in order to protect the institution's reputation are not acting in grace. They are acting in the same cowardice the Deuteronomic law was designed to prevent.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote in *Life Together* about the difference between the community that tells people what they want to hear and the community that is willing to name the truth at cost. He called cheap grace the deadliest enemy of the church — not because it was graceless but because it was grace without cost, reconciliation without genuine confrontation with the wrong that required reconciliation. [cite — confirm] What passes for pastoral sensitivity in many abuse cases is cheap grace of the most cynical variety: absolution for the powerful, silence for the powerless, and the institutional reputation intact.

The survivor who approaches a church leadership team expecting to be heard and protected, and instead finds herself encouraged toward forgiveness as a precondition of being believed — that woman has not been treated with grace. She has been managed. The church has protected itself at her expense and called it ministry.

---

The historical record here is not obscure. It requires only the willingness to look.

The pattern of institutional self-protection in the face of clergy abuse has been documented across traditions — Catholic, Southern Baptist, non-denominational evangelical, charismatic networks, independent churches. [cite — confirm] The particulars differ. The mechanism is identical: the institution's need to protect its reputation, its leadership, and its financial stability overrides the institution's theological commitment to justice and the protection of the vulnerable. The hierarchy closes ranks. The survivor is isolated. The abuser is moved, counseled, quietly retired, or given a reference that does not disclose what the institution knows.

This has happened not because these institutions were staffed by uniquely wicked people. It has happened because institutions, by their nature, develop survival instincts, and because the culture of deference to pastoral authority that many Christian communities have cultivated creates conditions where abuse can operate without detection and disclosure can be punished by the very community that should receive it.

Power without accountability is not just a management problem. It is a theological problem. The New Testament model of leadership is not the unchecked authority of a singular figure but the shared accountability of a plurality of elders, the congregation's capacity to name and address sin among its members, and the structural principle that no one in the community is above the truth. When the senior pastor is above accountability — when the board exists to affirm his vision rather than to genuinely oversee his conduct, when the staff are financially dependent on his approval, when the congregation has been formed to conflate loyalty to the pastor with loyalty to God — the conditions for abuse are already present, waiting only for a person willing to exploit them.

---

What justice requires here is not complicated, though it is costly.

Mandatory reporting. Not discretionary reporting, not prayerful discernment about whether this rises to the level requiring outside intervention, not the pastoral judgment call made by the same institution that has an interest in the outcome. Mandatory. To civil authorities. Before internal processes begin, not after they have already shaped the narrative.

Independent accountability structures with genuine authority — not advisory bodies that report to the leaders they are supposed to oversee. If the body investigating abuse reports to the senior pastor, it is not an accountability structure. It is a loyalty performance.

Survivor-centered processes rather than institution-centered ones. This means asking first what the person who was harmed needs, not what the institution needs in order to manage the situation. It means the survivor's account is received before it is evaluated. It means the burden of proof is not placed on the person with less power.

Named accountability, not managed transitions to other ministry contexts. The pastor who abused and is moved to another congregation without disclosure is not being given a second chance. He is being given a new supply of victims. This is not pastoral generosity. It is the creation of conditions for the harm to continue.

None of these requirements are uniquely demanding in light of what the church claims about the God it serves. They are the minimum institutional integrity that basic decency would require, even apart from the theological framework that makes the failure so catastrophic.

Psalm 82 pictures God standing in the divine council and indicting the rulers of the earth for failing to "defend the poor and the fatherless, do justice to the afflicted and the needy, rescue the weak and the needy" from the hand of the wicked (Psalm 82:3–4). The rulers of the earth. The ones with the power to protect. God holds them responsible for what they fail to prevent and what they actively enable.

The psalmist ends with a cry for God to rise and judge the earth, because the rulers have failed. The prayer is not naive about the likelihood of institutional reform. It is addressed to the only judge who cannot be managed.

What God requires of the church — and what the church has failed to provide — is the courage to say out loud what it already knows in private. The church knows that abuse happens in its spaces. It knows that its structures can enable rather than prevent it. It knows that the patterns of deference and silence that have served the institution have not served the people in its care.

The silence is not neutral. The silence is participation in the harm.

Naming it is the beginning of something that might, over time, look like faithfulness. Not comfort. Not reputation. Not managed stability.

Something that might, at cost, look like the shepherd who goes after the one who was lost, rather than the shepherd who counts heads on Sunday morning and calls it care.

The sheep who was taken were not lost. They were taken. The shepherd who says otherwise has not yet faced Ezekiel 34.
