title: "5. Ending Every Sermon With the Same Application Structure"

---

If your sermons consistently end with three practical steps, or a salvation invitation, or a call to join a small group, or any other fixed application structure, you are training your congregation to anticipate the landing rather than be genuinely surprised by where the text takes them.

The congregation learns this faster than the preacher realizes. After a few months — sometimes a few weeks — the experienced attender knows what is coming. They can feel when the third point is being made, when the illustration is wrapping up, when the shift to application is about to occur. The sermon becomes a format they navigate rather than a word that arrives. And once that happens, the preaching has lost its capacity to disrupt, because disruption requires surprise — and the structure has made the ending predictable.

This is not a minor craft problem. It is a formation problem.

---

The text does not always end in three steps. That is the fundamental issue.

The Psalms end in lament, in doxology, in the raw undisguised cry of a man who cannot find God and says so. They do not resolve into action items. They end where they end — in the presence of the irresolution, in the refusal to pretend the problem is tidier than it is. Psalm 22 opens with "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1) and moves through desolation to praise — but the praise does not erase the desolation; it holds alongside it, and the reader is meant to feel both at once. There is no three-step application at the end of that psalm that does justice to what happened in it.

The prophetic texts end in judgment, or in hope that arrives only after the judgment, or in the kind of ambiguity that requires the reader to sit inside the tension rather than resolve it. Lamentations ends with a question: "Or have you utterly rejected us? Are you exceedingly angry with us?" (Lamentations 5:22). That is the last verse. God does not answer. The ending is open and weighted and it does not offer comfort. A preacher who structures the application around comfort has not preached Lamentations. He has replaced it.

The New Testament letters were written to congregations in specific crises. When Paul writes to the Corinthians about the Lord's Supper (1 Corinthians 11:17-34), his application is particular, sharp, aimed at a specific failure in a specific community at a specific moment. When he writes to the Romans about the righteousness of God (Romans 1-8), the logic builds across chapters toward a cry — "Who will deliver me from this body of death? Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!" (Romans 7:24-25) — that is a verdict, not an action item. The sermon that ends at the same application structure regardless of the text has not followed the text. It has imposed its own shape on it.

---

The seduction toward fixed structures is real, and any honest preacher should name it.

Consistent structure generates consistent feedback. Congregants who leave with three things to do this week feel equipped. They can report to their spouses what the sermon was about. They feel that the hour was productive — that the time spent yielded something actionable. Preachers receive more positive feedback from structured sermons than from sermons that leave the congregation in the unsettled territory the text actually inhabits.

There is also the internal need for closure. Preaching is a high-pressure act. The sermon has to end somewhere, and ending with a clear application structure gives the preacher a landing strip. The alternative — ending in the weight of the text itself, trusting the congregation to carry what cannot be resolved — requires a confidence that the structure relieves.

And some application structures are genuinely good. The salvation invitation at the end of an evangelistic sermon is not wrong in the right context; it is responsive to what the text and the moment call for. The call to a specific practice can be exactly what a passage demands. The problem is not application. The problem is the application structure that does not change because the preacher has decided in advance what shape every sermon should take.

---

Consider the pastoral scene this produces over time.

A woman in the congregation has been in a marriage that is quietly failing for three years. Her husband is not violent, not unfaithful — just absent, present in body and missing in every way that matters. She has been in the pew through that entire season. She has heard dozens of sermons. She has received scores of application structures — three things to do, four habits to develop, five ways to strengthen your relationship with God.

None of them have touched what is actually happening in her. Not because the sermons were theologically wrong but because the form has trained her to receive content and process it, rather than to be exposed — genuinely exposed — to a God who might say something unexpected and unwelcome and true directly into her specific situation. The predictable landing has given her a place to process information. What she needed was a sermon that did not give her a place to land — that put her in the presence of something she couldn't manage.

The sermon that formed her was the one that ended in tension, that didn't offer a resolution she could take home and file away, that left her carrying a question she had to keep living inside. Those are the sermons she still remembers. The ones with the clean application structures are indistinguishable from each other.

---

The preacher who wants to break this habit will face an uncomfortable interim period.

Congregations that have been trained on consistent structure will initially find its absence disorienting. They will give the preacher slightly less confident feedback. They may not be able to articulate what the sermon was about in the same efficient way. Some will report that the sermon felt incomplete. All of that is data — but it is data about the formation the congregation has already received, not data about what the sermon needed to do.

The preacher's task is not to produce the sermon that generates the most confident immediate feedback. It is to form the kind of people who can live faithfully in the actual texture of their lives — which are not organized into three actionable steps, which do not resolve on a predictable schedule, which require the capacity to sit inside difficulty without the comfort of a structure that tells them what to do next.

Preaching is one of the primary means God uses to form that capacity. Or it can be. When the structure preempts the text, the capacity atrophies.

---

The counter-position deserves its due.

Some will argue that application structures serve people who are genuinely new to Scripture — that the person who has never read the Bible needs a clear entrance ramp, that to end in ambiguity is to fail the person who came in confused and leaves more confused. There is something to this. Not every sermon is the same kind of sermon, and not every congregation is in the same place. A pastor who always ends in unresolved tension has overcorrected in the other direction.

But the argument proves too much if it becomes the defense of the fixed structure. The congregation that is always given a clear landing never develops the theological muscles to stand without one. The faith that requires every sermon to tell it what to do is a faith that will collapse the moment the sermon structure is not available — which is most of life. Monday through Saturday, the preacher is not there. What the congregation needs is not more steps. It is more capacity — the deep interior formation that comes from years of genuine encounter with the text in its full texture.

That formation is what great preaching actually produces. Not information transfer. Not inspiration that fades by Tuesday. The slow, cumulative reshaping of how a person sees God, suffers loss, uses power, loves difficulty, reads their own heart. That reshaping does not happen through three-step application structures. It happens through sermons that are formally responsive to the specific passage — that end where the text ends, apply what the text applies, demand what the text demands, and trust the congregation to carry what cannot be neatly resolved.

---

The habit is worth breaking. Not as a stylistic adjustment but as a theological one.

If the application structure is predetermined and consistent, the sermon is shaped by the structure rather than the text. And a sermon shaped by something other than the text is, at the deepest level, a sermon about something other than what the text is about — regardless of how many verses appeared in it.

These are honest words, and they are offered in the spirit that the Pastors Connection Network tries to embody: not as critique from a distance, but as the kind of honest, collegial conversation that pastors need from people who genuinely care about the quality of their ministry.

The preacher who sits down with a text and asks first, *what does this text demand of the ending* — not *how do I fit this into the structure I always use* — is already doing something different. They are letting the text set the terms. They are trusting that the God who inspired the Psalms' open-ended laments and the prophets' unresolved cries and the apocalyptic visions that refuse to become manageable — trusting that this God knows what he is asking a congregation to carry home, and that it is sufficient.

The congregation does not need to know what to do next. They need to be formed. Those are different things, and the sermon that understands the difference will shape a people who can actually live in the world as it is — which is the only world where the gospel has to work.

Let the text end where it ends. Trust what it leaves open. That is not carelessness. That is fidelity.
