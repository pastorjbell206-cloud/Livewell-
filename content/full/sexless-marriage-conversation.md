There is a number that marriage researchers use: fewer than ten times a year. Below that threshold, the clinical language is "sexless marriage." The research suggests it describes somewhere between fifteen and twenty percent of marriages at any given time, and that the actual number is likely higher, because this is not a topic people report accurately [cite — confirm].

It is also not a topic most pastors address. The silence from the pulpit on this specific subject is near-total, which means the couple experiencing it has nowhere in their faith community to bring it. They carry it alone, each in their own interpretation of what it means, each accumulating a story about the marriage and the other person that may or may not be accurate.

The conversation that does not happen is not only costing them intimacy. It is costing them the chance to understand what the absence is actually about.

## The Biblical Vision That Makes the Silence Indefensible

Song of Solomon 7:10 — "I am my beloved's and his desire is for me" — is a statement of mutual desire as the natural condition of a covenant marriage. The biblical vision of sex in marriage is not merely permissive; it is expectant. The entire arc of Song of Solomon treats erotic desire as a gift embedded in the creation order, not a concession to human weakness. The church's historical discomfort with the book's frankness has sometimes led to allegorical readings that evacuate its most immediate content. But the most straightforward reading is also the most theologically productive: God made human beings with bodies, with desire, with the capacity for pleasure in each other, and that making is called very good (Genesis 1:31).

Paul's instruction in 1 Corinthians 7:3-5 is explicit and anatomically frank in a way that surprises first-time readers: "The husband should give to his wife her conjugal rights, and likewise the wife to her husband. For the wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does. Likewise the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does. Do not deprive one another, except perhaps by agreement for a limited time, that you may devote yourselves to prayer; but then come together again, so that Satan may not tempt you because of your lack of self-control."

The instruction is mutual — notice that the authority runs in both directions, a genuinely countercultural statement in a Greco-Roman context where the husband's authority over the wife's body was assumed and the reverse was not. The implied baseline is regular, mutually engaged sexual intimacy. Paul treats its absence not as a neutral condition but as a vulnerability that requires specific justification and a defined endpoint.

**The body keeps honest what the conversation has avoided. What a couple is not doing in the bedroom is usually connected to what they are not saying in the kitchen.**

## The Historical Weight of Church Silence

The church's silence on this topic has deep roots. The patristic period produced a theology of sexuality in which the body was a problematic inheritance from the fall rather than a gift from creation. Augustine's influential treatment of concupiscence — the disordered desire that marks fallen sexuality — cast a long shadow over the tradition's capacity to affirm sexual desire within marriage as genuinely good [cite — confirm]. The shadow persists. Many Christians who have grown up in churches that preached purity culture have internalized a view of the body as primarily dangerous rather than primarily good — a site of temptation rather than a vehicle of covenantal connection.

This formation does real damage to marriages. A person who has spent their formative years learning to manage and suppress sexual desire in order to be holy does not automatically reverse that training when they receive a marriage certificate. The suppression continues. The body that was managed as a spiritual liability is now expected to function as a relational gift. That transition is harder than any pre-marital curriculum adequately prepares couples to make.

The history here is not uniformly negative. John Calvin's theology of marriage as a genuine good, Martin Luther's robust affirmation of sexual intimacy within the covenant, the Puritan tradition's surprising frankness about marital sexuality — all of these represent genuine corrections within the tradition [cite — confirm]. But they have not fully displaced the older suspicion of embodied desire that the patristic period installed.

## The Causes Are Not Monolithic

The gap between that vision and what many marriages are actually experiencing is real. And the gap is not primarily a moral failure. It is almost always a symptom of something else.

Sometimes it is medical — hormonal shifts, chronic illness, medication side effects, pain conditions that are being treated as personal failure rather than physical reality. Testosterone decline in men and estrogen decline in women, the sexual side effects of antidepressants and blood pressure medications, the genuine physical disruption of chronic pain or illness — these are not spiritual problems. They are physical conditions that deserve medical attention without shame.

Sometimes it is psychological — unprocessed trauma, depression, anxiety, the crushing exhaustion of a season of life that has stripped every margin. The connection between mental health and sexual desire is direct and well-documented. A person in the grip of clinical depression does not have low desire because they do not love their spouse. They have low desire because the condition suppresses the neurological systems that generate it.

Sometimes it is relational — resentment that has migrated from the emotional to the physical, the body refusing what the heart cannot yet offer. This is perhaps the most common form, and the most important for couples to understand: the absence of sexual intimacy in this case is not the problem. It is the symptom of an unaddressed relational problem. Addressing the physical without addressing the relational produces at best compliance without desire, which is not the biblical vision.

Sometimes it is the slow default of two people who got busy and kept intending to reconnect and kept not doing it until the gap became its own obstacle. Inertia is a real force in marriages. The couple who went three months without intimacy during a particular season of stress often discovers that the three months became a year before they noticed, and that the year has now built its own awkwardness around reengaging.

## The Counter-Position Worth Hearing

Some pastoral counselors argue that the explicit clinical discussion of sexual frequency — the setting of targets, the therapeutic language of "desire discrepancy," the behavioral interventions — reduces what should be an expression of intimate covenant love to a performance metric. The concern is that treating sexual intimacy as a goal to be achieved by technique misses the relational soil in which genuine desire grows.

This concern has real weight. The couple who has a better technique but no better emotional connection has not solved the actual problem. Desire is not primarily mechanical — it is relational, and the relational conditions for desire are built or eroded by everything that happens outside the bedroom as well as within it.

But the concern should not produce pastoral silence. The couple sitting in private shame about something that pastoral care and good clinical support can address is not served by the preacher's discretion. They are abandoned by it. The pastoral responsibility is not to be clinical about something intimate — it is to name the reality enough to give permission to the couple to address it, with whatever support they need.

## What the Conversation Needs to Include

It needs honesty about which person is experiencing more distance from desire — and why. It needs the other person to receive that honesty without defensiveness, which is one of the hardest things to do in a long marriage. It needs both people to distinguish between "I do not want intimacy with you" and "I do not have access to desire right now," because those are very different situations with very different implications.

It also needs to include the possibility of professional help — medical, therapeutic, or both — without shame. The couple who has not had sex in eighteen months is not beyond help. They are, in most cases, a few honest conversations and a competent counselor away from a path forward.

What the conversation almost never needs: performance pressure, comparison to other couples, or the implied accusation that the lower-drive partner is withholding something they owe. Desire cannot be compelled. It can be cultivated — in an environment of safety, kindness, and the genuine belief that the other person sees you as a whole person and not a need-delivery system.

## Three Questions for This Week

Has this been named directly between you — not hinted at or argued around, but named clearly, without accusation?

Do you know what is actually driving the distance? Is it relational, physical, psychological, or some combination? Have you asked your doctor or a counselor?

Does the lower-drive partner feel safe enough to tell the truth about what they actually need in order for desire to return? Does the higher-drive partner know what that safety requires of them?

**This week:** a conversation that begins with "I want to talk about this without it becoming a fight. I am not accusing. I am trying to understand."

The church's silence on this topic has left too many couples in private shame about something that pastoral care and good clinical support can actually address.

The conversation is hard. The silence is harder.

And the silence is not faithfulness. It is its own kind of abandonment of the people who needed someone to say out loud what they could not say in private.
