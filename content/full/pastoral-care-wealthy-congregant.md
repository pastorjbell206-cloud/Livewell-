There is a pastoral failure we do not discuss, because discussing it is uncomfortable, and because the person most likely to leave when it becomes uncomfortable is also the person most likely to have funded the building.

I have committed this failure. I am still capable of it.

The wealthy congregant is not a problem to manage. He is a soul for whom Christ died, carrying the particular burden that Jesus named directly and without softening in Mark 10:23 — "How hard it is for the rich to enter the kingdom of God." The disciples were astonished. They had assumed the opposite. Wealth looked like favor. It looked like evidence of righteousness, the visible sign of God's blessing on a life well-ordered. Jesus was not gentle in correcting them. He pressed: harder than a camel through the eye of a needle. The disciples asked each other who could possibly be saved. That is the right response. That is the response that takes the text seriously.

Most of us do not take it seriously. We take the wealthy member seriously — as a donor, as a board member, as a person whose opinion carries weight in elder meetings and capital campaigns — and we call that pastoral care. It is not pastoral care. It is institutional management.

## What the History Tells Us

The church's accommodation of wealth is not a recent development. Constantine's conversion in the early fourth century [cite — confirm] changed the social composition of the church overnight. The community that had been primarily composed of the working poor, slaves, artisans, and women on the social margins became, within a generation, the preferred religion of the empire's upper classes. The social pressure that had functioned as a filter — the cost of following Christ in a hostile culture — was removed. Wealth and power entered the church without being required to submit to the same scrutiny that poverty and vulnerability had.

Chrysostom, the fourth-century bishop of Constantinople, stood against this with a directness that eventually got him exiled. His sermons on Matthew and on the letters of Paul are extended prophetic indictments of wealthy Christians who built elaborate houses while the poor of Constantinople went without food and shelter [cite — confirm]. He preached that the wealthy had no right to their wealth while anyone in the city was hungry. He named the donors in his congregation and the gap between their giving and their assets. The wealthy responded by engineering his removal. The empress herself pressed for his exile. He died in 407 on a forced march to the Black Sea. His consistency cost him everything.

Most of us are not Chrysostom. Most of us have made the trade he refused.

## What Care Actually Requires

The steel-man case for the approach we take is this: wealthy congregants are humans with real griefs, real marriages, real anxieties, real fear of death. They deserve pastoral presence in those moments. The CEO sitting across from me at the hospital bed of his dying mother is not his balance sheet. He is a son who is about to lose his mother, and he needs the same word of the Lord that anyone needs. He needs presence, Scripture, prayer, the assurance that death is not the last word. That is not wrong. That is exactly right.

But pastoral care is not only crisis response. It is formation. It is the long, patient work of shaping a soul toward the kingdom. And the kingdom, as Jesus described it, makes specific demands on the accumulation of wealth that pastoral care must eventually name. Not once. Not as a disclaimer slipped into a stewardship sermon. As a recurring, patient, self-implicating conversation that comes from a pastor who is inside the same struggle.

Because I am inside the same struggle. I am not rich by the standards of my wealthy congregants. By the standards of the world's population, I am extraordinarily comfortable. The question of what wealth does to the soul is not a question I ask from the outside. It is a question I ask as someone who has felt the pull — the pull toward security, toward comfort, toward the accumulation of margin that insulates me from the kinds of dependency that are actually central to the kingdom life Jesus described.

## What the Prophets Saw That We Are Afraid to Name

Amos did not comfort the wealthy of Samaria. He addressed them: "Woe to those who lie on beds of ivory and stretch out on their couches, and eat lambs from the flock and calves from the midst of the stall, who sing idle songs to the sound of the harp and like David invent for themselves instruments of music, who drink wine in bowls and anoint themselves with the finest oils, but are not grieved over the ruin of Joseph" (Amos 6:4–6). The ruin of Joseph was the suffering of the poor. The comfortable had ceased to feel it. Not because they were monsters. Because comfort produces a specific kind of insulation from the suffering that happens outside its radius.

The prophetic job was not to expel the wealthy. It was to awaken them. To break through the insulation. To make them feel, again, what comfort had anesthetized. That is a pastoral act. It is the most demanding pastoral act — more demanding than hospital visits, more demanding than marriage counseling, because it requires the pastor to name something the wealthy person has arranged their entire life to avoid hearing.

Jesus's encounter with the rich young ruler in Mark 10 is the paradigm. The man had kept the commandments. His life was, by external measure, morally ordered. He was earnest. Jesus "looked at him and loved him" (v. 21) — the text is explicit about the affection before the demand. Then: "Go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me." Something specific. Something costly. Something that went to the center of what the man's wealth had become: his security, his identity, the evidence he had accumulated that his life was working. The man went away sorrowful. He did not stay to argue. He went away.

Jesus did not chase him. He did not soften the word. He let the man go.

## What We Refuse to Say

The wealthy in our congregations generally know that Jesus said hard things about money. They have read Luke 12:15 — "one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions." They have heard it preached around the edges. What they have rarely experienced is a pastor willing to sit with them and ask: What is this doing to your soul? What does your wealth cost the people downstream from the decisions your wealth makes possible? Is the kingdom of God actually the organizing center of your financial life, or is your financial life a separate sovereignty that coexists peacefully alongside your religious practice?

Those questions are not comfortable. They are also not optional if you believe the Sermon on the Mount is binding on the community you are forming.

I know a pastor — a good man, a careful man — who has spent fifteen years serving a congregation with several families of significant wealth. He has never once had a direct conversation with any of them about money and discipleship. He tells himself they know. They do not know. They have never been told. They have been prayed over, counseled in marriage difficulty, eulogized when they lost parents, and handed the offering plate on stewardship Sunday. They have not been pastored in the place where the hardest work of discipleship is required.

He is afraid. I understand the fear. I have lived it. The fear is specific and real: he might leave; he might pull his pledge; the building campaign might fall short; the conflict might become public; the congregation might choose sides. Those are real possibilities. They are not theological reasons to remain silent.

## What Complicity Looks Like

Pastoral care that does not include prophetic challenge is not pastoral care. It is what the prophets called false peace — speaking smooth words to those who have not been reached by grief (Jeremiah 6:14). The prophet who assures the wealthy that their wealth is a sign of God's blessing while the poor of the congregation struggle with medical debt has spoken a false word. He has not pastored. He has performed.

The prosperity gospel is the most visible form of this failure — the explicit theological claim that wealth is God's reward for faith, that abundance is evidence of righteousness. But the failure is not limited to congregations that name the prosperity gospel explicitly. It operates wherever pastors preach about generosity without examining the structural sources of wealth, wherever the stewardship campaign focuses on what donors give without asking what the rest of their financial lives look like, wherever the wealthy are celebrated for their philanthropy without being asked about their supply chains, their wage practices, their investment portfolios.

The word of God for the wealthy congregant is not "give more to the building campaign." It is what Jesus gave to the rich young ruler: something specific, something costly, something that goes to the center of the question that wealth always raises — what does it mean to hold loosely what the world says you have earned?

## The Hardest Sentence in the Text

Some will leave. The rich young ruler left. He "went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions" (Matthew 19:22). Jesus did not chase him. He did not soften the word. He did not call after him to say that the requirement was merely illustrative, that the principle was the thing, that actual divestiture was not really what was meant.

He let the man go.

That is the hardest sentence in the text. Not because it is harsh. Because it refuses the consolation that the pastoral instinct reaches for — the assurance that there is always a way to say the hard thing in a soft enough register that no one leaves. Sometimes there is not. Sometimes the word is what it is, and the person it reaches will respond as the rich young ruler responded, and the pastor's job is to have spoken it truly and to let the Spirit do what the Spirit does.

The wealthy congregant needs to be loved enough to be told the truth. That love is more demanding than the management of the relationship that keeps the pledge coming. It requires the pastor to care more about the soul than the budget — which is itself a test of whether the pastor believes what he preaches about where treasure actually is.

The congregation that produces that love has made a different trade than the one most of us have made. It has traded the comfort of the wealthy for their formation. The formation is far more valuable. It is what the pastoral office was actually created to produce.

Most of us have not made it. The text keeps asking whether we are ready to begin.
