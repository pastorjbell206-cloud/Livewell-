There is a moment that most pastors who have experienced a genuine city-wide worship gathering can describe with unusual precision. The moment when the room is full of people from churches that would never normally be in the same building — different ethnicities, different traditions, different musical idioms, different theological emphases — and the worship somehow holds all of it, and something that feels genuinely like the body of Christ becomes visible in a way that no single congregation can make visible on its own.

These moments are not automatic. They require significant coordination, genuine humility from the pastors and leaders involved, and a willingness to set aside the particular cultural preferences of any single congregation in the interest of something larger. They are not easy to produce. But they are worth producing, and the case for making them a regular, intentional practice is stronger than most pastoral communities have yet been willing to act on.

## What the Theological Tradition Demands

The case for city-wide gathering is not primarily pragmatic. It is theological, and the theology is demanding.

The New Testament's vision of the church is not a network of discrete congregations each pursuing its own mission in its own community. It is a singular body — the body of Christ — that takes plural and locally differentiated forms without losing its fundamental unity. When Paul writes "there is one body and one Spirit" in Ephesians 4:4, he is not describing an aspiration. He is describing a reality that the church's visible behavior is meant to express. When he describes the church at Corinth as "the body of Christ" (1 Corinthians 12:27), he does not mean the individual congregation — he means the community of all who call on the name of Christ in that city.

The early church's practice in the city of Rome, where multiple house churches functioned in relationship with one another without a single unified building, was not experienced as fragmentation. It was experienced as the one church of Rome expressed in multiple gathering points, with genuine relationship between them and genuine accountability to a common identity. The fragmentation that characterizes the modern city — where the church exists as hundreds of independent organizations with no meaningful relationship to one another — is not a continuation of this pattern. It is a departure from it.

Lesslie Newbigin argued in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* that the credibility of the gospel in a pluralistic culture depends significantly on the visible quality of the community that the gospel produces [cite — confirm]. A city full of churches that never gather together does not provide the watching city with evidence that those churches believe something that actually overcomes the divisions of the world. They provide evidence that the church has organized itself according to the same divisions that organize everything else.

The city-wide gathering is one of the primary practices by which the church's theological claim — that it is one body despite its diversity — is made visible and therefore believable.

## What City-Wide Gatherings Actually Do

The most important thing a city-wide gathering does is make the invisible visible. The body of Christ in any given city is real — it includes people from every tradition, every ethnicity, every economic background who share a common allegiance to the same Lord. But in ordinary practice, that reality is completely invisible. Each congregation experiences itself as a discrete unit. The larger body is a theological conviction, not a lived experience.

When the pastors of a city decide to gather their congregations together for worship, mission, or prayer, they make the theological conviction experientially real. People encounter the breadth of the church in a way that permanently expands their sense of what it is and what it can be. The Baptist who has spent twenty years convinced that contemporary worship is theologically shallow encounters the Pentecostal congregation whose worship is both contemporary and deeply formed. The evangelical who has privately assumed that mainline Christians are not really serious encounters the Methodist pastor whose biblical depth and pastoral seriousness dismantles that assumption.

These are not incidental outcomes. They are the kinds of formation that cannot happen within a single congregation's walls, no matter how excellent that congregation's programming is. The exposure to the breadth of the church is itself a form of theological education.

And the witness to the city of a unified church is dramatically more compelling than the witness of a thousand separate congregations each doing their own thing. When multiple congregations mobilize together for a prayer service before city elections, or join forces for a shared service initiative in the city's lowest-income neighborhood, or simply fill an arena for an evening of worship that is visibly diverse and visibly unified — the watching city sees something it does not expect and cannot explain from within its ordinary categories.

## The Pastoral Work That Makes It Possible

City-wide gatherings do not emerge from organizational momentum. They emerge from pastoral relationships. The pastors who have met regularly, who know each other's names and stories, who have developed enough trust to work through the inevitable decisions about who leads, who speaks, whose music is used, and whose tradition sets the liturgical tone — those pastors are the ones who can make a genuine city-wide gathering happen.

The decisions are genuinely difficult. Which pastor will preach? Whose worship team will lead? Will there be communion, and if so, whose liturgical tradition will govern it? Will the gathering represent only the evangelical stream or will it include the full breadth of the city's Christians? Every one of these decisions carries weight, and every one of them requires the pastors involved to subordinate their own preferences and their congregations' expectations to the larger purpose.

This requires the kind of maturity that comes only from genuine relationship. The pastor who does not know the pastor across town well enough to have an honest disagreement with him cannot make a city-wide gathering with him. The relationship is the prerequisite, and the relationship takes time.

This is part of why organizations like the Pastors Connection Network matter beyond their explicit programming — the ongoing relationships they build between pastors are the infrastructure that makes genuine collaboration possible when the moment calls for it. The gathering does not create the relationship. The relationship makes the gathering possible.

## The Steel-Manned Objection

The strongest objection to city-wide gathering emphasis is ecclesiological: if the gathered congregation is the primary expression of the church in the New Testament — and it is — then the gathered congregation should be the primary focus of pastoral investment, and the energy that goes into city-wide coordination is energy drawn away from the community that is the pastor's primary pastoral responsibility.

This objection is not wrong about the priority of the gathered congregation. It is wrong to construct a competition between that priority and the larger commitment to the church's unity. The pastor who invests in city-wide relationships is not neglecting his congregation. He is modeling for his congregation what it means to understand their church as part of something larger than itself — which is formation that the congregation genuinely needs.

Moreover, the city-wide gathering consistently produces energy that flows back into the local congregation rather than away from it. Every pastor I know who has participated in a genuine cross-church collaboration reports that their congregation came away from it with renewed sense of the church's mission, renewed generosity, and renewed engagement. The gathering does not drain the congregation. It expands it.

## Starting Smaller Than You Think You Need To

The city-wide gathering does not need to be a stadium event on its first attempt. It can begin with three churches sharing a prayer service in the lead-up to Easter. It can begin with a joint outreach project in a neighborhood that no single church has the capacity to address alone. It can begin with a Thanksgiving service that rotates between congregations, gradually expanding as trust develops and logistics become familiar.

I have participated in gatherings of fifteen hundred and gatherings of sixty. The gathering of sixty — three congregations in a neighborhood church on a Wednesday evening before Good Friday, led by a worship team assembled from all three churches, with a message shared by a pastor none of the three congregations had previously heard — produced something in those sixty people that I have not forgotten.

The visibility of the body of Christ does not require scale. It requires the genuine presence of people who would not normally be in the same room, gathered around the one thing that makes them one.

Start where you are, with who is willing. The pastors in your city who would say yes to an invitation are probably closer than you think. The first gathering will be imperfect. It will also produce something none of the individual congregations could have produced alone.

That something is the point. Let that be reason enough to make the call.
