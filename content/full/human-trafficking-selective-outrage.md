The church mobilizes for human trafficking with a coherence it rarely displays around other forms of exploitation. Conferences fill. Awareness campaigns spread. Youth groups raise money for rescue organizations. And much of this is genuinely good — the trafficking of human beings for sexual and labor exploitation is among the most direct violations of human dignity imaginable, and the church is right to oppose it.

But there is something else present in the church's response to trafficking that deserves examination. It is not enough to ask whether we oppose trafficking. We must also ask which trafficking we oppose, whose trafficking we name, and whose exploitation we walk past on the way to the rescue conference.

## The Full Shape of the Problem

Human trafficking in the United States takes two primary forms: sex trafficking and labor trafficking. Sex trafficking receives the overwhelming majority of the church's attention. Labor trafficking — the coerced and exploitative use of human labor through debt bondage, document confiscation, threats of violence, and systematic deception — is considerably less prominent in evangelical awareness campaigns, despite affecting large numbers of workers in agriculture, domestic work, construction, and manufacturing. The theological stakes are the same in both cases. A person trapped in labor trafficking has been stripped of the agency God built into them as image-bearers as surely as a person trapped in sex trafficking. The mechanism is different. The violation of imago Dei is identical.

Why, then, the asymmetry? The question is worth pressing not to diminish the importance of fighting sex trafficking but to interrogate what the asymmetry reveals about whose suffering we are trained to notice and whose we are trained to manage.

Labor trafficking touches supply chains. It runs through the agricultural fields where produce is harvested, the factories where goods are assembled, the construction sites where buildings rise, the domestic work arrangements in homes that look, from the outside, like normal employment. The products of labor trafficking sit in grocery stores and on kitchen tables and in church fellowship halls. The person who has been trafficked for their labor may have produced something we purchased last week, in a transaction that passed through legitimate-seeming commercial channels. Our outrage is selective partly because our implication is much harder to contain if we widen the frame.

## What the Bible Says About Exploitation

The prophetic tradition in Scripture does not distinguish between types of exploitation based on how disturbing they are to examine. Jeremiah 22:13 is direct: "Woe to him who builds his palace by unrighteousness, his upper rooms by injustice, making his own people work for nothing, not paying them for their labor." The sin named is the extraction of labor without just compensation — the building of wealth on someone else's exploitation. Amos spent most of his ministry condemning economic systems that ground the poor into dust, selling "the needy for a pair of sandals" (Amos 2:6). James 5:4 addresses labor exploitation with unusual directness: "Look! The wages you failed to pay the workers who mowed your fields are crying out against you. The cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord Almighty."

The text is not interested in whether the exploitation was accomplished through formal coercion or through market structures that left workers no viable alternative. What it names is the system that takes from the vulnerable what is rightfully theirs and transfers it to those who already have enough. That system has many forms. Human trafficking in its rawest form is simply the extreme end of a continuum that runs through wage theft, predatory contract arrangements, debt bondage through payday lending, and the full range of labor practices that extract human value without returning adequate compensation. The prophets named the whole continuum. The church has tended to name only the extreme end, and only the version that allows us to feel entirely outside the system we are condemning.

## The Selective Frame and What It Costs Us

Selective outrage is not morally neutral. It is a form of moral positioning that maintains the appearance of prophetic witness while protecting the positions and arrangements that benefit us.

The church that rallies around sex trafficking and stays quiet about agricultural labor trafficking is not simply choosing its battles. It is revealing something about whose vulnerability activates its instincts and whose does not. The women and girls exploited in sex trafficking are real people whose suffering is real and whose rescue matters. And the migrant workers confined to debt bondage on farms that supply national grocery chains are also real people whose suffering is real and whose liberation matters. The question is why one group's suffering fills evangelical conferences and the other's barely registers.

Part of the answer is social distance. Sex trafficking maps onto narratives of individual victimization and rescue that are theologically familiar and emotionally accessible. Labor trafficking maps onto economic systems, supply chains, regulatory policy, and consumer behavior — all of which implicate the church's members in ways that sex trafficking does not. The rescue narrative is clean. The supply chain narrative is not. We choose the clean narrative.

Part of the answer is political alliance. In the American evangelical world, the political affiliations that dominate make it relatively comfortable to condemn sex trafficking — it fits within a shared moral framework — and relatively uncomfortable to condemn labor exploitation of migrant workers, because that conversation touches immigration policy, agricultural industry lobbying, and the supply chain practices of companies whose executives may be in the pews. Prophetic witness that stops at the edge of political discomfort is not prophetic witness. It is politics with a theological wrapper.

## The Counter-Position Taken Seriously

There is a real argument on the other side. Organizations that focus specifically on sex trafficking argue that concentration of resources on a single, clearly defined target is more effective than spreading awareness across multiple forms of exploitation. A diffuse campaign is a weak campaign. Focused attention, they argue, saves more lives than distributed attention. This is not a dishonest position. It reflects genuine strategic thinking about how change happens.

But strategic focus does not require theological silence about the wider pattern. A church can direct its practical resources toward a specific form of intervention while still forming its people's moral imagination around the full scope of exploitation the text condemns. The problem is not that churches focus on sex trafficking as a ministry strategy. The problem is that the narrowness of the focus becomes the narrowness of the moral vision — that what we campaign against becomes the full extent of what we see. And what we do not see, we do not interrogate.

The prophets were not selective in this way. Amos moved from indicting sexual exploitation to indicting economic exploitation to indicting the legal corruption that protected the wealthy to indicting the religious ceremonialism that dressed all of it in spiritual legitimacy. He did not separate these sins into categories of the actionable and the awkward. They were all the same sin: the use of power to take what belonged to the vulnerable.

## A Honest Self-Accounting

I have been moved by anti-trafficking campaigns. I have given money to rescue organizations. I have preached the horror of sex trafficking with genuine anger. And I have not asked, with the same rigor, who picked the tomatoes at the bottom of my grocery bag, or whether the supply chain behind the shirt I am wearing moved through factories where workers were held in debt bondage, or what the labor conditions were in the manufacturing that built the things in my home.

That is not a small inconsistency. It is a structural one. My outrage is calibrated to the forms of exploitation I can condemn without examining my own economic life. That calibration is exactly what the prophets refused. Amos preached to people who brought their offerings to Bethel and were certain of their own righteousness. They opposed the sins they could see in others. They could not see the sins built into the arrangements that sustained their comfort.

Isaiah 58 names the form of religion that is energetic about its visible practices and blind to its structural ones: "You fast and quarrel and strike with a wicked fist." The people were performing their religious obligations while the systems around them ground down the poor. God's response was not to commend their visible devotion. It was to name the form of justice that their fasting was designed to avoid.

## What Faithfulness Requires

Not abandoning anti-trafficking work. The rescue of the person trapped in sexual exploitation is urgent and right. The work of the organizations doing this work deserves support.

But faithfulness requires that the church's moral attention be as wide as the text's — which means naming labor trafficking alongside sex trafficking, examining the supply chains our consumer lives run through, asking what the labor conditions are behind the goods we purchase cheaply, and being willing to apply the same prophetic energy to the exploitation that implicates us as to the exploitation that does not.

It requires being honest about what our outrage actually covers. The church that is loud about one form of human exploitation and silent about others has not taken the full measure of the evil it claims to oppose. It has found a version of opposition that costs very little — no reexamination of purchasing habits, no uncomfortable political positions, no renegotiation of supply chain relationships, no naming of the people in our own congregations who benefit from exploitative labor arrangements.

Selective outrage is not nothing. But it is less than what the prophets demanded. Less than what the text requires. Less than what the people in the labor trafficking camps, the fields, and the factories deserve from a church that claims to speak for the God who hears the cry of the exploited and asks whose cry we have decided to hear.

Every human being trapped in exploitation — by sex traffickers or by labor traffickers, by the dramatic violation or the slow grinding of systemic extraction — bears the image of the God who said: the wages you withheld are crying out against you. The Lord Almighty heard them.

The question is whether we have.
