The word solidarity carries freight it did not earn in every room where it is spoken.

In some rooms it sounds like a union hall. In some rooms it sounds like a protest slogan. In American evangelical Christianity it has acquired, through a process of cultural association, the smell of progressive politics — which means that the word itself, before the argument is made, has already been sorted by many listeners into "not our category, not our language, not our concern." The sorting happens fast and almost entirely without examination.

The irony is severe. The concept the word describes is one of the most thoroughly biblical claims about the character of God and the calling of the church that the tradition contains. We did not get the word from the Left. The Left borrowed it from a history the church should recognize as its own.

---

The Greek word in its New Testament vicinity is sympatheo — to suffer with. Paul uses it in Hebrews 4:15 to describe the nature of the high priest Jesus: "We do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin." This is a claim about the kind of God the incarnation reveals — not a God who administers human suffering from outside but a God who enters it, who bears it, who knows from the inside what it weighs.

The entire arc of the Old Testament prepares for this. The God of the Exodus is a God who descends to see, descends to hear, descends to act. "I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters. I know their sufferings, and I have come down to deliver them" (Exodus 3:7-8). Three movements: see, hear, come down. The God of Scripture is not neutral between the oppressor and the one being crushed. He takes a position. He moves toward the one in need. He does not stay in a position of safety while managing the suffering from a comfortable distance.

That is solidarity. And it is not borrowed from any political tradition. It is the shape of God's activity through the whole canon.

Isaiah 57:15 says this with the kind of precision that stops me every time: "For thus says the One who is high and lifted up, who inhabits eternity, whose name is Holy: 'I dwell in the high and holy place, and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit, to revive the spirit of the lowly, and to revive the heart of the contrite.'" The God who inhabits eternity dwells specifically, particularly, with the contrite and lowly. Not primarily with the powerful. Not most naturally at home with the institutions that bear his name while accumulating the security that his name commends them to surrender. He dwells with the lowly.

The incarnation is the logical completion of this pattern. The Word became flesh and dwelt — the Greek is eskenosen, pitched a tent — among us (John 1:14). Not among us in the sense of proximity. Among us in the sense of full human embodiment, in the conditions of poverty and marginality, in a province occupied by an empire, born in a stable because the established places were full. God did not send a message about human suffering. God entered human suffering, in the body, in the class and social position of those at the bottom of every list that the powerful keep.

---

Solidarity, as a theological virtue, means this: to stand with people in conditions you did not create and do not fully share, not as a manager of their problem but as a participant in their reality. This is different from charity. Charity typically moves from a place of security to a place of need with a gift in hand. Charity is good. The tradition praises it and the Scriptures command it. But solidarity is more demanding than charity in every respect.

Charity can be anonymous. You can write a check and never know whose life it touches. Solidarity requires presence. You have to actually go there, to the place where the need is, and let the conditions of that place become conditions you know in your body rather than in your mind.

Charity gives from where you are. Solidarity goes to where they are. The distance matters. The willingness to close the distance, to let your social position be visible in proximity to those whose social position costs them — that is what solidarity adds to charity. Not instead of giving. In addition to giving, the willingness to be seen standing next to the person the world has sorted into the category of expendable.

Charity addresses need. Solidarity addresses the loneliness of being in need — the particular weight of carrying a condition that no one around you seems willing to share or even witness.

Paul names the theological logic in Philippians 2: "Let each of you look not only to his own interests, but also to the interests of others." He grounds it immediately in the christological hymn that follows — Christ, who was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but made himself nothing, taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of human beings (Philippians 2:6-7). The downward movement of Christ is the template for the movement the church is called to make. Not to manage the needy from a distance. To move toward them, which means moving away from the securities that distance provides.

---

I have not done this consistently. I am better at charity than at solidarity — which means I have helped from a position that protected my own comfort and social location while occasionally distributing from my surplus. That is not the same as standing with. It is closer to standing near, which preserves the option to move away when proximity becomes costly.

The steel-man version of the resistance to solidarity is worth sitting with. The argument goes like this: charity is sustainable because it operates from strength; solidarity, by compromising your own position, eventually leaves you with nothing to give. The person who gives away too much cannot keep giving. Better to maintain your position and give generously from it than to sacrifice the position and have nothing left. This is not a cynical argument. It has a logic.

But the argument misreads what solidarity actually is. Solidarity is not self-destruction. It is not the erasure of every distinction between your condition and the condition of the people you are standing with. Paul did not become a slave. He called himself one, he lived as one in terms of his freedom of movement and his dependence on others' hospitality — but the call to solidarity is not a call to pretend you have resources you do not have. It is a call to stop pretending that your resources insulate you from the obligation of presence.

The argument also misreads what the early church actually did. The observers of the ancient world who noted the unusual practices of Christian communities were not describing congregations that had bankrupted themselves. They were describing communities that shared across class lines in ways that had no precedent in the social arrangements of the empire [cite — confirm] — communities where Greek and Jew, slave and free, shared the same table and the same Lord, where the distinction between who was inside the circle of concern and who was outside had been redefined by the practice of baptism. They were describing a community that had concluded, from its theology, that no one in the room was expendable.

That practice was not a program. It was a theology worked out in the body — the theology of a God who had refused, definitively and publicly, to remain at a safe distance from human need.

---

The centuries that followed complicated the picture. Constantine's embrace of Christianity in the fourth century made it safe and then powerful, and power predictably creates distance. By the time John Chrysostom was preaching in Constantinople in the late fourth and early fifth centuries [cite — confirm], the church had become wealthy enough that his sermons against the wealth of the church sound, to our ears, almost outrageous. He called the person who keeps more than he needs while others starve a thief. Not a theologically confused person. Not a spiritually underdeveloped person. A thief. He was not considered a radical. He was considered orthodox, standing squarely in the tradition of the prophets and the apostles.

What Chrysostom understood was what Amos had understood before him: that the presence of great wealth alongside great poverty in the community of God is not a neutral fact about economic arrangements. It is a theological statement about who the community believes God to be. The God who dwells with the contrite and lowly does not quietly bless the structures that produce the contrite and lowly without remedy.

This is why the comfortable American church's ease with economic inequality is not simply a failure of generosity. It is a theological displacement — a substitution of a different god for the one the Scriptures announce. The God of Amos and Isaiah and Chrysostom and the early church is not comfortable with the arrangements we have made. The God who came down to deliver the enslaved is not satisfied with a church that prays for the vulnerable while maintaining a safe distance from their conditions.

---

Solidarity as a theological virtue requires three things that charity does not.

First, proximity. You cannot practice solidarity with people you do not know, in conditions you have not witnessed, from neighborhoods you have never entered. The incarnation is not metaphorical here. God actually went there. The people who have embodied solidarity most fully in the history of the church — Dorothy Day, Desmond Tutu, Oscar Romero [cite — confirm] — are people who put themselves physically in the proximity of suffering and let the proximity teach them what they could not have learned from a distance.

Second, willingness to be changed. Charity can be practiced without being affected. You can give and remain exactly who you were before the giving. Solidarity is more dangerous than that. Standing with people in conditions you did not create will eventually challenge your assumptions about how the world works, about who is responsible for what, about what "deserve" means in the economy of the kingdom. You will find that the people the comfortable church has categorized as problems are, in fact, people — and that the people the comfortable church has categorized as successful have, in many cases, benefited from arrangements that required someone else's deprivation.

Third, willingness to be seen. Solidarity means letting your proximity to the marginalized be visible. This is the cost that the comfortable church most reliably avoids. To stand publicly with people whose condition makes the powerful uncomfortable is to accept some of their social costs. Jesus did this in ways that attracted explicit criticism — eating with tax collectors and sinners, touching the unclean, being found in the company of those whom the religious establishment had correctly identified as beneath association. His proximity was not accidental. It was the argument.

---

The political language has borrowed the word solidarity. We should not let the borrowing become our excuse to abandon the original. The church does not need to baptize any political movement to practice what the word describes. What the church needs is to recover the theology it has always held: that the God revealed in Scripture is a God of downward movement, that the church that follows him is called to the same movement, and that the distance we have maintained from the suffering of our neighbors is not prudence. It is a theological problem.

Solidarity is a virtue. It is a virtue rooted in the character of God, demanded by the prophets, embodied by the incarnation, and practiced by the early church at a cost that included their blood. We did not invent it and we cannot afford to surrender it. Your condition is my concern — not because I have surplus to give, but because you are my neighbor and the God I follow went to where you are.

That is the claim. It is not a political position. It has always been theology.
