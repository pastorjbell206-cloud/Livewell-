Jesus taught with authority, and the crowds were astonished — "not as the scribes" (Matthew 7:28–29). The scribes had credentials. They had lineage and training and the full institutional weight of the synagogue behind their words. They held the recognized positions, possessed the formal qualifications, had been through the approved processes of authorization. What they did not have, and what Jesus had, was the kind of authority that does not require the institution to enforce it. It arrived before the argument did. It was present in the room.

We have spent a great deal of energy in the church debating the source of pastoral authority — biblical, ecclesiastical, congregational, charismatic — and the debate is not without merit. But it tends to skip the most observable fact about how authority actually functions in a congregation: people follow leaders they trust, and trust is built the same way it has always been built. Slowly. Through kept promises and honest presence and being found reliable when it costs something.

## What Positional Authority Can and Cannot Do

Positional authority is real. The office of pastor carries weight in the text and in the tradition. The elders who lead well are worthy of double honor (1 Timothy 5:17). The congregation is to obey its leaders and submit to their authority (Hebrews 13:17). This is not merely courtesy language. Leadership structures matter and the pastoral office carries genuine theological weight.

But positional authority alone can compel attendance. It cannot compel following. A congregation can sit in the pews, vote the right way, serve on the right committees, and not be following you in any meaningful sense — not actually moving in the direction you are calling them toward. They are present without being persuaded. They are compliant without being committed.

I have led that way. I know what it produces. It produces a church where the pastor is the center of everything and the congregation is the audience, where vision is issued and received rather than built and owned, where people measure the institution by whether the pastor delivers rather than whether they belong to something they believe in. It looks like a functioning church from the outside. From the inside, it is waiting to fracture along whatever fault line appears first.

The church that follows its pastor because he has the title is an institution. The church that follows its pastor because they know him is a community. The difference between those two things is the difference between a pastor who leads until the moment of crisis and a pastor who can lead through crisis because the people around him have enough history with him to know, in their bones, that he is for them.

## The New Testament Pattern

The pastoral letters in the New Testament are striking for how relational they are. Paul does not write to churches as an executive issuing directives to subsidiary offices. He writes as a man who loves the people he is writing to, who names them by name, who is anxious about them and proud of them and sometimes desperate about them.

The letter to the Philippians opens with Paul's expression of deep personal affection: "I thank my God every time I remember you" (Philippians 1:3). The letter to the Thessalonians speaks of Paul's care like a nursing mother who cares for her children (1 Thessalonians 2:7). The letter to Philemon is an appeal based entirely on personal relationship — Paul does not command Onesimus's release; he asks, as the man who led Philemon to faith, for a gift freely given rather than a compliance compelled (Philemon 14). His authority in those churches was real — he defended it vigorously when it was challenged — but it was not separable from relationship. The authority was the relationship, demonstrated through years of shared life and real cost and genuine care.

This is not accidental to Paul's theology. In 2 Corinthians 1:24, he tells the Corinthians: "Not that we lord it over your faith, but we work with you for your joy." The phrase "lord it over" is the same construction Jesus uses in Mark 10:42-45 when he contrasts the leadership of the Gentile rulers with the leadership of the kingdom: "Whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant." The authority of the kingdom is not the authority that stands above and compels. It is the authority that stands alongside and serves. It is the authority that is earned by the quality of the service, not granted by the quality of the credential.

## The Good Shepherd Model

"I am the good shepherd," Jesus said. "The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). The sheep follow him "because they know his voice" (John 10:4). Not because he has a title. Because they know him. That knowing is built through proximity, through care that costs something, through the experience over time that this person is for me rather than for the institution.

The contrast in John 10 is with the hired hand, who runs when the wolf comes because the sheep are not his own — because the relationship is transactional rather than covenantal, because what he values is the wage rather than the welfare. The sheep do not follow the hired hand with the same confidence because the hired hand's behavior in the moment of crisis reveals what the normal times concealed: that his commitment to them was contingent on cost.

The congregation knows this about their pastor long before the crisis arrives. They know it in the accumulation of small moments — whether the pastor appeared when it was inconvenient as well as when it was easy, whether he kept the promise he made on Tuesday when no one was watching, whether his care for them costs him anything real or whether it is exercised within the comfortable limits of a professional role. The crisis just reveals what was always there.

## What Relational Authority Requires

Relational authority is more demanding than positional authority, because it cannot be granted by a vote or maintained by a document. It must be earned continuously. A pastor can have twenty years of track record and squander it in a single season of unavailability or dishonesty or decisions that communicate that the institution matters more than the people. Trust accumulates slowly and departs quickly.

The pastor who has been operating on positional authority will often not realize how thin the relational foundation has become until the moment of crisis when he needs people to follow him somewhere hard and discovers they will not. Not because they dislike him. Because they do not know him well enough to trust him with something that costs them.

The counter-position has genuine weight: the pastor cannot be personally available to every member of a congregation of any substantial size. The pastoral relationship has institutional dimensions that are not in themselves a failure of relational care. A pastor of two thousand people cannot know every family the way a pastor of forty can. The structures of formal pastoral authority exist precisely because congregational size makes purely relational authority impractical.

This is true. But the conclusion it suggests is not "therefore positional authority is sufficient." It is "therefore the pastor must be even more intentional about where relational authority is actually built — with the leaders who lead the leaders, with the people whose trust multiplies through the structures of congregational life, with the communities within the community where the real pastoral work happens." Size changes the strategy. It does not change the principle. The principle is that people follow who they trust, and trust is built through personal presence that costs something.

## The Specific Work

The answer is not complicated, but it is demanding. Be present. Keep your word to people who cannot advance your career. Show up in the Tuesday-afternoon moments when no one is watching and nothing is being built. Know the names and the losses and the fears of the people in your congregation well enough that your care for them is evident rather than performed.

That kind of knowing takes time you feel like you cannot spare. It is time that does not produce a sermon, does not advance a program, does not generate the output a ministry culture tends to count and report. It is time spent in hospital waiting rooms and at gravesides and across kitchen tables in conversations that have no agenda except the pastoral relationship itself. It looks, from the outside, like inefficiency. It is actually the work. The sermon is built from what happens in those rooms. The vision is trusted or distrusted based on whether the people to whom it is offered have enough history with the pastor to believe he is seeing the same thing they are.

The congregation that follows you into the hard seasons — the seasons of financial difficulty or staff failure or theological recalibration or genuine conflict — is the one that knew you in the ordinary ones. They follow because they have accumulated enough experience of your trustworthiness to extend it into territory they cannot yet see. That extension is relational. It cannot be manufactured in a sermon or generated by a vote or produced by any credential the institution can grant.

It is built slowly, in the Tuesday afternoons, one kept promise at a time. By the time you need it, it is either there or it is not.

Build it before you need it.

That kind of authority takes years. Begin.
