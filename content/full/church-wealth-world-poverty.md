American Christianity owns a great deal of property.

Sanctuaries with professional lighting rigs. Multi-campus facilities with commercial kitchens and gymnasium floors. Endowments, investment portfolios, media budgets. The precise aggregate is debated, but no one disputes the basic fact: the institutional church in America controls resources on a scale that would have been incomprehensible to the communities in which the New Testament was written. Those early communities shared possessions, sold property, and distributed to anyone in need (Acts 2:44–45). They were also, in most cases, economically marginal — a gathering of artisans, freedpersons, and the occasional household of modest means, meeting in borrowed rooms.

The contrast is not a reason for guilt. It is a reason for examination.

I have sat in budget meetings where we spent more time discussing the replacement of aging pew cushions than we spent on benevolence allocations. I have done that. I have approved facilities expenditures while hearing, in the very next agenda item, about the family in the congregation facing eviction. I am not pointing from a distance. I know what it costs to maintain a building and to keep a staff and to run the programs that form the visible life of a congregation. I know those costs are real and those programs are not meaningless. I am saying they are not the whole conversation. And we have made them the whole conversation.

## What the New Testament Actually Says

The question the New Testament will not let us avoid is not "can a wealthy church be faithful?" but "what does faithfulness require of a wealthy church?" The answer the early church gave was concrete. When the Jerusalem community faced famine, the Gentile churches took up a collection — Paul describes it in 2 Corinthians 8–9 with language about equality and surplus meeting need. "Your abundance at the present time should supply their need, so that their abundance may supply your need" (2 Corinthians 8:14). This is not an abstract principle. It is a description of how wealth was expected to move: from where there was more, toward where there was less.

Paul sets up the collection by pointing to a theological foundation that modern stewardship campaigns typically omit. He does not say: the Jerusalem church needs your help, so give. He says: "For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9). The incarnation is the prototype for this movement. God's abundance moved toward human poverty. That movement is not a strategy to be imitated selectively. It is the shape of the gospel, and the church's economic life is supposed to reflect it.

John says it plainly in his first letter. "If anyone has the world's goods and sees a brother or sister in need, yet closes his heart against them, how does God's love abide in him?" (1 John 3:17). The Greek word is bios — the material conditions of living. To have bios and withhold it from someone who lacks bios is not merely stinginess. It is evidence that God's love does not actually abide in us. John intends this strongly. He is not warning about the spiritual danger of indifference. He is saying that indifference is evidence of a love that was never there.

James is the harshest voice in the New Testament canon on this question, and he is writing to people who are gathered in Jesus's name. "Your riches have rotted and your garments are moth-eaten. Your gold and silver have corroded, and their corrosion will be evidence against you and will eat your flesh like fire" (James 5:2–3). He is not writing to pagan merchants who have never heard the gospel. He is writing to the assembly. The wealth he indicts is Christian wealth held while brothers and sisters lacked. The corrosion is not metaphorical. It is the physical decay of resources left in storage while people starved.

## The Historical Arc

The early church's relationship to wealth was shaped by the specific social composition of the movement. Rodney Stark has argued [cite — confirm] that early Christianity spread in part through its practice of caring for the sick, the poor, and the dying at a time when other social institutions offered nothing. During epidemics in second and third century Alexandria and Carthage [cite — confirm], Christians stayed to care for the dying when others fled. They buried the dead at personal risk. They provided material relief to people the broader Roman social network had abandoned. This was not a program. It was the logical outworking of a theology that said every person bears the image of God, and that bearing that image means you are never expendable.

By the fourth century, the settlement with Constantine changed the church's position dramatically. Imperial patronage brought resources; it also brought the temptation to use those resources to build institutions rather than to redistribute wealth toward those who lacked it. The construction of the great basilicas — monuments to the Christian empire's power and prestige — was not entirely divorced from the tradition. But it began a centuries-long negotiation between the church's prophetic instinct toward redistribution and its institutional interest in accumulation. That negotiation has never been resolved cleanly.

The Reformation addressed many things. It did not substantially address the church's relationship to accumulated wealth. Luther's theology of the two kingdoms [cite — confirm] tended to locate economic questions in the secular sphere, leaving the institutional church relatively undisturbed in its management of property. The Social Gospel movement of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries tried to reconnect the church's economic life to its prophetic tradition, but it was largely reduced to charity rather than transformed into justice. Walter Rauschenbusch, whose *A Theology for the Social Gospel* appeared in 1917 [cite — confirm], understood that personal conversion without structural change left the structures intact to produce the same misery generation after generation. The American church absorbed his concern for the poor while resisting his analysis of the systems that produced them.

## The Steel-Man Position

The honest defense of institutional church wealth deserves engagement. Institutional resources exist for institutional purposes, and a church must maintain itself to serve at all. Programs require staff. Staff require salaries. Buildings require maintenance. A congregation that gives away its operational budget has no operational capacity. The megachurch with its enormous facilities is, in many cases, genuinely serving thousands of people — running addiction recovery programs, operating food banks, providing counseling, supporting mission organizations around the world. The resources do not simply accumulate. They circulate.

This is true. And the question is not whether to have buildings or budgets. The question is proportion, and who decides what proportion is faithful. What percentage of collective resources reaches people outside the walls? What percentage is consumed by the maintenance of the institution that is supposed to serve them? When those numbers are examined honestly — and many churches resist examining them — the results often call for theological reckoning rather than institutional satisfaction.

The deeper question is not operational. It is theological: what does the way we spend our money say about what we actually believe? Budgets are confessions. The line items in a church's operating budget reveal its priorities more accurately than its mission statement. When a congregation spends four times as much on facilities maintenance as on benevolence, it has made a statement. The statement may not be intentional. It may be the accumulated result of decisions made over decades, each of which seemed prudent in isolation. But the total is a testimony, and it is not the testimony of 2 Corinthians 8.

## The World's Poverty Is Not an Abstraction

Over 700 million people globally live on less than $2.15 per day [cite — confirm]. Not as a conceptual statistic but as specific people in specific places — the woman in the Nairobi slum, the child in the Central American village, the elderly man in rural India — who lack what the average American church building cost to construct. The gap between the church's accumulated resources and the world's documented need is not a natural fact. It is the result of choices about who gets access to what.

I am not arguing for a simple redistribution prescription. The relationship between institutional church resources and global poverty is complex, and naive divestment would destroy the very institutional capacity that makes sustainable engagement possible. I am arguing that the gap must be held before us, not as a cause for despair but as a spur to the kind of uncomfortable self-examination that might produce something more than it has.

Zacchaeus, when he encountered Jesus, did not wait for a systematic theology of wealth redistribution. He stood and made a declaration: half of what he owns he will give to the poor, and anyone he has defrauded he will repay fourfold (Luke 19:8). Jesus says, in response, that salvation came to this house. Not that Zacchaeus had intellectual assent to correct doctrine. That salvation came. The evidence of salvation in the house of Zacchaeus was a changed ledger.

The audit of our own ledgers is not comfortable. I have not conducted it with the honesty it deserves. I have presided over institutions that accumulated while the world's poor remained poor, and I have found ways to describe that accumulation as stewardship.

That is also a theological statement. It is not the one I want to be making. But the only way to make a different one is to stop pretending the question does not exist, and to sit in the discomfort of what Paul's language about equality actually requires — not a vague aspiration toward generosity, but the concrete movement of surplus toward need, because that is the shape the gospel takes when it is not merely preached but practiced.

We cannot claim to follow Jesus through a building he wouldn't recognize while the people he specifically named as the measure of our faithfulness sleep outside.

The gap between the church's wealth and the world's poverty is not an indictment we can preach past. It is the condition in which we preach, and it will shape the credibility of everything else we say.
