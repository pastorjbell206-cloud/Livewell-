There is a building in most American cities that sits on land whose story it does not know. It has a mortgage, a tax bill, a property line. It may even have a plaque marking the year it was built. What it almost certainly does not have is a reckoning with the full history of the ground beneath it — who lived there before, how the title passed from one hand to another, what was taken, what was paid, what was never paid at all.

The church is one of those buildings. And the theology we have inherited has not given us much help in reading the land we stand on.

---

The Bible begins with land. Not with individual souls and their private transactions with God, but with the earth — *ha'aretz* — declared good, filled with creatures, entrusted to humanity for cultivation and care (Genesis 1:28-31, 2:15). The first command is not a moral law. It is a vocation: tend this. Keep it. The original human task is not primarily religious in the narrow sense. It is agricultural, ecological, custodial. The human being is set into the garden to serve it — the Hebrew *abad*, to serve, is the same word used for the service of the Levitical priests. Tending the earth is liturgy.

The fall has a land dimension. When the first human beings fracture their relationship with God, the fracture extends immediately into the soil: "cursed is the ground because of you" (Genesis 3:17). The rupture between humanity and God produces a rupture between humanity and the earth. The biblical vision of redemption is therefore not the evacuation of souls from a material world that is simply discarded. It is the restoration of the whole system — the repair of the relationship between the Creator, the human creature, and the created world. The prophetic vision of shalom is explicitly ecological: mountains dripping with sweet wine, threshing floors full of grain, vats overflowing with wine and oil (Joel 2:24). Isaiah's vision of the new creation includes wolves and lambs together, children playing over the serpent's den (Isaiah 11:6-9). These are images of the earth healed, not the earth abandoned.

---

In the Hebrew covenant, land is not property in the modern sense. It is gift. It is trust. The Torah's land theology is revolutionary and remains largely unabsorbed by the contemporary church: the land belongs to God, not to the people who occupy it. "The land shall not be sold permanently, for the land is mine. For you are strangers and sojourners with me" (Leviticus 25:23). The Jubilee ordinance that follows from this premise — the return of land to its original families every fifty years, the cancellation of debt, the release of those held in bondage — is precisely a structural mechanism for preventing the permanent accumulation of land in the hands of the wealthy at the expense of the poor. The land cannot be finally sold because it was never finally the seller's to sell.

The prophets returned to this again and again because the accumulation of land was exactly what the wealthy of Israel were doing. "Woe to those who join house to house, who add field to field, until there is no more room, and you are made to dwell alone in the midst of the land" (Isaiah 5:8). Micah indicted those who devised wickedness in the night and implemented it in the morning — who coveted fields and seized them, houses and took them away (Micah 2:1-2). The prophetic indictment of land accumulation is not incidental to the prophetic witness. It is central.

The church has largely read these texts as spiritualized allegory or as specific historical instructions for ancient Israel, neither of which applies to modern property law. But the theological premise — that the earth is God's and that human ownership is always a form of stewardship, always conditional, always accountable — does not depend on the specific legal mechanisms of Levitical law. It is a claim about the nature of creation, not a piece of agricultural legislation.

---

Land theft is as old as the covenant people's temptation to acquire it. The story of Naboth's vineyard in 1 Kings 21 is among the most pointed land texts in Scripture. Ahab, the king of Israel, wants Naboth's vineyard for a vegetable garden. He offers to buy it or to trade for it. Naboth refuses: "The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers" (1 Kings 21:3). Naboth is not being stubborn or irrational. He is operating within a theological framework in which the ancestral land is a covenant inheritance, not a commodity to be traded at the king's convenience.

Jezebel solves the problem by arranging Naboth's judicial murder and confiscating the land. Elijah is sent to confront Ahab in the vineyard itself, the specific scene of the crime: "Have you killed and also taken possession?" (1 Kings 21:19). The question is one of the most compact prophetic indictments in Scripture. The murder and the land theft are named together as a single act. The prophet does not address one without the other.

This is the pattern. In the biblical imagination, land acquisition and violence are inseparable. Land is not an abstraction. It is the site of human life — the place where children grow up, where the dead are buried, where the family practices its vocation and maintains its economic security. To take the land is to take all of that.

---

The American church stands on land with a specific history. The continent was occupied before European settlement. The displacement of Indigenous peoples from their land was accomplished through a combination of treaty, military force, disease, and legal mechanisms — and the church was implicated in that displacement in multiple, documented ways. The doctrine of discovery — the fifteenth-century papal principle that gave Christian nations the right to claim territory inhabited by non-Christian peoples [cite — confirm] — provided the theological cover for centuries of colonial land seizure. The residential school system that forcibly removed Indigenous children from their communities and attempted to eliminate their cultures was operated in significant part by Christian denominations. [cite — confirm]

This is not ancient history. Many of those residential schools operated into the twentieth century. [cite — confirm] The land on which many American church buildings stand was taken from Indigenous communities within recorded history, in some cases within the lifetimes of people still living.

The same structural pattern repeated itself in the dispossession of Black land ownership in the South during and after Reconstruction — a period when Black families, having been prohibited from owning property for generations, acquired land only to have it taken by legal manipulation, by violence, and by the system of convict leasing that effectively re-enslaved formerly free people. [cite — confirm] The church watched, and in many cases participated.

---

None of this means that the contemporary church must hand its building back to someone. It means the contemporary church must know the story it is standing in. The pastor who leads a congregation on ground with this kind of history and never names it is not being neutral. He is choosing not to look.

Walter Brueggemann, in *The Land: Place as Gift, Promise, and Challenge in Biblical Faith* [cite — confirm], argues that land is one of the most important and underexamined categories in biblical theology — that the biblical story is fundamentally a story about land, about the promise of it and the betrayal of it and the hope of its restoration. The exile is not primarily about individuals losing God's favor. It is about a community losing its land and being forced to reckon with what the land meant. The return from exile is not primarily about individual spiritual renewal. It is about coming home to a specific place.

The American church has tried to be a community without a place — or rather, a community that occupies a place without owning its history. That is not an option the Bible gives us. The God of Israel is the God who met his people at specific places — Bethel, Sinai, Jerusalem — and who named those places as sites of encounter and accountability. The land remembers what the people have done on it. The prophets were certain of that.

---

What does a theology of land ask of the church today?

It asks for honesty about the ground we stand on and the history that put it in our hands. It asks for the theological seriousness to take Leviticus 25 and Isaiah 5 and Micah 2 as words addressed to us, not just to ancient Israel. It asks for engagement with the Indigenous communities whose land we occupy — not as a program, but as a long-term relationship built on acknowledgment and accountability.

It asks for the courage to say, in the language of the psalmist: "The earth is the LORD's, and everything in it" (Psalm 24:1). Not as a worship chorus. As a claim that reorganizes our relationship to the ground beneath our feet.

The land is not ours. We are stewards of it. We are accountable for what happens on it and what we do with it.

That accountability has not yet been fully faced.

It is waiting.
