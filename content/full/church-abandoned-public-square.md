There is a story American Christianity tells about itself regarding public life — that the church retreated from politics to protect the gospel, that the separation of church and state is a theological commitment as much as a constitutional one, that keeping faith private preserves its integrity. It is a coherent story. It is also, historically, mostly false. And understanding why the church abandoned the public square requires understanding that the retreat was not a principled stand. It was a defeat that was later reframed as virtue.

The church was not always absent from the public square. It was, for centuries, the primary architect of it.

In the medieval synthesis — the world that Dante describes, that Aquinas systematized — the categories of the sacred and the secular had not yet been pried apart in the modern sense. The church managed hospitals, educated children, structured the calendar of civic life, and adjudicated disputes that we would now call civil matters. This was not an arrangement without serious problems. Power concentrated in institutions that claimed divine mandate produced the abuses the Reformers rightly named. But the pre-modern church did not suffer from the particular pathology that afflicts its modern descendant: the belief that faith is essentially a private affair between an individual and God, with no particular claim on the arrangements of public life.

The Reformers themselves did not embrace that privatization. Luther's controversy with Rome was about the terms of salvation, not a declaration that salvation had no public implications. Calvin's Geneva was an attempt — however coercive and problematic — to order a city around theological convictions. The English Puritans crossed the Atlantic not simply to practice faith in private but to construct a "city on a hill" — Winthrop's phrase from Matthew 5:14 — that would be a public demonstration of what covenant life looked like. This was not the church abandoning the public square. It was the church staking an enormous claim in it.

The rupture came later. And it came in waves.

The first wave was the Enlightenment, which constructed a new account of public reason — a way of arguing about common life that proceeded from natural reason rather than revealed religion. Locke's political philosophy, Kant's moral philosophy, and eventually Jefferson's constitutional settlement proposed that the public square could be governed by principles accessible to everyone, regardless of their religious commitments. This was not, in its original intention, an attack on religion. It was an attempt to create a shared civic space in which people of different faith commitments could nonetheless reason together. But it had a long-term consequence that its architects did not fully anticipate: it gradually established the norms of public discourse in terms that systematically disadvantaged arguments grounded in Scripture or tradition.

The second wave was the Scopes Trial of 1925. The historiography of that moment has been thoroughly revised since H.L. Mencken wrote his contemptuous dispatches from Dayton, Tennessee [cite — confirm], but the cultural damage was real. Whatever the merits of the legal and scientific arguments, the trial became, in the popular imagination, a symbol of religion as backward, anti-intellectual, and unsuited for serious public engagement. The fundamentalist response was, in large part, a strategic withdrawal — a decision to build parallel institutions (schools, seminaries, radio networks) rather than to contest the mainstream public square. That withdrawal felt like faithfulness. It had the effect of ceding the terms of public discourse to those who had defined religion out of it.

The third wave was the post-World War II suburbanization of American Christianity. The church that moved to the suburbs moved with a particular class and a particular set of interests. The prophetic tradition — the tradition of Amos and Micah and Jeremiah, the tradition of speaking uncomfortable truth to power, the tradition that got prophets expelled from the sanctuary and executed outside the city gates — that tradition was not compatible with the suburban church's primary social function, which was to bless the aspirations of its members and provide a morally serious backdrop for a comfortable life. Walter Brueggemann has spent a career documenting what the church lost when it accepted this settlement [cite — confirm]. What it lost was the capacity to stand outside the dominant culture and name what the dominant culture refused to name.

This is the thing that needs to be said directly: the church did not abandon the public square because it had a theology that demanded privatized faith. The church that gave us Wilberforce and the abolitionist movement did not believe that. The church that gave us Bonhoeffer did not believe that. The church that animated the Civil Rights movement — which was a profoundly theological movement before it was a political one — did not believe that. The church that abandoned the public square did so because engagement was costly and withdrawal was comfortable, and it constructed a theology to justify the withdrawal after the fact.

The price of that construction has been high. When the church has no voice in the public square — when it has defined itself as an institution that addresses private faith and not common life — it has no standing to speak about the systems that impoverish the people it claims to serve. It can feed the hungry at the food pantry on Thursday morning, but it cannot address the wage structures and agricultural policies and housing policies that produce the hunger. It has voluntarily amputated the prophetic arm of its witness and then wondered why its service is so inadequate to the scale of the suffering it encounters.

I want to be precise about what I am not arguing. I am not arguing that the church should become a partisan political actor, that pastors should endorse candidates, or that the church's task is the capture of political power. The Constantinian settlement — the church aligning itself with the empire — produced its own devastating distortions of the gospel that took centuries to name clearly. Reinhold Niebuhr was right that Christians who enter politics must account for the particular temptations of power, including the temptation to baptize their own preferences with divine authority [cite — confirm].

But that is a counsel of caution about how the church engages the public square, not a counsel to avoid it. And the church has used legitimate cautions about political entanglement as cover for a wholesale retreat from prophetic engagement — from speaking publicly about the conditions that produce poverty, the systems that perpetuate injustice, the specific arrangements of power that determine whose children have full stomachs and whose do not.

The prophetic tradition of Israel was not primarily about elections. It was about the conduct of kings, the treatment of the poor, the fairness of the courts, the honesty of the markets. The prophets named specific practices — the taking of bribes in the gate (Amos 5:12), the joining of house to house and field to field until there is no room for anyone else (Isaiah 5:8), the grinding of the faces of the poor (Isaiah 3:15). They were not interested in the separation of religion from public life. That separation had not yet been invented. They were interested in whether the arrangements of public life honored the God who made all people in his image.

The church that has accepted the modern settlement — faith in the pew, silence in the square — has accepted a settlement that none of the prophets would have recognized as faithfulness. It has confused political non-partisanship with prophetic witness, when they are two entirely different things. The church can decline to endorse a candidate and still name what is unjust. It can refuse to be captured by any political party and still speak clearly about the conditions under which the image of God is honored or dishonored in a society.

What would it mean for the church to return to the public square without repeating the pathologies that made the withdrawal seem like a reasonable response?

It would mean recovering the distinction between prophetic witness and partisan power. The prophets had no interest in running the empire. They were interested in speaking truth to the empire about the terms of covenant faithfulness. The church can learn this distinction. It is not easy — the temptation to translate prophetic conviction into political power is strong, and the machinery of partisan politics is designed to absorb and instrumentalize religious energy. But the distinction is real, and it is the beginning of a public witness that does not become what it opposes.

It would mean recovering the full arc of what Scripture requires of public life. This is not a selection of proof texts about marriage and sexuality, though those texts exist and have standing. It is the full weight of what the canon demands: care for the widow, the orphan, and the stranger (Deuteronomy 10:18); honest weights and measures (Leviticus 19:35-36); the cancellation of debt in the year of jubilee (Leviticus 25); justice in the courts for the poor (Leviticus 19:15); the principle that the worker deserves his wages (Deuteronomy 24:15). A church that engages public life on the full terms of what Scripture demands will be comfortable in no political camp, which is exactly where it belongs.

It would mean the church being willing to speak when speaking is costly. The church that only speaks when its speech is welcome is not a prophetic voice. It is a chaplain. Chaplains are necessary. They are not prophets. The test of whether the church has actually returned to the public square is whether it is willing to speak the truth that costs it something — relationships, donors, influence, political access — because the truth is what the moment requires.

The church abandoned the public square. The abandonment was not a theological achievement. It was a failure of nerve dressed as a principle.

The question now is whether the nerve can be recovered.

Not to win. Not to rule. To witness. To speak. To stand where the prophets stood — at the gate of the city, where commerce and justice were administered, where the poor and the powerful moved through the same space — and say what God requires of the arrangements by which people live together.

That is the church's calling in the public square. It has always been.
