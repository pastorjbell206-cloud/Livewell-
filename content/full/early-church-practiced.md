The text is not ambiguous. Acts 2:44-45: "All the believers were together and had everything in common. They sold property and possessions to give to anyone who had need." Acts 4:32-34: "All the believers were one in heart and mind. No one claimed that any of their possessions was their own, but they shared everything they had... God's grace was so powerfully at work in them all that there were no needy persons among them. For from time to time those who owned land or houses sold them, brought the money from the sales and put it at the apostles' feet, and it was distributed to anyone who had need."

No needy persons among them.

That is a remarkable claim. It is also a description of a practice — not a theology, not an aspiration, not a metaphor. A practice. The early church, in Jerusalem, in the first years after Pentecost, had organized its common life so that there were no needy persons among them. They sold assets. They distributed to anyone who had need. The sharing was not voluntary in the sense that contemporary evangelicals mean when they use that word — meaning, you can do it if you want to but the expectation is that you will keep what is yours. The sharing was constitutive. It was what the community was.

This text has been handled in several ways by American Christianity, most of them designed to neutralize it.

The most common move is the historical-theological one: this was an early, unique expression of the Jerusalem church, shaped by the specific expectation that Christ would return imminently, and it did not work even for them — see Acts 6 and the dispute over the distribution to Hellenistic widows. The practice was unsustainable and was not repeated in the Pauline churches. The collections Paul organizes in 1 Corinthians 16 and 2 Corinthians 8-9 are voluntary and proportional, not total.

This argument is partially right. The Jerusalem community faced specific conditions — the concentration of believers in a single city, the hostility of the surrounding religious establishment, the early theological expectation of an imminent end — that made a particular form of economic sharing natural. The precise form of Acts 2 was not replicated wholesale across the Pauline mission churches.

But the argument is also evasive. Because Paul's collections for the Jerusalem church in 2 Corinthians 8 do not describe a different theology of economic sharing — they describe the same theology in a different form. "For if the willingness is there, the gift is acceptable according to what one has, not according to what one does not have. Our desire is not that others might be relieved while you are hard pressed, but that there might be equality. At the present time your plenty will supply what they need, so that in turn their need will supply what you need" (2 Corinthians 8:12-14). The goal Paul names is *equality* — the Greek word is *isotes*, meaning equalization. The goal is not charity. The goal is the elimination of the disparity. That is continuous with Acts 2, not a departure from it.

The second move is the individualist one: this was a voluntary act of individual generosity, not a community practice, and it teaches us about the spirit of generosity rather than any specific economic arrangement. Barnabas was admired for selling his field and bringing the money to the apostles (Acts 4:36-37). Ananias and Sapphira were judged not for withholding money — Peter says explicitly, "Wasn't the land yours before you sold it? And after it was sold, wasn't the money at your disposal?" (Acts 5:4) — but for lying about the amount. Therefore this is about individual integrity and generous hearts, not about any particular economic practice.

This argument also fails, for the same reason: it addresses the mechanism while ignoring the aim. The community aim, stated plainly, was that there would be no needy persons among them. However that aim was achieved — through voluntary sales, through communal distribution, through proportional giving — the aim itself was the standard. The question the text puts to the contemporary church is not "did they have a mandatory sharing policy?" but "do you have any needy persons among you?" And if you do — and you do — what does that say about the adequacy of your current practice?

The third move is the most honest and the most uncomfortable: yes, this is what the early church practiced, and no, the American church is not practicing it, and the gap between those two realities is one of the most significant ways that the American church has accommodated itself to the values of its surrounding culture rather than the values of its Lord.

The surrounding culture teaches that what you earn is yours and what you do with it is your private business. The New Testament teaches that everything belongs to God and that the community of Jesus is accountable for the condition of its most vulnerable members. These are not compatible claims. The American evangelical church, for the most part, has resolved the tension in favor of the surrounding culture and called it a theological position. It is not a theological position. It is a capitulation.

Deuteronomy 15:7-11 sets the expectation in terms that leave little interpretive room: "If anyone is poor among your fellow Israelites in any of the towns of the land the LORD your God is giving you, do not be hardhearted or tightfisted toward them. Rather, be openhanded and freely lend them whatever they need." The text then addresses the natural objection — what if a sabbath year is coming and I will lose the debt? — and answers it with a command: "Do not be hardhearted or tightfisted toward them." The economic system of Israel included mechanisms — gleaning laws, sabbath years, Jubilee — specifically designed to prevent the permanent accumulation of wealth by some and the permanent destitution of others. These were not voluntary. They were covenantal obligations.

The early church understood itself as the fulfillment of this covenantal tradition, reinterpreted through the resurrection and the arrival of the Spirit. The community that had the Spirit of God was the community in which the vision of Deuteronomy — no needy persons among you — was actually realized. The fact that they failed to sustain it perfectly, that Acts 6 describes a distribution dispute, that Paul has to correct the Corinthians' behavior at the Lord's Table (1 Corinthians 11:17-34) because the wealthy are not waiting for the poor — none of that changes the standard. It confirms that the standard was real and that failing to meet it was treated as a failure, not a theological alternative.

The contemporary church that has replaced this vision with individual tithing to a budget that the congregation does not control and does not understand has not recovered the theology of Acts 2. It has institutionalized a different thing and called it the same. And I am not standing outside that observation. I have led a church with a budget, received tithes, and distributed money through programs without asking whether the people in my pews who were struggling had what they needed. The institutional structure I was working within was not designed to know. It was designed to fund.

The question the text puts to us is not "are we doing the Acts 2 experiment again?" The early Jerusalem church's specific form was time-bound and historically particular. The question is: do the people in your congregation who have need know that the community will meet it? Do they feel the freedom to say they have need? Is the economic life of your community organized in any way around the principle that no one among you should go without what they need while others have abundance?

If the answer is no — and for most American congregations, the answer is no — then the question is not whether Acts 2 is binding. The question is what we are actually practicing, and what that practice reveals about what we actually believe.

The early church practiced something the contemporary American church mostly will not. The gap is not a minor liturgical preference. It is a theological statement about whether the resurrection changed anything real about how we hold our stuff.

That statement is still being made. We are making it right now, with our budgets and our boundaries and our carefully maintained financial privacy in the company of the people we call brothers and sisters.

No needy persons among them.

We should not read past that phrase as quickly as we have been reading past it.

The early church did not hold this standard perfectly. Acts 6 shows the community in dispute over fairness in the daily distribution. The Corinthians were shamed for their behavior at the Lord's Table, where the wealthy ate their fill while the poor went hungry at the very meal meant to declare their equality (1 Corinthians 11:20-22). The letters of James and John address wealthy members who were treating the poor badly. The record is not a record of communities that had arrived at shalom. It is a record of communities that held the standard, failed to meet it, were corrected, and were expected to try again.

That is the tradition we are called to inhabit. Not the tradition that achieved Acts 2:44 and moved on. The tradition that named the failure, returned to the standard, and kept going.

The contemporary church has largely stopped naming the failure. It has replaced the standard with a more comfortable metric and called it a theology of grace. That is not grace. Grace is what we need when we fail to meet a real standard. Replacing the standard is something else entirely.
