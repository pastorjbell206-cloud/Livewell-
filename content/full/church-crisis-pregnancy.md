A woman in a crisis pregnancy is not a political cause. She is a person in the middle of the hardest decision she may ever face, usually alone, often afraid, and listening — whether she says so or not — to see if anyone in the room actually cares about her.

The church has often failed this moment. Not by having a wrong position, but by having a position without a presence. By being louder in the public square than in the hospital waiting room. By caring more about what she decides than about what happens to her after she decides it.

This is not a minor discrepancy. It is the difference between having a theology of life and performing one.

## Ruth and the Shape of Covenantal Love

Ruth's story is, among other things, a study in what unconditional loyalty actually looks like when someone has nothing to offer in return. Naomi had lost her husband and both sons. She was returning to Bethlehem in poverty, without prospects, and without the capacity to provide Ruth with anything resembling a future. She told her daughters-in-law to return to their own families and gods. The reasonable thing for Ruth to do was to take that permission and use it.

Ruth refused. "Where you go I will go, and where you stay I will stay. Your people will be my people and your God my God" (Ruth 1:16). The word behind this refusal — hesed, covenantal loyalty — appears throughout the book and describes not only Ruth's commitment to Naomi but God's commitment to both of them. Hesed does not calculate whether the other person deserves the commitment. It extends the commitment precisely when the other person cannot reciprocate.

The church's response to the woman in crisis pregnancy needs to take that shape. Not a conditional welcome contingent on the right outcome. Not support that evaporates when the decision has been made. Not presence that extends only as far as the delivery room. A loyalty that persists through the outcome into whatever comes after it — including the outcomes that are complicated, the outcomes that do not match what the church hoped for, the outcomes that are expensive and long and require more than a one-time donation to a crisis pregnancy center.

## What a Specific Woman Actually Needs

The mistake the church most frequently makes is moving from principle to program without passing through the person. The principle is clear: human life is sacred, the unborn child deserves protection, women deserve support. The program gets launched: a pregnancy resource center, a baby shower fund, a partnership with an adoption agency. The program is real and it is good and it still misses the thing the woman in the room most immediately requires.

What she needs first is someone who will sit with her before someone who will advise her. The difference is not small. The advisor has something to offer — information, options, theological perspective, practical resources. The person who sits with her first is offering something the advisor cannot: evidence that she is known before she is assisted, that her experience matters before her decision does, that the church's first move is presence and not management.

She needs material support — not a pamphlet with a list of resources, but actual housing, actual food, actual childcare, actual prenatal care, offered by people who will be available after the referral has been made. The woman who is afraid to continue a pregnancy because she cannot afford to house a child and keep her job and manage without any family network nearby is not facing a crisis of conviction. She is facing a crisis of infrastructure. The church that gives her a pro-life sermon when she needs a co-signer on an apartment has missed the assignment.

She needs the community to still be present six months after the decision, when the attention has moved on. The pastoral bandwidth that flows toward a woman in crisis pregnancy tends to peak around the decision and drop sharply once the immediate drama has resolved. But the woman who chose life and is now alone with a three-month-old and no sleep and a job she can barely hold and no one returning her texts — she is in a different kind of crisis, one that the church's programmatic response was not designed to address. She needs the community to still be there.

She needs to know that her shame, whatever it is and wherever it comes from, is not the last word the church will speak over her.

## "Carry Each Other's Burdens"

Paul's instruction in Galatians 6:2 — "Carry each other's burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ" — is not metaphorical. He is not describing emotional support. The Greek word for burden, baros, refers to a weight so heavy that bearing it alone is not possible. It is the weight you carry when someone helps, not the inconvenience you manage on your own.

The law of Christ that Paul says this burden-carrying fulfills is not the promotion of a particular legislative outcome. It is the carrying of actual weight alongside actual people. The fulfillment of that law is not achieved by voting correctly or by funding a crisis pregnancy center's annual gala. It is achieved by specific people taking on specific weight for specific women over extended and inconvenient periods of time.

I do not think most congregations have a fully honest picture of how much that costs. A crisis pregnancy is not a discrete event that ends at birth. It is the beginning of a life that will require support for years. The woman who chose life with the church's encouragement and then found herself abandoned by the church's follow-through has been given something and then had it taken away, and that is worse than not having been given it at all.

## The Counter-Argument About Institutional Limits

The honest counter-argument is that the church is not a social services agency and cannot be expected to function as one. Congregations have finite budgets, finite volunteer bandwidth, and a primary calling to worship and proclamation that must not be colonized by ancillary social programs. The attempt to turn every church into a comprehensive support system for every crisis it encounters produces congregational burnout and institutional diffusion that ultimately serves no one well.

This argument has real force. The church is not the government. It cannot be expected to solve systemic problems with volunteer programs. There is a real danger in the expectation that congregational care can substitute for the structural supports — healthcare, childcare policy, housing assistance, parental leave — that would actually change the conditions in which crisis pregnancies occur.

But the counter-argument proves less than it claims. It establishes that the church cannot do everything; it does not establish that the church should do nothing beyond its political position. The gap between "we cannot solve the problem systematically" and "we can care for this specific person in our congregation who is in front of us" is large, and most congregations have not come close to exhausting the second before claiming the impossibility of the first.

The church does not need to be a social services agency to show up for the woman in its own building. It does not need a comprehensive program to offer a meal, a ride, a co-signed lease, a friend who will return texts at midnight. These things require willingness more than infrastructure.

## What the Political Position Without Presence Actually Communicates

When the church is loudest about abortion in the abstract and quietest about the specific woman in the third row who is considering one, it is communicating something. Not what it intends to communicate — not that it has a high view of human life. What it is actually communicating is that the theological position is more important than the person the position is about.

That is a communicative failure with consequences. It tells the woman in crisis that the church's concern is the outcome, not her. It tells the woman who has already made the choice that there is no room for her here. It tells the watching world that the church's pro-life commitment is primarily a political identity rather than a pastoral practice.

The church that is pro-life in its politics but not in its practice has chosen the cheaper version of the conviction. The more demanding version requires presence — not once, but all the way through. Through the decision and past it. Through the pregnancy and past it. Through the delivery and past it. Through the first year and the second and the third, until the woman who received the church's commitment is actually standing on her own feet and can say, with something more than charity, that the church's presence made the choice possible.

That version of the commitment is harder than a vote. It is also the version that takes the conviction seriously enough to pay for it with something other than other people's money and other people's time.

She is still in the room. She is still listening. What the church does next is the sermon she will actually hear.
