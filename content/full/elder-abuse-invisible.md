The law comes to Moses in the wilderness, and among its provisions is a command that recurs with enough insistence to suggest it is load-bearing for the whole structure: *Stand up in the presence of the aged, show respect for the elderly and revere your God* (Leviticus 19:32). The instruction appears in the same chapter as the commands to leave grain at the field's edge for the poor and the foreigner, to not defraud or rob your neighbor, to not oppress the hired worker. The honor of the elderly is not a category of sentimental social custom. It is law, in the same legal register as the prohibitions on theft and oppression.

The church has preached this in the general. It has largely missed it in the specific. The specific is this: elder abuse — the physical, emotional, financial, and sexual abuse of older adults — is one of the most widespread and underreported forms of violence in America, and the church has treated it as though it is not there.

Approximately one in ten Americans over the age of sixty experiences some form of elder abuse each year [cite — confirm]. That figure is almost certainly an undercount. Elder abuse is notoriously underreported because its victims are often dependent on their abusers, isolated from community, cognitively impaired, or living with the particular shame of being abused by the people they trusted most. The perpetrators are frequently family members — adult children, spouses, paid caregivers — which means the victim faces a choice between naming the abuse and losing the relationship or remaining silent and enduring the harm. Most remain silent.

The church is in a position to see what the general society misses. The older adults who attend services, who receive pastoral visits, who are known to the congregation by name — these are exactly the people whose isolation and dependence can be interrupted by genuine community. A pastor who visits, an elder who checks in, a deacon who notices that the woman who used to be in every Sunday is missing — these are not extraordinary acts. They are the ordinary functions of a community that takes its theology of the elderly seriously.

But this requires the church to first take the problem seriously.

The invisibility of elder abuse in the church's prophetic discourse is partly a function of the invisibility of the elderly in the broader culture. The United States does not have a strong theology of aging. The culture of youth, productivity, and independence treats the old as people who have finished doing the things that matter and are now waiting for what comes next. The congregation reflects this: the programs, the growth strategies, the outreach efforts are overwhelmingly oriented toward young adults, young families, the demographic groups the church considers its future. The elderly are present, often generously present in their giving and service, but they are rarely the focus of deliberate pastoral attention.

This is a theological failure before it is a programmatic one. The biblical tradition does not treat the elderly as people who have finished being useful. Psalm 92:12–14 describes the righteous who still bear fruit in old age, full of sap and green — an image of continued vitality and contribution that contradicts the culture's account of aging as primarily loss. The wisdom tradition in Proverbs and Job treats the accumulated experience of the elderly as a genuine form of knowledge that the young cannot access through their own effort. Leviticus 19:32 makes the honor of the aged a direct expression of reverence for God — *revere your God*, the command concludes, which means that dismissal of the elderly is not simply a social failure. It is a theological one.

The specific forms of elder abuse deserve concrete naming, because the church tends to engage more readily with what it can see and name.

Financial exploitation is the most common form, and it frequently goes undetected in congregational settings. An older congregant gradually transfers control of finances to an adult child who begins to use those resources for personal benefit. A cognitively declining member signs documents she does not understand. A paid caregiver with power of attorney begins to access accounts. The external presentation at church can remain entirely normal — the person arrives on Sunday, appears to be cared for, seems comfortable — while the exploitation proceeds invisibly at home. The pastoral relationship that includes genuine, regular conversation about how the person is actually living is the thing that can interrupt this pattern.

Physical and emotional abuse are less common but more severe. The older adult who arrives at church with unexplained injuries, who flinches at certain interactions, who seems afraid to speak honestly in the presence of a particular family member — these are signs the congregation is equipped to see if it has been taught to see them. Most have not.

Sexual abuse of the elderly is the form most difficult to discuss, and therefore most completely absent from the church's attention. It happens, disproportionately in institutional settings and at the hands of trusted caregivers [cite — confirm]. The silence around it in the church is not different from the silence around every other form of sexual abuse the church has been slow to name. The same dynamics — shame, disbelief, the instinct to protect institutions and relationships — operate here. The corrective is the same: naming it, creating safe channels for disclosure, believing the person who reports it.

Isolation is both a form of abuse and the condition that makes other forms possible. The abuser who separates an older adult from the community that would notice — from the congregation, from the adult children who don't live nearby, from the neighbors — has created the conditions in which everything else becomes possible. The congregation that maintains regular contact with its older members is not only providing fellowship. It is providing protection.

Proverbs 31:8–9 issues a direct command: *Speak up for those who cannot speak for themselves, for the rights of all who are destitute. Speak up and judge fairly; defend the rights of the poor and needy.* The older adult whose cognitive impairment or social isolation has made self-advocacy impossible is exactly the person this command is addressing. The church that regards the command as applying in principle but lacks the structures to implement it in practice has not yet obeyed the command.

The steel-man for the pastoral constraint is real: the church is not a social services agency. Pastors are not trained as elder abuse investigators or geriatric care specialists. The complexity of family systems, the legal dimensions of intervention, the risk of false accusation — these are genuine considerations that should not be dismissed. A congregation that begins accusing families on the basis of incomplete information can do serious harm.

This is true. And it does not resolve the obligation. The response to complexity is not silence. It is preparation: training congregational care teams in the signs of elder abuse, building relationships with social service agencies and adult protective services, creating clear channels through which members can raise concerns, and cultivating the kind of pastoral relationship with older congregants that makes honest disclosure possible. None of this requires the pastor to be a forensic investigator. It requires the congregation to take seriously that the people it has been entrusted with include some of the most vulnerable people in its community.

The theological test the prophets return to, again and again, is the treatment of those who cannot protect themselves. The widow, the orphan, the stranger — the triad that appears across Exodus, Deuteronomy, the Psalms, and the prophets as the measure of the community's faithfulness. The list is not exhaustive. It is a principle: God's interest is particularly with the ones who are vulnerable to exploitation because they lack the power to prevent it. The elderly person who is cognitively declining, financially dependent on an adult child, isolated from community, and afraid to speak — that person is exactly the human being the prophetic tradition most demands the community protect.

The congregation that walks past this vulnerability — that attends to the programs and the preferences of the visible and the vocal while the elderly sit quietly and endure what they are enduring — has not failed a program objective. It has failed the theological test.

I have been in rooms with elderly congregants and known something was not right and said nothing because I did not know what to say, because I did not want to create conflict, because I was not sure. I have prioritized my comfort over their safety in moments when the pastoral call was to speak. That is my part in the failure I am describing. Naming it does not absolve me. It identifies what the correction requires.

Honor the face of the old man (Leviticus 19:32, ESV). Not as sentiment. Not as courtesy. As law, grounded in reverence for the God who made him — and who will hold accountable the community that looked away.
