There is a cowardice that dresses itself as respect. You do not press on certain areas. You keep the conversation in safe waters. You visit when called, offer what is asked, and take careful note of the check in the offering plate. You are professionally warm and spiritually incurious about the one domain of this person's life that Jesus spent more time on than almost any other.

We call this pastoral care. The Gospels would not recognize it.

---

Jesus was not afraid of wealthy people. He ate with them. He was moved by them. He loved them specifically — the rich young ruler is the only person in the Synoptic Gospels of whom Mark says Jesus, looking at him, loved him (Mark 10:21). The love was not a dismissal. The love was the reason for the hard word that followed. "Go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me." The man went away sorrowful. He had many possessions. And Jesus, watching him leave, did not soften the conclusion: "How difficult it will be for those who have wealth to enter the kingdom of God" (Mark 10:23).

The disciples were astonished. They had a category for wealth that Jesus was now explicitly dismantling — the assumption, inherited from a certain reading of the Deuteronomic tradition, that prosperity was a sign of divine favor and poverty a mark of divine discipline. Jesus dismantles it with an image so stark that interpreters have been retreating from it ever since: it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God (Mark 10:25).

There is no metaphorical exit from that sentence. No ancient gate called "the needle's eye" that camels could pass through with some effort. Jesus means what he says. Wealth is dangerous. It is spiritually dangerous in a way that requires the specific pastoral attention he gave to no other condition. And the danger is not primarily external — not a matter of what the wealth does to others, though that is real — but internal. It is what wealth does to the soul of the one who holds it.

---

This is the pastoral challenge with the wealthy congregant, and it is harder than most pastors acknowledge.

The challenge begins with access. Wealthy people in most congregations occupy a structural position that makes honest pastoral relationship genuinely difficult. They have resources the church needs. They sit on boards. They write checks that fund staff positions. They are known in the community in ways that carry social weight. The informal dynamics of a congregation's financial life create a kind of protective distance around the wealthy — not always out of deliberate calculation, but out of the accumulated weight of small decisions: who gets visited when the schedule fills, whose input is sought when decisions are made, whose discomfort prompts a quick recalibration.

I have been in conversations where I adjusted my pastoral approach because of what I knew about the family's financial situation. I have softened a line I intended to press. I have let a question die that deserved to live. The rationalization is available: this is the pastoral art, reading the room, timing the word for when it can be received. Sometimes that rationalization is correct. Often it is not. Often it is the pressure of money on the pulpit, operating below the level of conscious decision.

The prophet Micah named a version of this with unsparing specificity: "Its priests teach for a price, its prophets practice divination for money; yet they lean on the Lord and say, 'Is not the Lord in the midst of us? No disaster shall come upon us'" (Micah 3:11). The worst version is when the financial relationship is explicit and transactional. But the subtler version is when the financial relationship is simply present — present enough to shape what the pastor is willing to say and not say. The prophets called this corruption. We tend to call it wisdom.

---

The ancient church was not naïve about this dynamic. The Epistle of James confronts it in terms so direct they are still startling: "For if a man wearing a gold ring and fine clothing comes into your assembly, and a poor man in shabby clothing also comes in, and if you pay attention to the one who wears the fine clothing and say, 'You sit here in a good place,' while you say to the poor man, 'You stand over there,' or, 'Sit down at my feet,' have you not made distinctions among yourselves and become judges with evil thoughts?" (James 2:2-4). James is not describing an extreme case. He is describing the default of unreformed human social behavior in religious settings.

The default runs deep. Luke Timothy Johnson, in his work on the social world of the early church, traces how the patronage system of the Roman world shaped — and often distorted — the earliest Christian communities. [cite — confirm] The wealthy patron was a figure of enormous social power in that world. His (almost always his) favor was worth cultivating. His displeasure had consequences. The temptation for leaders of new communities was to accommodate these dynamics rather than confront them, which is precisely why James and Paul spend so much time confronting them.

The theology underneath the accommodation is worth naming. It is a functional prosperity theology — not the explicit variety of the television preacher who promises financial returns on faith, but the structural variety, the quiet assumption that the wealthy congregant's success represents a kind of divine approval that the church should not disturb. It protects the wealthy from the word they most need to hear: that their wealth is a spiritual emergency, not an occasion for congratulation.

---

Jesus's teaching on wealth is more sustained and more demanding than almost any other single topic in the Synoptics except the kingdom of God itself. The parable of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19-31) is not primarily about the afterlife. It is about the moral demand that proximity to suffering places on the wealthy. Lazarus was at the gate every day. The rich man knew he was there. The failure was not dramatic cruelty. It was the more common failure: the capacity to remain comfortable in the presence of suffering, to integrate that suffering into the background of a life organized around one's own comfort, and to continue in that life without the disruption the suffering should produce.

The parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14-30) has been domesticated by centuries of application to "using your gifts for God." But the economic context is specific and the judgment is specific: the servant who buried his master's money out of fear is condemned not for theft but for non-use. And in the broader narrative of Matthew 25, the criterion for final judgment is not theological correctness but what you did with the suffering in front of you: the hungry, the thirsty, the stranger, the naked, the sick, the imprisoned (Matthew 25:31-46). These are not spiritual categories. They are material ones.

---

What does faithful pastoral care of the wealthy congregant actually look like?

It begins with honest theological conversation about wealth — not as a rebuke, not as a fundraising strategy, but as the pastoral work of helping a person inhabit their faith in the part of their life that Jesus found most spiritually urgent. The question is not whether they tithe. The question is what wealth is doing to their soul: their capacity for trust, their relationship with anxiety, their ability to take risks for the sake of the kingdom, their posture toward the poor people whose lives are affected by the economic decisions they make as employers and investors and citizens.

It means that the prophetic word cannot be withheld on the basis of the check in the offering plate. This does not mean attacking the wealthy from the pulpit. It means treating them as the full adults they are, capable of receiving the word Jesus gave the rich young ruler — given with love, given directly, and not softened because the love makes the truth too awkward.

And it means the pastor must examine his own relationship with the wealthy people in his congregation. Not to manufacture suspicion. But to ask: is there a conversation I have been avoiding? Is there a topic I have consistently not addressed because of who is in the room? Have I given this person the pastoral care I would give anyone else — the genuine attention to where they are spiritually struggling, including in the place where Jesus most clearly identified their struggle?

The rich young ruler walked away. That was a possibility Jesus accepted. The alternative — softening the word so the man would stay — would have been the pastor's failure, not the man's freedom.

We preach about sacrifice to people who have little. We owe the same sermon to people who have much. Their souls are on the table at that moment just as much as anyone else's, and the silence that protects our institutional ease is not pastoral wisdom. It is a form of abandonment dressed in the language of tact.
