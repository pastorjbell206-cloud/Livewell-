We have treated creation care as a political category when it is a theological one. That confusion has cost us badly — and I mean the church, and I include myself.

The command arrives in Genesis 2:15 before anything else has gone wrong. Before the fall, before the flood, before any of the arrangements that might make the command conditional or complicated: *The Lord God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to work it and keep it.* The Hebrew word translated "keep" is *shamar* — the same word used for the watchful protection of a shepherd over his flock, the same word the Aaronic blessing employs: *the Lord bless you and keep you* (Numbers 6:24). To keep the garden is not to exploit it. It is to guard it with the attentiveness of someone who knows it belongs to another.

That framing is the one we have most thoroughly abandoned. The earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof (Psalm 24:1). Not ours. Not humanity's to dispose of as we choose. We are stewards, which is to say: accountable. Accountable to the one who made it, who declared it good, who will one day judge what we did with it.

The theological category here is stewardship, not ownership. The distinction matters enormously. The owner does what he likes with what is his. The steward answers for what was entrusted to him. The Genesis creation narrative insists on the stewardship frame: dominion (Genesis 1:28) is given to humanity as an exercise of royal responsibility, not as a license for extraction. A king who ruins his kingdom has failed as a king, regardless of the authority he held. The *imago Dei* — the image of God in which humanity is made — carries in the ancient Near Eastern context the specific connotation of royal representation: the one who bears the image bears responsibility for the realm [cite — confirm].

The evangelical tradition has been suspicious of creation care for the wrong reasons. Some of that suspicion is understandable — environmentalism has sometimes been accompanied by a thoroughgoing rejection of Christian anthropology, treating humanity as a parasite rather than a creature made in the image of God. That is a real error worth resisting. But the response to bad theology is not no theology. The response is better theology. And the better theology here is clear: dominion was never license for destruction. The Greek and Hebrew both carry the weight of responsibility, not only authority.

The historical shape of the evangelical suspicion is also worth tracing. Lynn White Jr.'s 1967 essay in Science argued that the Christian doctrine of dominion was the historical root of the environmental crisis — that by desacralizing nature and giving humanity absolute authority over it, Christianity had licensed the exploitation that was producing ecological disaster [cite — confirm]. The essay provoked a generation of responses, some defensive and some genuinely theological. The best responses did not simply deny White's argument. They engaged the text more carefully, distinguishing between the dominion of Genesis 1-2 and the exploitative reading that had sometimes been derived from it, and recovering the stewardship language that the exploitative reading had buried.

The evangelical tradition missed that recovery. Instead of engaging White's argument theologically, much of it dismissed the environmental concern as a secular political project and closed the door. That decision — made in the 1970s and 1980s primarily for cultural-political reasons rather than theological ones — has left the church without a coherent position on one of the defining moral questions of the present generation.

The dimension the church has been slowest to reckon with is justice. Environmental damage is not evenly distributed. The communities most exposed to polluted air, contaminated water, and the worst effects of a warming climate are, with remarkable consistency, the communities that are already poor, already marginalized, already the ones the prophets named as God's particular concern — the widow, the orphan, the stranger. The evidence for this pattern in the United States is not ambiguous [cite — confirm]. The communities bearing the heaviest environmental burden are not the ones who made the decisions that produced the burden. They received the cost without receiving the benefit.

To be indifferent to environmental destruction while claiming a heart for justice is not a coherent position. You cannot love your neighbor in the abstract and ignore the poison in his water.

I am not positioning this as a partisan argument. I am positioning it as what it is: a biblical argument. Amos condemned those who sold the poor for a pair of sandals (Amos 2:6). The economic and environmental arrangements of our time have their own versions of that transaction — arrangements where the costs of consumption are exported to people who had no vote in the decision. That pattern is not invisible to God. It was not invisible to the prophets. It should not be invisible to the church.

The Revelation 21-22 vision is striking in its materiality. The new creation is not an escape from the physical world but its renewal — a renewed earth, a renewed city, the tree of life with leaves for the healing of the nations. N.T. Wright argues at length that the Christian hope is not for the destruction of creation but for its liberation, that Romans 8:19-22 ("the whole creation has been groaning as in the pains of childbirth") is not a prelude to abandonment but to the liberation of creation from its bondage to decay [cite — confirm]. If the creation will be renewed and not discarded, then what we do to the creation now is not irrelevant to God's ultimate purposes. It matters.

The counter-position that requires honest engagement is this: if the earth is going to be renewed by God in the new creation, does it matter how we treat it now? Is not creation care at best a secondary concern — maintaining a ship that God has already decided to refurbish from the keel up? This argument has been more theologically influential than it is usually credited with being. The implicit eschatology of much American evangelicalism — a rapture theology that anticipates the departure of the righteous before the earth's final destruction — is not merely a view about chronology. It is a view about the earth's ultimate disposability, and disposable things are not worth protecting carefully.

The response is the one Wright and others have made: the eschatological renewal of creation does not license its present abuse any more than the promise of bodily resurrection licenses bodily self-destruction. If the body will be raised, that is an argument for its present dignity, not for its present negligence. If the creation will be renewed, that is an argument for treating it now with the care appropriate to something God regards as worth keeping.

The practical response is not primarily political, though political engagement is not excluded. It is personal and communal. The individual Christian who examines her consumption, who asks whether her ordinary choices are consistent with stewardship, who refuses to treat convenience as a theological trump card — that person is doing something prophetic, regardless of whether it shows up in any legislation. The congregation that reduces its building's environmental impact, that teaches its children to see creation as gift rather than resource, that advocates for clean air and clean water in its community as a justice matter rather than a partisan one — that congregation is bearing witness to the God who made the world and called it good.

This is not a call to make creation care the center of the congregation's life. It is a call to stop treating it as someone else's concern. The earth belongs to God, and we have been placed here to tend it. The man or woman who reads Genesis 2 and does not feel the weight of that responsibility has read it too quickly.

We were placed in a garden to work it and keep it. The garden is larger now. The accountability is the same.

The Noahic covenant (Genesis 9:8-17) is often read as God's promise to Noah. It is also God's explicit covenant with "every living creature" — the land animals, the birds, the animals of the field. The covenant is not made only with human beings. It encompasses the whole of the created order. God's commitment is not only to humanity but to creation itself. The theological implication is that creation has standing before God independently of its utility to humanity. The earth is not merely the stage on which human redemption is enacted. It is a covenant partner.

Psalm 104 meditates on the creation with a specificity that the church has rarely matched: the springs that give drink to every wild animal, the birds that sing among the branches, the young lions that roar for their prey and seek their food from God (Psalm 104:11, 12, 21). The psalmist is not making a general point about creation's goodness. He is cataloguing the particular, diverse, non-human lives that are sustained by God's direct provision. The world he describes is not a background for human activity. It is a community of creatures, each fed and sustained by the same God who feeds and sustains humanity.

To read that psalm and then treat the destruction of species and ecosystems as a matter of political preference rather than theological concern requires a selective reading of the text that the text itself does not permit.

The church's failure to engage creation care seriously has had institutional consequences beyond the ecological. It has ceded this issue entirely to movements that have no biblical framework, and it has thereby forfeited its capacity to offer a theological account of why creation matters — which is the only account that grounds the imperative deeply enough to sustain it across generations. Secular environmentalism can produce policy. It cannot produce the conviction that the earth is the Lord's, that its flourishing is God's concern, that the person who poisons water and air for profit is answerable to a court higher than any regulatory agency.

That account belongs to the church. It is time to reclaim it.

To keep it is to begin. We have been slow to begin, and the slowness has consequences for people with names and addresses who did not cause the problem they are living inside.
