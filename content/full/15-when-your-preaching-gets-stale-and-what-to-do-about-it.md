There is a particular shame that accompanies the recognition that your preaching has gone stale. You are a minister of the Word — your whole vocation is organized around the conviction that these ancient texts have living power, that the God who speaks through Scripture is still speaking, that the gospel is inexhaustible. And yet you are sitting in your study on a Tuesday afternoon and you cannot make yourself care about the passage you are supposed to preach on Sunday. You have said this before. You know what you think it means. You are going through the motions of a process that used to feel alive.

This is a more common experience than most pastors will admit publicly, and it is not a sign that the calling has ended or that the faith is gone. It is a sign that something in the preaching ecology has stopped being fed, and that a change — not a dramatic one, necessarily, but a real one — is needed.

## The History of This Particular Struggle

The staleness of preaching is not a modern problem. George Herbert, the seventeenth-century Anglican priest and poet whose collection *The Temple* contains some of the most theologically precise devotional poetry in the English language, wrote extensively about the condition he called "dryness" — the experience of coming to the altar or the pulpit in a state of spiritual flatness that felt like a betrayal of the office [cite — confirm]. His response to it is instructive: he did not conclude that the dryness invalidated the ministry or the faith. He concluded that it was a condition to be inhabited honestly, brought before God in its full weight, and allowed to speak in the sermons themselves.

Charles Spurgeon, who preached to thousands at the Metropolitan Tabernacle and was perhaps the most celebrated preacher of the nineteenth century, suffered periods of depression so severe that he would lie in his study unable to prepare, unable to rise, convinced that the words had stopped coming [cite — confirm]. He named this from the pulpit when it was over. He wrote about the "Minister's Fainting Fits" in his pastoral lectures — not as a shameful aberration but as a structural feature of the preaching life that required pastoral honesty rather than concealment.

The pattern runs through the history of preaching: the moments of staleness and dryness are not interruptions to the ministry. They are part of it. The preacher who expects to sustain thirty or forty years of weekly proclamation without seasons of flatness has misunderstood the nature of the vocation.

## Why Preaching Goes Stale

The most common cause of homiletical staleness is the collapse of genuine personal engagement with Scripture outside the preaching task. When a pastor's primary relationship with the Bible is as sermon raw material — texts to be mined for Sunday content — the hunger that makes preaching alive tends to diminish. The text becomes a task rather than an encounter, and the congregation senses it even when they cannot name what is different.

Walter Brueggemann has argued, in a different context, that the prophetic imagination requires what he calls "counter-cultural" practices — disciplines that create a kind of interiority that the dominant culture cannot produce and does not value [cite — confirm]. The preacher who has allowed the efficiency demands of ministry to crowd out genuine contemplative engagement with the text has, functionally, conformed to the dominant culture's logic rather than resisting it. The result is a preacher who processes texts rather than inhabits them.

A second common cause is the absence of genuine curiosity about the congregation's life. Preaching is always a dialogue, even if only one person is speaking. The preacher who is genuinely curious about what the people in front of them are actually experiencing — what they are afraid of, what they are hoping for, what they are carrying into the room — tends to preach sermons that engage those realities. The preacher who has stopped being curious about their congregation tends to preach at them rather than to them.

A third cause — less often named but very real — is the absence of genuine intellectual stimulation. The preacher who has stopped reading broadly, who has narrowed their intellectual diet to sermon preparation materials and ministry resources, will eventually run out of the material that preaching draws on: the understanding of the human condition, the historical imagination, the awareness of what is happening in the culture and why. The sermon becomes a closed loop — Scripture interpreted by Scripture, theological concepts arranged and rearranged without the friction of contact with the world outside the church.

## A Steel-Manned Counter-Position

The strongest version of the case against the diagnosis I have just offered goes like this: the preacher who is always looking for intellectual stimulation, who insists on fresh experience and new curiosity as preconditions for vital preaching, has made their own psychological state the center of the preaching enterprise. But the text does not change. The gospel does not change. The work of the Holy Spirit through the proclaimed Word does not depend on the preacher's subjective experience of freshness. The congregations who most need the Word preached are often in contexts where the preacher is exhausted, depressed, and experiencing no particular freshness whatsoever — and the Word preached under those conditions is not therefore less the Word. The preacher who waits to feel alive before they can preach has made their own interiority the gatekeeper of what the congregation receives.

This is a real concern. The theology of the Word's power is not dependent on the preacher's psychological state, and the pastor who has confused their own staleness with the gospel's inadequacy has made a category error.

But the argument proves too much. The same logic would exempt the preacher from any investment in their own formation, since the Word is powerful regardless of the instrument. We do not actually believe this. We invest in preaching training, homiletics, biblical scholarship, and pastoral formation because we believe that the instrument matters — not that it determines the outcome, but that it shapes the quality and depth of what the congregation receives. The preacher who is alive to the text, to the congregation, and to the world is not producing the power of the Spirit. They are removing impediments to it.

## Feeding the Preacher

The most direct remedy for homiletical staleness is an investment in the preacher's own spiritual and intellectual life that is entirely disconnected from sermon production. This means reading broadly — not just theological and pastoral works, but history, literature, biography, science, poetry, whatever expands the preacher's imagination and their understanding of the human condition. It means personal prayer and Scripture engagement that has no sermonic agenda. It means experiences that bring the preacher into genuine contact with realities outside their usual orbit.

Karl Barth is reported to have said that the preacher should preach with the Bible in one hand and the newspaper in the other [cite — confirm]. Whatever the accuracy of that specific attribution, the instinct is right: the preacher who is cut off from genuine contact with the world the congregation inhabits will eventually have nothing to say to that world. The sermon must come from somewhere — from genuine encounter with the text, genuine attention to the congregation, and genuine engagement with the culture in which both preacher and congregation are trying to live as followers of Jesus.

Genuine encounter is not produced by technique. It is produced by exposure. The preacher who takes a week to read a novel they have been meaning to read for three years, or who spends a day in a part of their city they have never spent time in before, or who has a long conversation with someone whose life experience is significantly different from their own — that preacher may find, weeks later, that something in the preaching has changed. Not because of the technique. Because of the exposure. The soil was amended, and the roots went deeper.

## A Concrete Scene

I know a pastor in his early fifties who had been preaching in the same congregation for fourteen years when he realized, sitting in his study one Wednesday morning with the passage for Sunday in front of him, that he had nothing to say. Not that he had run out of words — he had plenty of words. He could have assembled a technically competent sermon in two hours. What he did not have was anything he actually wanted to say, anything he genuinely believed the congregation needed to hear, anything that felt like it had come from somewhere real.

He did something he had not done in years: he closed his notes, got in his car, and drove to the neighborhood around the church. He walked for two hours. He talked to two business owners and a man sitting on the steps of a vacant building who turned out to have more theological insight than most of the seminarians the pastor had trained with. He came back and preached on Sunday with something he had not had on Wednesday: a reason.

Not a new topic. Not a new technique. A reason. He had been reminded of the actual people the sermon was for.

## Practical Experiments Worth Trying

If the preaching has gone stale, consider changing something about the process — not the commitment to biblical faithfulness, but the mechanics that surround it. Preach through a book of the Bible you have never preached before, one that genuinely challenges you. Invite a small group of laypeople to engage the text with you before you preach it and bring their questions and observations into the sermon. Change where you do your study. Change when you do your primary exegesis. Take a Sunday to preach something personal and difficult — not for the sake of drama, but because there is something you have been carrying that the congregation needs to hear.

Sometimes the staleness is not about the preaching at all — it is about the pastor's overall condition, and no homiletical adjustment will address what a genuine season of renewal needs to. If the staleness is accompanied by the other markers of depletion — disconnection, numbness, loss of joy — the sermon is not the problem to solve. The pastor is the condition to address, with the gentleness and seriousness that requires.

## The Weight of the Work

The congregation is not waiting for a polished performance. They are waiting, in the words they would never use, for someone who has been with God and can tell them what it was like. That is still the work. It was always the work.

The staleness is a signal. Not a sentence. The preacher who is honest about it, who takes the signal seriously and responds to it with the investment it requires, will find — most of the time, not always — that the dryness is not permanent. That the Word still has something to say through a person who has stopped pretending they already know what it is.

Go find out. Preach what you find.
