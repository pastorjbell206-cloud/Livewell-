The missions presentation is a reliable feature of the American evangelical calendar. Somebody stands up with a PowerPoint that includes a map of the unreached world, a photograph of children in a village, a statistic about the number of people groups without a gospel witness, and an appeal to give. The congregation politely applauds and mostly does not give substantially more than they gave before.

The presentation was not wrong. It failed for a different reason.

It gave people information about a need rather than a story that could carry them into it. Information produces awareness. Story produces solidarity. And it is solidarity, not awareness, that moves people to give their money, their time, and eventually their children to a mission that is geographically distant and culturally foreign.

This distinction matters because of how the human imagination actually works — and how biblical communication about mission actually worked. Paul understood this. When he wrote to the Romans about his mission to the Gentiles, he did not lead with statistics about the unreached population. He led with his own body — beaten, shipwrecked, hungry, in danger from rivers and from people and from false brothers (2 Corinthians 11:24-27). He led with the faces of specific people: Lydia, the dealer in purple cloth from Thyatira (Acts 16:14); the jailer at Philippi who washed Paul's wounds at midnight (Acts 16:33); the synagogue ruler Crispus in Corinth who believed with his whole household (Acts 18:8). The mission became concrete because the people were concrete. You cannot be indifferent to Lydia. You can be indifferent to a demographic category.

The pastor who wants to actually move his congregation on global missions must become a storyteller first and a strategist second.

This means knowing your missionaries as people rather than as organizational representatives. The missions presentation that says, "We support seventeen missionaries in fourteen countries" communicates institutional breadth. It moves almost no one. The presentation that says, "Let me tell you about David and Maria, who have spent the last six years in a city in Southeast Asia where the gospel has not gone before, who lost a child there, who built the first community of believers that city has ever had, who received an email last week that said —" that presentation is working with entirely different material. The congregation can accompany David and Maria. They cannot accompany a missionary count.

This means visiting. Not the short-term mission trip circuit, which has its own complicated relationship to the actual mission. But the pastor who has stood in the city where his congregation's missionary works, who has met the local believers, who has seen the conditions of ministry and come home changed by what he saw, can preach missions from inside the experience rather than from a map. That difference is heard. The congregation can tell the difference between a pastor who went and a pastor who read about it. The authority of firsthand experience is not about expertise. It is about the specific weight that direct encounter gives to testimony.

This means making the global local. The congregation member who has no visceral connection to the unreached world across the ocean may have a deep connection to the refugee family from that same region who lives three blocks from the church. The student in the congregation who grew up in the Philippines, the nurse whose grandmother emigrated from Guatemala, the engineer whose college roommate was from Nigeria — these connections exist in almost every congregation, and they are almost never used. When the pastor makes the connection explicit — when he shows that the mission in Southeast Asia and the mission to the refugee family in your city are the same mission, drawing on the same theology, demanding the same posture of openness — the abstraction collapses. The congregation discovers that the unreached world is closer than they thought.

There is a legitimate objection here that deserves serious engagement. Some congregational leaders argue that the move from global statistics to personal stories is a form of emotionalism — that it substitutes sentimental identification for genuine theological conviction about God's character and the Great Commission. The person who gives because a story moved them will stop giving when the story stops moving them. The person who gives because they believe that every nation and tribe and tongue will stand before the throne of God (Revelation 7:9) and that God has chosen the church as the instrument of that gathering will give through decades of invisible, discouraging, unsexy mission work.

This objection is real. Emotion without theology produces giving that does not last. The answer is not to choose between story and theology, but to embed the story within the theology — to tell Lydia's story and explain why Lydia's story is only possible because God had already told Abraham that all the nations of the earth would be blessed through his offspring (Genesis 12:3). The story needs the arc. The arc needs the story. The pastor who can hold both — who can move from the specific face to the cosmic purpose and back again — is communicating missions in the way that the New Testament itself communicates it.

This means being honest about cost. The American church has largely made missions into a comfortable transaction — you give money, someone else goes, and you remain as you are. That transaction is not wrong. But it is insufficient, and the congregation that has only ever been asked for that transaction will not be moved deeply enough to sustain the mission through the decades when the results are invisible and the news is discouraging. Genuine engagement with global mission eventually costs the congregation something more than money. It costs them people. It costs them their best young leaders who go and do not come back. It costs them the comfortable assumption that the church's primary purpose is to serve its current members.

Being honest about that cost from the beginning — naming it as a cost and then making the theological case for why it is worth bearing — produces a different quality of commitment than guilt or enthusiasm alone can generate. The congregation that has been told the truth about what global mission requires, and has decided to bear that cost anyway, is a congregation with a genuine theology of the Great Commission. The congregation that has been managed into giving through techniques and presentations will manage its way out when the techniques stop working.

The congregation that gives deeply to global mission is a congregation that understands its own story as part of that mission — that sees its gathering as a moment in the long arc of God's redemptive purpose that began with Abraham and will end with every nation represented before the throne. That theological location changes everything. It means the congregation's mission giving is not charity toward distant strangers but participation in their own calling. It means the missionary in Southeast Asia is not an outsider they are supporting but a member of the same body doing what the congregation cannot be present to do.

Telling them that story well — the full arc, from creation to new creation, with specific faces along the way — is the pastor's job.

Most missions presentations do not do that. They give information. They show the map. They make the appeal. They receive polite applause.

The congregation that has been told its own story, located in the story God is telling, and shown the specific faces through whom that story is advancing — that congregation does not politely applaud. It shows up.

Telling them their story well is the pastor's job.

There is a practical dimension here that deserves naming. Most churches that struggle to move their congregations on global missions are churches whose missions committee handles missions as a budget line rather than as a pastoral priority. The committee approves support levels, reviews missionary newsletters, and reports to the congregation once a year during missions month. The senior pastor is not the champion of the mission — he is an endorser of the committee's work. The result is that the congregation receives the mission as an institutional program rather than as the pastor's personal conviction, and institutional programs do not produce the kind of solidarity that sends people and sustains giving across decades.

When the pastor of a congregation carries the global mission the way he carries everything else he believes deeply — in his preaching, in his pastoral conversations, in the way he talks about his own life and calling — the mission stops being a budget category and starts being a conviction. The congregation gives from conviction. It gives from relationship. It gives because the pastor they trust has told them, from a place of genuine belief, that this matters as much as anything they do with their money.

Revelation 7:9-10 is the vision that ought to haunt every pastor who cares about the Great Commission: "After this I looked, and there before me was a great multitude that no one could count, from every nation, tribe, people and language, standing before the throne and before the Lamb." Every nation. Every tribe. Every language. That gathering is already accomplished in the mind of God. The church's mission is to participate in the gathering — to be the instrument through which the scattered peoples of the earth are brought into the family of God.

That is not a missions committee's program. It is the reason the church exists. When the pastor tells that story — plainly, concretely, from inside his own conviction — the congregation does not politely applaud.

It understands, perhaps for the first time, why it is there.
