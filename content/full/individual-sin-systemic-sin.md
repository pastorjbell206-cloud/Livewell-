The evangelical tradition I was formed in had a clear account of sin. Sin was personal. It was the individual will turned against God and neighbor — the specific choices a specific person made in specific moments. Confession was individual, repentance was individual, transformation was individual. The gospel addressed the human heart, and the human heart, once changed, would change the world.

There is truth in this. The broken will is real. Accountability before God is real. Personal repentance is not a category error. I will not surrender it.

But for the better part of a decade in ministry, I preached sin as if it were always and only a personal transaction. A man lusts. A woman deceives. A person steals. The solution: conviction, repentance, grace, change. I was not wrong about any of that. I was incomplete. I was reading Amos as if Amos were simply a better version of a recovery group facilitator, addressing individual hearts when he was actually indicting structures — the grain merchants who shorted their measures, the judges who took bribes, the wealthy who built houses of hewn stone on the backs of the poor. Individual people were doing it. The system was designed to let them.

---

The church's current allergy to the language of systemic sin is not a theological conviction. It is a cultural reflex.

In the decades since the civil rights movement was reframed as a political rather than a prophetic event, many white evangelicals came to treat any reference to systems or structures as a code word for a particular political program. The logic went: if you talk about structures, you must be talking about government programs; if you talk about government programs, you are a progressive; if you are a progressive, you are the enemy. That conflation is understandable as a historical accident. It is inexcusable as a permanent hermeneutic.

Scripture knows no such restriction.

Amos 5 does not only condemn the man who takes a bribe. It condemns the gate — the system of adjudication where bribery operated as standard procedure: "You hate the one who reproves in the gate, and you abhor the one who speaks the truth" (Amos 5:10). The gate was an institution. It was the place where disputes were settled, contracts enforced, and the vulnerable either protected or abandoned. When bribery captured the gate, the individual corrupt judge was sinning, but the institution itself had become a mechanism for injustice.

Isaiah 10 does not merely scold corrupt judges individually; it announces woe upon those who "make iniquitous decrees" — legislation, law, policy — "to turn aside the needy from justice and to rob the poor of my people of their right" (Isaiah 10:1–2). Iniquitous decrees. The sin here is structural: laws written to produce unjust outcomes. The people writing them were sinning individually, yes. But so was the legal architecture they built.

Ezekiel 22 catalogs the sins of Jerusalem not merely as individual transgressions but as systemic arrangements: the princes who take bribes and shed blood, the priests who violate the law and profane the holy things, the prophets who whitewash the arrangements with false visions, the people of the land who practice extortion and commit robbery — a whole ecosystem of interlocking corruptions, each one feeding and enabling the others. You cannot address one without confronting the system that connects them.

---

Paul understood this. His language about "principalities and powers" in Ephesians 6 has been domesticated into a vocabulary about personal spiritual warfare, but the social dimension of those powers is embedded in the original context. [cite — confirm] These powers are not merely individual demons attached to individual lives. They are the spiritual reality behind institutional arrangements, the invisible architecture of systems that dehumanize and destroy. To fight them requires more than changed hearts, though changed hearts are essential. It requires confronting the structures that make certain sins profitable, certain injustices invisible, certain people disposable.

The Hebrew prophets understood something we have lost. They knew that sin calcifies into systems — that injustice writes itself into law, that what begins as individual wickedness eventually becomes the water everyone swims in, invisible, assumed, normal. The prophet Amos did not merely address the wealthy individuals in Samaria about their private spiritual conditions. He addressed the arrangement they had built and benefited from. He named what the arrangement cost the people at the bottom of it. He declared that God could not receive Israel's worship while Israel's economic structures were grinding the poor into the dust.

That is not a private spiritual matter dressed up in social language. That is the prophetic tradition in its fullest expression: the refusal to separate what happens in the temple from what happens in the marketplace, what God requires in worship from what God requires in the law courts, the condition of the soul from the condition of the neighbor.

---

The standard objection is worth taking seriously. Systems do not sin, people do. No institution repents; only persons can turn. This is true.

And it does not defeat the argument.

Individual repentance that produces no structural change is incomplete repentance. The slaveholder who has a genuine conversion experience but keeps his enslaved people in chains has not finished repenting. The banker who privately regrets predatory lending practices but continues them has not repented of them. The developer who feels genuine discomfort about displacing a poor neighborhood but signs the contracts anyway has not yet answered the question that repentance requires.

The question "What must I do?" is always also the question "What must I stop participating in?" — and sometimes, "What must I work to dismantle?"

Zacchaeus understood this. His encounter with Jesus produced not only gratitude and religious transformation but a specific structural response: "Half of my possessions, Lord, I will give to the poor; and if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I will pay back four times as much" (Luke 19:8). The fourfold restitution is not charity. It is structural repair. It is the recognition that genuine turning means addressing the material consequences of the wrongs committed, not merely the interior condition of the one who committed them. Jesus called it salvation. Not the beginning of a spiritual feeling. Salvation.

---

I have done what I am warning against. I have preached against the sin of materialism to congregants who worked in industries structured to produce and reward exactly that materialism, and I have called that prophetic. It was not. It was the comfortable half of the biblical witness, stripped of its most expensive demands.

There is a reason the comfortable half is the one that gets preached. Addressing individual sin rarely costs the preacher anything — the sin in question is usually someone else's, and even when it names the congregation's failures, it names them in the category of private spiritual life, which carries a certain acceptable discomfort. Addressing systemic sin costs the preacher the approval of the people who benefit from the system. In a congregation that includes employers who pay poverty wages, investors in predatory industries, homeowners who benefit from exclusionary zoning — naming the structures is not a spiritual exercise. It is an act of prophetic risk.

Jeremiah knew this. He was thrown into a cistern for it (Jeremiah 38:6). Amos was expelled from Bethel for it (Amos 7:10–17). John the Baptist lost his head for naming what the powerful did not want named. The prophetic tradition is not comfortable. It was never designed to be.

---

The practical question for the contemporary pastor is not whether systemic sin is theologically real. It clearly is, by any honest reading of the canon. The practical question is whether we are willing to name it.

Naming it means saying, from the pulpit, that the structure of a particular industry is itself the sin, not only the individual actors within it. It means saying that the political arrangements that produce mass incarceration, food deserts, and generational poverty are not politically neutral facts to be managed but moral arrangements to be confronted. It means saying that the congregation's economic choices have structural consequences, and that those consequences carry theological weight.

It also means, with equal force, saying that personal transformation is not optional, that structural critique without changed hearts is hollow, that the prophetic tradition's condemnation of unjust systems was never a substitute for the call to individual repentance and moral formation. Both. Always both.

The prophets were not sociologists who had abandoned theology. They were theologians who refused to let the theological categories be captured by the comfortable and the powerful. They insisted that what God requires of individuals — justice, mercy, faithfulness — is also what God requires of the arrangements those individuals build and inhabit.

---

Individual sin is real. The broken will is real. Personal accountability is real, and I will not surrender it.

But so is the grain merchant's scale. So is the unjust gate. So is every law written to protect the powerful at the expense of the powerless. So is every institutional arrangement that makes certain sins invisible, certain people disposable, certain injustices normal enough to be unremarkable.

The church that addresses one without the other has chosen the easier half of the prophetic tradition and called it faithfulness. That is not faithfulness. That is the management of an incomplete gospel for the comfort of the people who benefit from the incompleteness.

Amos said it better. God wants justice to roll down like waters and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream. Not a trickle of individual conversion that leaves the riverbed dry. A torrent — continuous, structural, reshaping the terrain it moves through, cutting new channels where none existed before.

That is what the full gospel demands. Not less than personal transformation. Never only personal transformation.

Both. The stream does not choose between the banks.
