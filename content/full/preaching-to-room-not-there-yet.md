Sunday morning holds more than one kind of person. There is the elder who has sat in the third row for twenty years, whose Bible is underlined in four colors, who will correct the Greek if you get it wrong. And three rows back sits someone who came because a coworker kept asking, who does not own a Bible, who is frankly skeptical that any of this is true but is trying to stay open. The question is whether you can preach a sermon that speaks honestly to both of them without lying to either.

Most preachers solve this problem by choosing one audience. Either the sermon is deeply exegetical and the skeptic gets lost by the second point, or the sermon is so accessible that the third-row elder is eating candy for the third week running. Neither of these is preaching. One is graduate seminar. The other is inspirational speaking with cross-shaped props.

The problem is not primarily homiletical. It is missional. The decision about who you are preaching to encodes an assumption about what the church is for. A congregation that only hears sermons designed for the already-convinced will gradually lose the capacity to even notice when the unconvinced are present — and then will gradually stop being the kind of community where the unconvinced want to come. The loop closes on itself. The room that is not there yet stops attending, because the room has made clear, week after week, that its conversations are for insiders only.

The biblical model is more demanding than either instinct. When Paul stood on the Areopagus in Athens (Acts 17:22–31), he was speaking to a room that had no Scripture background, no category for resurrection, no prior commitment to the God of Israel. He did not begin with Genesis. He began with the altar to an unknown god that they had already built — their own intuition that there was something they had not yet accounted for. He moved from what they already knew toward what they had not yet received. He quoted their own poets: Aratus, and possibly Epimenides. He built the argument from inside their frame before he pressed beyond it.

This is not accommodation. It is not the softening of the gospel into something more palatable. Paul did not soften the resurrection — he named it plainly, and some of them mocked him for it (Acts 17:32). What he did was earn the right to be heard by demonstrating that he had actually listened to where they were before he began telling them where to go. He took their questions seriously enough to engage them on their own ground. He did not stand at the Areopagus and announce that the Athenians needed to abandon their framework first and then listen. He started inside their framework and argued toward the resurrection from within it.

This is the discipline that most preachers have not done the work to develop. It requires knowing what the unconvinced person in your congregation actually thinks — not a caricature of secular skepticism, but the real texture of the doubt. What is the precise form of the objection? Is it the problem of evil — the sense that a God who permits the suffering they have witnessed cannot be described as good? Is it the credibility of religious institutions after decades of scandal? Is it the specific claim about resurrection, which strikes many educated adults as simply indefensible given what they understand about biology and history? Is it the relationship between Christianity and political power, such that hearing the name of Jesus activates associations with the political culture Christianity has been wedded to in their lifetime?

Each of these is a different objection requiring a different engagement. The preacher who has not done the work of understanding which specific form the doubt takes in the people sitting three rows back will deliver a sermon that addresses some other doubt — a strawman that the real skeptic does not hold, which confirms the suspicion that the church has not listened.

Preaching toward the unconvinced does not require abandoning the theological substance that feeds the already-committed. It requires placing that substance inside a frame of genuine questions rather than assumed answers. The skeptic is not opposed to depth. The skeptic is often hungry for it — specifically, for evidence that Christianity can sustain serious intellectual engagement rather than demanding that you check your questions at the door. What drives the unconvinced away is not rigor. It is the sense that the room has already decided, that doubt is unwelcome, that the only acceptable posture is one they cannot honestly hold.

The preacher who says, out loud, "I understand why this is difficult to believe, and here is how I think about that" has done something the unconvinced person almost never expects from the pulpit. He has acknowledged that they are there. He has invited them into the argument rather than past it. He has demonstrated that the faith he holds is one that has engaged the objection honestly rather than dismissing it.

Tim Keller's practice [cite — confirm] was to insist that a sermon should be summarizable in a form that a skeptic would recognize as a fair representation of what they actually think — before the argument is mounted. Not a caricature. Not the weakest version of the objection. The real thing. That discipline, applied rigorously, changes everything about how you prepare. It forces you to spend time inside the frame of the person who does not yet believe, to take seriously the weight of their reasoning, to acknowledge what is genuinely difficult about the claims you are making. That acknowledgment is what makes the rest of the sermon trustworthy.

At the same time, preaching toward the skeptic must not become performance of uncertainty. The congregation needs a pastor who has genuine convictions and can say so plainly. The preacher who resolves every tension in advance, who cannot name a doubt or sit with a hard question because it might undermine his authority, reads to the unconvinced not as confident but as insulated. That kind of certainty does not persuade the skeptic. It confirms the suspicion that the faith is not actually in contact with the hardest questions.

There is a form of intellectual dishonesty in the pulpit that the unconvinced detect before the already-convinced do: the move that preemptively dismisses the objection without engaging it, the rhetorical pivot that avoids the hard question by reframing toward something easier, the testimony that presents a faith with no struggle as evidence for the faith's truth. The skeptic in the third row has usually been thinking about these questions longer and harder than the sermon is willing to acknowledge. He knows when the argument skips a step.

The room that is not there yet will not be reached by a different style or a better production value. It will be reached by a preacher who has done the intellectual and spiritual work of taking their questions seriously — who has sat with the hardest objections to Christian faith, not as rhetorical problems to be deflected but as genuine challenges that deserve genuine engagement. C.S. Lewis did not reach the skeptics of his generation through charm alone. He reached them because he had been a skeptic himself, because he knew the exact shape of the objection from the inside, and because his arguments engaged the real difficulty rather than a managed version of it.

The pastoral dimension of this cannot be separated from the intellectual. The room that is not there yet is not only intellectually unconvinced. It is also relationally alienated. Many of the people who arrive at a church carrying skepticism arrived not because their intellectual questions were perfectly calibrated but because something happened — a birth, a death, a marriage coming apart, a friend whose faith seemed to be sustaining them in ways that secular alternatives had not — that pressed them toward the question of whether there is more than they had accounted for. They are not only looking for an argument that answers their objections. They are looking for a community that can hold the weight of their life.

The preacher who can address both — who can engage the intellectual dimension honestly and who simultaneously demonstrates, through the texture of the sermon, that this faith has been tested by real suffering and has not collapsed — is preaching to the room that is not yet there. He is making it a room worth sitting in.

You cannot preach to the room that is not there yet if you have never spent time thinking inside their categories. That is not a method. It is a posture. And it begins before the sermon starts — in the reading, in the conversations, in the willingness to sit with the hardest version of the question and not look away until you have found something that can actually bear the weight.

That discipline is the difference between a congregation that keeps only its own kind and a congregation that keeps becoming larger than the people who already believe.

The room is not there yet. Preach to it anyway.
