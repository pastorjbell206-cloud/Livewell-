The first time a church sends someone from its own congregation into cross-cultural mission — not just giving money to a distant agency, but releasing one of its own people from the community they have built together, commissioning them, blessing them, and committing to support them over the long haul — something changes in the church permanently. The mission stops being abstract. It becomes personal. The congregation has skin in the game now, in the most literal sense, and the skin belongs to someone they love.

The churches that are most missionally vital tend to be sending churches — churches that have made the deliberate commitment to identify, develop, and release people from within their own congregation into the broader work of the gospel. But most churches have never done this, and the path from never having sent anyone to having a genuine sending culture is not obvious. This article is an attempt to map it.

## The Sending Culture Does Not Start With Sending

The first step toward becoming a sending church is not identifying someone to send. It is cultivating a congregation whose imagination is shaped by the global mission — a community that reads Scripture through the lens of the God who sends, that prays regularly and specifically for the unreached, and that understands the local church not as a destination but as a deployment center.

This formation happens in worship, in preaching, in how the church uses its giving, and in the stories it tells about itself. A church that celebrates the person who leaves for the mission field with the same energy it would celebrate the person who joins the staff — that treats sending out as a sign of health rather than a loss — is building the cultural infrastructure that makes genuine sending possible.

The biblical case for this is not obscure. From Genesis 12, where God tells Abram "I will bless you so that you will be a blessing," the direction of God's purposes has always been outward. The covenant community exists not for itself but for the world. Jesus' commission in Matthew 28 is not a program the church adds to its existing agenda — it is the shape of the church's existence. And the church in Antioch, described in Acts 13, models what the Spirit-led sending community looks like: the congregation is worshipping and fasting when the Spirit says, "Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them" (Acts 13:2). They do not merely release these two. They fast, they pray, they lay hands on them, and they send them off.

The Antioch church did not get there overnight. Acts 11 describes its formation through the ministry of Barnabas and Saul, through the prophetic ministry of people who had come from Jerusalem and Cyprus and Cyrene, through the multiethnic community that was built before the sending ever happened. Sending culture was the fruit of prior formation. It still is.

## The Theological Problem Underneath

Most churches that have never sent anyone are not opposed to mission. They give to mission agencies. They have a missions line in the budget. They host the occasional missionary who comes home on furlough and shows slides. The problem is not indifference. It is a theological misunderstanding about what the local church is.

Lesslie Newbigin spent his ministry — first as a missionary to India, then as a theologian addressing the Western church — arguing that the church is not primarily a gathered community that occasionally sends out individuals. The church is, by its nature, a missionary community [cite — confirm]. The gathering is in service of the sending. The worship equips the witness. The community is formed so that it can be dispersed. When the local church treats mission as a department — something the missions committee handles, funded by a percentage of the budget — it has accepted a structural lie about what the church is and what it is for.

Getting to a genuine sending culture requires correcting this theological error at the level of ecclesiology, not just programming. It requires the pastor to preach, with clarity and regularity, that the church exists for the world and not for its own comfort — that the highest expression of a congregation's health is not a beautiful campus or a flourishing program but the willingness to release its best people into the service of the unreached.

I have preached this and felt the discomfort it produces. The family whose children grew up in this church does not want to watch them go to Southeast Asia. The elders who invested years in developing a gifted young leader do not want to send him to a place where he cannot be replaced. These are real losses, and the pastor who minimizes them is not being more missional — they are being less honest. The call to send costs something. Churches that have never sent anyone have often, at some level, been protecting themselves from that cost.

## The Counter-Position, Answered

The counterargument from a cautious elder board sounds like this: our church has real needs right here. Our neighborhood is underserved. Our congregation has people in genuine crisis. The idea that we should invest in sending someone overseas when there are unreached people at our own doorstep is a failure of stewardship and a misreading of mission priority.

This is a serious argument. It deserves a serious answer. The answer is that it presents a false binary. The New Testament does not pit Jerusalem against the ends of the earth — it puts them in sequence and holds them together simultaneously (Acts 1:8). The church in Antioch that sent Paul and Barnabas to Asia Minor and Greece was also, simultaneously, the church that collected funds for the poor in Jerusalem (Acts 11:27-30). Sending into global mission and serving the local community are not competing goods. They are expressions of the same missionary orientation.

Moreover, the experience of the churches that have sent is consistent: sending does not drain a congregation. It expands it. The church that has skin in the game — that is praying weekly for specific people in specific places, that is receiving reports and seeing God at work in ways they participated in — tends to become more generous, more praying, more engaged with mission at every level. The sending creates the culture that makes the local work more vital, not less.

## Identifying Potential Missionaries

Potential missionaries — people who may be called to cross-cultural ministry — are almost always already in the congregation, often unaware that the church would recognize and affirm that calling if they named it. The job of a sending church is to create the context in which that calling can be identified, named, tested, and developed.

This means preaching about vocation and calling in ways that include the possibility of missionary service as a genuine option for ordinary church members. It means asking questions — in pastoral conversations, in small group settings, in annual conversations about how members understand their calling — that surface the people who are already feeling the pull. And it means responding to those people with genuine investment: not just a referral to a mission agency, but the commitment of the church community to walk with them through discernment, training, and preparation.

## The Commitment of the Sending Church

Sending someone is not primarily a financial transaction. It is a relational commitment. The sending church that commissions a missionary and then relates to them primarily through a quarterly newsletter has not sent them — it has offloaded them. The sending church that maintains genuine, ongoing, personal relationship with the people it has sent — praying for them specifically, communicating regularly, receiving them well when they come home on furlough, advocating for them in the congregation as real people with real struggles — has sent them in the biblical sense.

This level of commitment is demanding. It requires someone in the church to take responsibility for the ongoing relationship, to maintain the connection between the congregation and the missionary, and to be the advocate for the missionary's wellbeing and needs within the church community. That role — the mission advocate or church missions liaison — is one of the most important lay leadership roles a church can cultivate. When it is filled by someone with genuine passion and relational capacity, the connection between church and missionary tends to thrive and deepen in ways that benefit both.

## A Concrete Scene

A family in our congregation spent three years preparing before they were sent. During that time the church walked with them through fundraising, language study, and the emotional arc of leaving — the selling of a house, the farewell to parents, the last Sunday, the commissioning service that felt both like a celebration and a grief. The congregation prayed them out the door.

Eighteen months later, at a Sunday service, we played a short video they had recorded from their apartment in the city where they served. They named the colleagues they had been praying with. They described a family in their building who had begun asking questions about Jesus. They asked the congregation to pray for a specific conversation coming the following Thursday.

The room was different after that video. Not dramatically, loudly different. Quietly different. The mission was no longer a line in the budget or a map on the wall. It was their people, in a specific apartment, praying for a specific family, going into a specific Thursday. We had sent them and they had taken us with them. That is what a sending culture does: it binds the whole congregation to the frontier, whether they cross the ocean or not.

## The First Step

The church that has never sent anyone begins not by identifying who to send but by confessing, honestly, why it has not sent anyone. The reasons are almost always the same: the cost felt too high, the need felt too abstract, the internal needs felt more urgent, or no one asked the question seriously enough to force a decision. Naming that, from the pulpit, with genuine repentance and genuine vision for what is possible — that is the first act of a sending church.

The God who said "as the Father has sent me, I am sending you" (John 20:21) was not offering an optional program. He was describing the shape of the church's existence. The congregation that receives that commission as its own — that allows it to reorganize not just its missions budget but its whole imagination of what the church is for — will find that the act of sending is not a sacrifice it makes for the mission. It is the thing that makes the church most fully itself.

The church exists to be sent. The sending culture is not an advanced feature for mature congregations. It is the baseline expression of what the church is.

That is the weight the pastor carries into the first conversation about becoming a sending church. And it is heavy enough to be worth carrying.
