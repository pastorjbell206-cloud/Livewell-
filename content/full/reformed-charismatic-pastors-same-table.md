We sat in a circle of folding chairs in a church basement in Fenton and disagreed about almost everything for three hours. A Reformed Baptist, two Pentecostals, an Assemblies of God pastor who had been in ministry for thirty years, a Presbyterian, and me. The coffee was bad. The conversation was one of the most clarifying I have had in fifteen years of ministry.

That is not what I expected. I expected politeness. I expected the careful avoidance of the live wires — cessationism, the *ordo salutis*, the question of whether what happened at Azusa Street in 1906 was the work of the Spirit or the work of human emotion or some theologically complicated combination of both. I expected us to talk around the edges and call it unity.

We did not. That is why it worked.

**How We Got There**

The Pastors Connection Network exists precisely because pastoral isolation is the default condition of most ministry. A pastor in a small or medium-sized church in a mid-sized American city — which describes the overwhelming majority of pastors in this country — operates with almost no genuine peer accountability, no one who understands the specific weight of the calling from the inside, no one to whom he is genuinely answerable in real time. He is surrounded by people he serves but not by people who serve him. The loneliness is structural, not incidental.

What we were trying to do in that basement was something deliberately different: put pastors with genuinely different theological commitments in the same room and see whether real conversation was possible, or whether the denominational identities had thickened to the point where all that was left was parallel monologue dressed as dialogue.

The risk was real. These were not hypothetical disagreements. The Reformed tradition and the charismatic tradition have been in genuine and often bitter conflict over significant questions for more than a century. The Fundamentalist-Modernist controversies of the early twentieth century, the emergence of neo-Pentecostalism in the 1960s and the charismatic renewal in mainline churches, the Third Wave controversies of the 1980s and 1990s — these were not abstract theological debates. They produced institutional splits, personal accusations, churches that refused to share platforms, and a mutual suspicion that in some circles has never fully lifted.

To put pastors shaped by those traditions in a room and ask them to be honest required either recklessness or conviction. I believed then, and believe now, that the John 17 prayer — "that they may all be one, as you, Father, are in me, and I in you, that the world may believe that you have sent me" (John 17:21) — was not a pious aspiration. It was the prayer of the Son to the Father on the night before his death. He thought our unity mattered. He thought it mattered enough to pray for it while facing the cross. That should give us pause when we treat our theological distinctives as the fixed boundary of our relational investment.

**What Each Tradition Got Right**

The Reformed pastor at the table did not believe tongues were a normative gift for the post-apostolic church. He said so, clearly, without apology, but without contempt. The Pentecostal pastor did not believe the confessional tradition had fully accounted for the Spirit's ongoing activity in the gathered community. He said so with the same directness. What followed was not resolution. It was something better than resolution. It was genuine engagement — the kind where you actually have to defend your position rather than assume the person across from you is simply less informed.

The charismatic tradition has genuinely recovered something the Reformed tradition is sometimes too tidy to receive: the wildness of the Spirit, the expectation that God is actually present and active in the gathered community, not just ratifying the exegesis from a safe doctrinal distance. When I have sat in Pentecostal prayer meetings where the grief and desperation were real, where people were not performing spiritual experience but genuinely crying out — I have felt a quality of expectation that the Reformed services I grew up in sometimes lacked. The expectation that God might do something unrehearsed. The willingness to wait on the Spirit rather than concluding when the agenda ran out. These are not small things.

The Reformed tradition has genuinely preserved something the charismatic tradition sometimes loses in the fog of experience: the norming authority of Scripture over private revelation, the accountability of doctrine to the community across time, the understanding that enthusiasm without theology has historically gone off the rails very quickly and disastrously. The Great Awakening produced both Jonathan Edwards and the excesses Edwards famously criticized in his *Treatise Concerning Religious Affections* — a work that remains precisely useful because it takes spiritual experience seriously enough to test it rather than simply celebrate it. [cite — confirm] The Reformed insistence that the interior experience must be evaluated against the external word is not a failure of faith. It is the prevention of a predictable pathology.

I came to faith from atheism. My first serious theological encounters were with Reformed writers — men who took ideas with enough seriousness to argue them carefully, who assumed that the life of the mind was not an obstacle to faith but an expression of it. That formation saved me from a Christianity I could not have sustained. But some of the most formative moments in my pastoral development have come in rooms where prayer was not performative but desperate, where people actually expected God to show up in a way that exceeded the sermon outline. Both of those things are real. Both of them are insufficient alone.

**What the Division Is Actually About**

What became clear at that table is that the division between these traditions is not always about the text. It is often about formation — about what kind of Christian experience you were converted into, what community shaped your initial assumptions about how God works. A man raised in a Pentecostal church has a different operating assumption about corporate worship than a man raised in a confessional congregation. Those assumptions are not always wrong. They are always partial.

The problem is not that we have different traditions. The problem is that we have allowed those traditions to become identities so thick that genuine engagement with the other becomes threatening. The Reformed pastor who cannot imagine that a Pentecostal might have something to teach him about prayer and expectation has confused his tradition with the whole of Christian truth. The charismatic pastor who cannot imagine that the confessional tradition might have something to teach him about doctrinal discipline and the testing of spirits has made the same error in the other direction. In both cases, the tradition has ceased to be a gift and has become a defensive perimeter.

The theological stakes here are not trivial. Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 12 is that the body of Christ is constituted by diversity — that the eye cannot say to the hand, "I have no need of you," without damaging the whole body (1 Corinthians 12:21). He was writing about gifts within a congregation, but the ecclesiological logic applies across traditions. When the Reformed wing of the church acts as though the charismatic wing has nothing of the Spirit, and when the charismatic wing acts as though the Reformed wing's commitment to the Word is a failure of expectation, both are amputating parts of the body of Christ and calling it faithfulness.

**What Actually Happened**

Nobody at that table converted anyone. The Reformed Baptist left still a cessationist. The Assemblies of God pastor left still believing the gifts of the Spirit are available today and that the Western church has systematically quenched them. The disagreements were real, and they remained real.

But something shifted. We left knowing that the pastor across the theological divide was not an obstacle to the mission. He was part of it.

That shift is harder to achieve than it sounds. The default pastoral posture — in evangelical culture, in the competitive attention economy of contemporary church life — is to treat other pastors as competitors, or at best as neutral figures who occupy adjacent territory. The church across town doing things differently is either irrelevant or vaguely threatening. The pastor from a different tradition is someone to be civil toward in public and to theologize against in private. The result is a pastoral culture of profound loneliness dressed up as theological fidelity.

What the PCN exists to disrupt is that pattern. Not by asking pastors to abandon their convictions — the table we sat around was not a lowest-common-denominator table, and nobody was being asked to pretend the differences do not matter. The differences matter. They shape real decisions about practice, about what the gathered community looks like, about what people are trained to expect from God and from each other. Pretending those differences are small would not be unity. It would be dishonesty that makes genuine unity impossible.

What the table does require is the willingness to sit with someone whose tradition is not yours and to receive what is genuinely there before you respond to what is wrong. The charismatic's experience of the Spirit's presence is not automatically explained by emotion or manipulation. The Reformed pastor's insistence on the sufficiency of Scripture is not automatically explained by fear or a failure of expectation. Before you engage the error, honor the substance. There is almost always substance.

**The Harder Question**

The harder question, the one we spent the final hour on, is whether genuine partnership is possible without doctrinal agreement. Can a Pentecostal and a Reformed pastor actually work together on a shared mission in the same city, or will the theological friction eventually produce either division or a flattened ecumenism that neither tradition can honestly endorse?

The answer we arrived at — tentatively, carefully, without resolving it — is that the mission can create partnership that the theology cannot. We cannot share pulpits on the questions we disagree about. We can share a soup kitchen. We can pray together for the city. We can call each other when one of us is in crisis, which happens to all of us, regardless of tradition. We can refuse to speak about each other as though the other's faith is counterfeit, which the volume of evangelical social media suggests is no small commitment.

Three hours and bad coffee. It was worth every minute. The unity Christ prayed for is not achieved by theological consensus. It is built, slowly and imperfectly, by people who decide that the prayer of the Son on the night before his death deserves to be taken seriously — even when it costs them something.

It costs something. That is how you know it is the real thing.
