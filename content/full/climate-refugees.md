The number is contested but the direction is not. Conservative projections estimate that by mid-century, climate displacement — people forced from their homes by rising seas, desertification, catastrophic flooding, and the collapse of agricultural systems — will reach into the hundreds of millions. [cite — confirm] The more severe scenarios project figures that exceed any refugee crisis in recorded human history. These are not primarily projections about the distant future. They are projections about people who are children now, who will be adults when the displacement arrives, and who will be looking for somewhere to go.

The church in the Global North will be asked a question it has not prepared for. How that question is answered will say something definitive about what the church actually believes about the image of God in the bodies of people it has never met.

Begin with the geography of culpability. The nations most responsible for the accumulated carbon emissions that are driving the climatic disruptions — primarily the industrial economies of North America, Western Europe, and historically the United Kingdom — are not the nations that will absorb the primary consequences. The nations most exposed to sea-level rise, desertification, and agricultural collapse are predominantly in South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific Islands. Bangladesh, home to approximately 170 million people, stands to lose a significant percentage of its land area to flooding within decades. [cite — confirm] The Pacific Island nations — Kiribati, Tuvalu, the Marshall Islands — are not facing the risk of inconvenience. They are facing the termination of national existence. Their people, bearing the image of God, are being displaced by a crisis they did not cause, originating in economic patterns primarily developed and maintained by people in countries that will largely be insulated from the worst effects.

This is not a natural disaster in the morally simple sense. Natural disasters — earthquakes, volcanic eruptions — fall outside human causation. Climate displacement is a different category. It is the accumulation of consequences from choices made by identifiable actors over identifiable periods of time, choices that benefited some populations enormously while imposing costs primarily on others. The theological term for the transfer of costs from those who benefit to those who do not is injustice. It is the same category the prophets used when they condemned the wealthy in Amos's Israel for "selling the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals" (Amos 2:6). The mechanism is different. The structure is the same.

The Mosaic law is explicit about the stranger. "You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Leviticus 19:34). The command is grounded in historical experience — Israel knew displacement, knew the vulnerability of living in someone else's land, and was therefore required to carry that knowledge into its treatment of those who arrived in their territory. The stranger was not a threat to be managed. He was a neighbor to be welcomed, a person with legal standing in the covenant community, someone whose vulnerability created a corresponding obligation in those with the stability and resources to help.

Deuteronomy returns to this command repeatedly. "He executes justice for the fatherless and the widow, and loves the sojourner, giving him food and clothing. Love the sojourner, therefore, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 10:18–19). The love of the sojourner is not a secondary ethic derived from a more primary principle. It is placed alongside the justice of God for the fatherless and the widow as a core expression of covenantal faithfulness.

In the New Testament, Jesus's parable of the sheep and the goats (Matthew 25:31–46) names the welcome of the stranger as one of the criteria of final judgment. "I was a stranger and you welcomed me" (Matthew 25:35). The Greek word for stranger — *xenos* — is the root of xenophobia, the fear of the stranger. Jesus is not addressing a secondary pastoral concern here. He is placing the welcome of the stranger at the center of the eschatological reckoning.

The climate refugee is the sojourner of the twenty-first century. He arrives not because of war, though climate displacement frequently produces conflict as it goes. He arrives because the conditions that made his land habitable have been altered by forces outside his control. He brings children. He carries documents that may or may not be recognized. He speaks a language that is not the language of the border he crosses. He is, in many cases, a Christian — the church in Bangladesh, in the Philippines, in sub-Saharan Africa is alive, vibrant, and significantly larger per capita than many of the churches in the wealthy nations that will be asked to receive the displaced. The climate refugee is not an abstraction. He is a brother in many cases, a neighbor in all cases, and a person carrying the image of God in all cases.

The counter-argument that the church most frequently hears from its political culture goes like this: nations have the right to control their borders, and unlimited migration is not a sustainable response to climate displacement. There are questions of national sovereignty, cultural capacity, economic integration, and security that cannot simply be dissolved by theological appeal. This is not an argument without content. Nations do have legitimate interests in how they manage migration. The practical questions of absorbing displaced populations are real. The argument deserves a hearing rather than dismissal.

But the argument is regularly deployed to justify not a measured response but no response — to treat the scale of the problem as an excuse for avoiding the obligation entirely. The Christian tradition does not permit this move. The obligation to the sojourner is not contingent on whether the sojourner's arrival is convenient. It is not suspended when the numbers are large. The law that required Israel to welcome the stranger did not contain an exception clause for high-volume displacement. The sheep and the goats parable does not offer a threshold above which the welcome of the stranger becomes optional.

What the church can do is not nothing. In the immediate term, the congregation that takes this seriously will support the organizations that are already working with climate-displaced communities — the advocacy organizations pushing for legal frameworks that recognize climate displacement as a category deserving protection under international law, the direct service organizations providing material support to communities currently in transition, the churches in affected regions that need material solidarity from the global communion. [cite — confirm]

In the medium term, the congregation has a formational responsibility: to produce members whose understanding of the climate crisis includes its justice dimensions, who know the geography of culpability and do not pretend it is morally neutral, who can hold the conversation about borders and belonging without reducing the image of God in the displaced person to a political inconvenience. Preaching that handles Leviticus 19 and Deuteronomy 10 and Matthew 25 as live texts — not as historical artifacts but as claims on the present — forms that congregation.

In the longer term, the question of how wealthy-nation churches engage their own political processes on behalf of climate action and climate refugees is unavoidable. It is, again, not a question about partisan alignment. It is a question about whether the people in the pews understand that their comfort is not theologically neutral, that the carbon economy that has funded their prosperity has a balance sheet that runs in the direction of Bangladesh and the Pacific, and that the people on the debit side of that balance sheet are children of God.

There will be, in the coming decades, more people displaced by climate than by any other cause in human history. The church will be present in both the countries of origin and the countries of destination. Whether the global body of Christ is a community of genuine solidarity across those borders, or a set of nationally bounded institutions that protect their own and manage the arrival of everyone else, will be one of the defining testimonies of twenty-first century Christianity.

The early church was itself a community of displaced people. The letter of 1 Peter opens by addressing the recipients as "elect exiles of the Dispersion" (1 Peter 1:1) — people scattered across provinces far from home, holding their identity and their hope in a kingdom that had not yet fully arrived. The posture of the church toward the displaced is therefore not charity from the settled toward the uprooted. It is recognition: we are, in the deepest sense, the same kind of people. The difference is degree, not kind. The Christian who has spent thirty years in the same house on the same street and the climate refugee who has lost both hold the same ultimate address — a city "whose designer and builder is God" (Hebrews 11:10). That shared eschatological citizenship is the deepest basis for the welcome the stranger requires.

The stranger at the gate is coming. He is already here in some communities and arriving in others. The question that the text has always asked has not changed.

Do you know his name? Do you have room?
