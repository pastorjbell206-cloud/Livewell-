The church spent fifty years arguing for a political outcome. The outcome arrived. And it became clear, almost immediately, that the church had not spent those fifty years preparing for it.

The Supreme Court's 2022 decision in Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization overturned Roe v. Wade and returned the question of abortion law to individual states. The ruling was the destination the pro-life movement had pursued for half a century. And within months of the decision, it was evident that the movement — and the church that was its most consistent institutional advocate — had not built the infrastructure that the victory required.

Some states moved toward protection of the unborn with legislation that restricted or prohibited abortion. Others moved toward expansion of access. The result was not the end of abortion in America. It was the redistribution of it, with the cost falling most heavily on women in restrictive states who had the fewest resources to work around those restrictions. The women with money, cars, flexible jobs, and supportive families found ways to access abortion in neighboring states. The women without those things found the restrictions more binding.

If the church's goal was the flourishing of unborn children and the women who carry them, that distribution of outcomes required serious attention. The victory had arrived. The work was just beginning.

## What Amos Said About Victory

The prophet Amos did not spend his ministry calling Israel to win political victories. He called them to account for what they did with those victories — whether the poor were cared for, whether justice ran "like a river" (Amos 5:24), whether the faith they practiced at the sanctuary on the Sabbath was visible in the marketplace on Monday.

Israel had victories. It had a functioning temple system, regular feasts, and a robust sacrificial culture. Amos called all of it hollow: "I hate, I despise your religious festivals; your assemblies are a stench to me" (Amos 5:21). The indictment was not that Israel had the wrong practices. It was that the practices were performing piety while the society organized itself around the exploitation of the poor.

The post-Roe church is at an analogous moment. The political victory has been achieved. The question Amos would ask is what the church is doing with it. Whether the women in restrictive states who now carry pregnancies to term without adequate support are being cared for by the communities that celebrated the restrictions. Whether the foster care system that will bear some of the load of unintended births has been reformed by the churches that advocated for the policies that directed more children toward it. Whether the maternal mortality rate — which, in the United States, is [cite — confirm] the highest among comparable wealthy nations — is now a priority for the organizations that were most passionate about keeping women pregnant.

## The Infrastructure That Was Not Built

In the fifty years between Roe v. Wade and Dobbs, the pro-life movement built extensive political infrastructure: advocacy organizations, litigation strategies, political coalitions, legislative networks. What it did not build — at anything approaching the same scale — was the material infrastructure required to support the additional births that a post-Roe environment would produce.

Crisis pregnancy centers exist in significant numbers, and many do good work. But the scope of what they offer — primarily counseling, material goods, and referrals — is not commensurate with the structural need. A woman who chooses life in a state with no Medicaid expansion, inadequate childcare subsidies, no paid parental leave policy, and a social safety net that phases out as she earns more than a subsistence income is not in a situation that a baby shower and a pamphlet can address.

The church knew this was the shape of the problem. The prophetic literature it reads on Sunday had been saying so for three thousand years. Deuteronomy 15, Isaiah 58, Ezekiel 16, Matthew 25 — the consistent message of Scripture about the community's obligation to its most vulnerable members was available and was being cited in the same churches that were celebrating the political victory. The gap between the proclamation and the practice was not a gap of ignorance. It was a gap of will.

## The Steel-Manned Defense of the Political Strategy

The honest defense of the approach the movement took is this: political change was the necessary precondition for everything else. The legal protection of unborn life had to be established before the material support for that life could be built. The fifty years were not misdirected; they were necessarily sequential. First you establish the right to be born, then you build the support for the born. The critique that the church failed to build social infrastructure assumes a simultaneity that was not politically possible.

This argument is serious and deserves engagement. Political change does require sustained organizational focus, and diffusion of that focus — toward both the legal and the material at the same time — would have risked achieving neither. The people who built the political movement made real strategic choices, and second-guessing those choices from a position of retrospective clarity is easier than it looks.

But the argument has a limit. Fifty years is long enough to begin building infrastructure even while the political work continues. The two are not mutually exclusive. A movement that spent fifty years building political capacity could have simultaneously built material capacity. That it did not is not a constraint imposed by the work; it is a choice made by the people doing the work. The question of why the material side was left so underdeveloped is a question about priorities, not logistics.

## What the Church Is Now Actually Required to Do

The post-Roe church has a choice. It can treat the political victory as the destination — wave the flag, claim the win, and return to ordinary ministry. Or it can treat the political victory as the beginning of a longer, more costly, more demanding project: actually building the conditions that make it possible for women to choose life without choosing poverty, isolation, or ruin.

The second option requires specific things.

It requires crisis pregnancy organizations to expand from counseling to comprehensive material support — housing, childcare, job training, medical care, ongoing mentorship for years after the birth. The funding for that expansion has to come from somewhere. The churches that celebrated Dobbs are the most obvious source.

It requires advocacy for the policy infrastructure that determines whether a woman can actually raise a child: healthcare access, childcare affordability, paid parental leave, housing assistance, poverty reduction. These are legislative questions. The churches that were willing to spend fifty years on abortion legislation are being asked to bring the same energy to the legislation that determines what happens to the children after they are born. That the second set of legislative questions lands on the other side of the political aisle from the first is a problem the church should be willing to be uncomfortable about.

It requires an honest reckoning with the maternal mortality crisis. Women in the United States die from pregnancy-related causes at rates that are significantly higher than in comparable countries [cite — confirm], and the rates are highest among Black women. A movement that claimed to care about the lives of pregnant women needs to care about that statistic.

It requires the church to examine its own congregational practice. For every woman in every congregation who has given birth in the last three years: was the church actually present? Not at the baby shower. After the baby shower. When the baby was three months old and the mother was not sleeping and the finances were not working and the relationship that produced the pregnancy was not intact. Was the church there?

## What This Actually Costs

The second option is harder. It costs money, volunteer time, political capital, and the comfort of a position that is clear and settled. The political victory was expensive and took fifty years. The pastoral and material work that the victory requires is longer, more diffuse, less measurable, and less likely to produce a moment when someone gets to celebrate.

Amos did not promise that faithfulness would be rewarded with the satisfaction of a clear victory. He called Israel to justice and mercy and humble walking before God — and then he delivered the news that God had already decided on judgment regardless (Amos 7-9). The prophetic call is not offered as a transaction. It is offered as a description of what faithfulness requires.

The post-Roe church knows what faithfulness requires. The question is whether it is willing to pay for it.

The failure to answer that question is itself an answer. A church that won the political argument and then returned to ordinary institutional life without building the material and pastoral infrastructure the victory requires has communicated something about what the victory was actually for. Not the flourishing of children and the women who carry them. Something cheaper than that.

The political victory is real. The work it requires is just beginning. That is the weight this moment carries — and it does not resolve simply because the argument has been won.
