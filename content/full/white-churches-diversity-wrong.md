The conversation about diversity in white evangelical churches usually goes wrong in the same direction. It begins with a genuine problem — the church is segregated, the statistics are as familiar as they are damning, and the people in the room know something should change. It ends with a program. A new initiative. A diversity committee. A guest preacher from a different tradition brought in for a Sunday in February. The programs come and go. The power structure of the church remains intact.

The reason is not usually bad faith. The reason is that the church has consistently misidentified what the problem is.

---

The classic statistic is Martin Luther King Jr.'s observation, made in the early 1960s, that eleven o'clock on Sunday morning is the most segregated hour in America. [cite — confirm] The observation was prophetic, and it was accurate, and it has remained accurate through sixty years of church diversity programs. The numbers have shifted at the margins in some congregations, but the basic structure of American Christianity — organized largely along racial lines, with Black churches and white churches occupying separate institutional worlds that occasionally acknowledge each other — has proven remarkably durable.

The question worth asking is why. If the desire for diverse congregations is as widespread as surveys suggest, if the theological case for the unity of the body of Christ is as clear as it obviously is, why does the segregated hour persist?

The answer that most white churches give is demographic: we are in a neighborhood that is predominantly white, or we have a worship style that happens to appeal to a particular demographic, or we haven't found the right outreach strategy yet. These answers treat the problem as external — as something that would change if the demographics changed or the strategy improved.

The more honest answer is that the segregation is the result of something structural, something that programs and strategies cannot reach: the church's organizing logic, its power distribution, its unexamined assumptions about what Christianity looks like, whose voices are authoritative, whose discomfort gets centered, and what belonging actually requires.

---

Diversity without power-sharing is not integration. It is decoration.

The church that adds people of color to its pews while maintaining an all-white leadership team has not become diverse. It has become a host — a system that recruits participants from outside its tradition while holding the actual decision-making, the cultural assumptions, the theological priorities, in the hands of people who represent one narrow slice of the body of Christ.

James K.A. Smith, drawing on Charles Taylor's analysis of the "social imaginary," describes how institutions carry embedded assumptions about what is normal, what is valuable, and who belongs that operate below the level of stated policy. [cite — confirm] You cannot change the social imaginary of a church by updating its mission statement or inviting a diverse set of guest speakers. The imaginary is carried in the liturgical practices, the aesthetic choices, the unstated rules about whose communication style is "professional" or "appropriate," the theological tradition that is assumed as the baseline, the history that is celebrated and the history that is not discussed.

When a predominantly white church says it wants to be diverse, it most often means it wants people of different backgrounds to come and participate in the church as the church currently is. This is not diversity. It is assimilation — the invitation to join while the joining requires leaving behind significant parts of your own tradition, your own theological inheritance, your own way of being in community with God.

The Black church tradition in America is one of the richest theological and liturgical traditions in Christian history. It was formed in the crucible of chattel slavery and Jim Crow, shaped by the daily requirement of holding together prophetic indictment and pastoral consolation in communities where survival was not guaranteed. Its preaching tradition, its theology of lament, its integration of justice and worship, its capacity to sustain hope under conditions of sustained oppression — these are gifts to the whole body of Christ. Not problems to be managed. Not exotic variations on white evangelical normalcy. Gifts.

The white church that is serious about diversity is serious about receiving those gifts, not just incorporating a few people who will adapt to the existing container.

---

The counter-position deserves a genuine answer.

Someone will say: ethnic-specific churches are not a sign of failure. People worship in their own cultural and linguistic contexts throughout history, and there is something appropriate about communities gathering in ways that reflect their own heritage. Shouldn't we celebrate the distinctiveness of different expressions of Christianity rather than forcing uniformity in mixed congregations?

This is a real argument and it contains real truth. There is a difference between the organic development of culturally specific worshipping communities and the segregation that resulted from deliberate exclusion. Black churches exist not because Black Christians chose separation as their first preference but because they were excluded from white ones — by policy in many cases, by practice in most. [cite — confirm] The historically Black church is not a product of voluntary ethnic preference. It is a product of white refusal.

That history matters. It means that the current arrangement is not a neutral expression of cultural diversity. It is the residue of injustice, and residue requires more than a program to address.

It also means that the move toward genuine integration requires something the white church has been reluctant to offer: actual redistribution of power. Not diversity as the addition of different faces to the existing structure. Diversity as the transformation of the structure itself — new voices at the leadership table, new theological traditions in the educational curriculum, new histories in the liturgical calendar, new assumptions about whose discomfort matters when the two communities in the room experience the same event differently.

---

The apostolic vision for the church was not ethnic uniformity under any flag. Paul's argument in Ephesians 2 is that the "dividing wall of hostility" between Jew and Gentile has been demolished in Christ — not that Jews should become cultural Gentiles or Gentiles should adopt Jewish practice, but that a new humanity has been created that is neither the one nor the other, but something genuinely new (Ephesians 2:14-15). That "new humanity" (kainos anthropos) is not achieved by assimilation. It is achieved by mutual transformation.

Revelation's vision of the redeemed community is specifically multiethnic: "a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages" (Revelation 7:9). These distinctions are not erased in the eschatological gathering. They are present. They contribute. The diversity is the point, not the obstacle. The church that exists as a preview of this gathering is called to look more like it, not less.

The work required to get there is harder than any program. It requires the white church to examine what it has assumed is universal Christianity and to discover how much of it is actually culturally specific. It requires willingness to receive correction from traditions that have thought about things the white evangelical tradition has avoided. It requires distributing power in ways that feel, to those who currently hold it, like loss.

It is loss. That is what repentance costs. It is also what fellowship requires.

The church that wants the multiethnic vision of Revelation without the redistribution of power that makes it possible has not yet understood what it is asking for. It is asking for the appearance of the kingdom while keeping the structure of the present age. That is not something the kingdom can be made to provide.

The concrete question — for the white pastor reading this, for the white congregation that has said the right things for several years without the structure changing — is what specifically you are willing to give up. Not: are you open to diversity? Not: do you believe every person is created in God's image? Those answers are easy. The hard question is: who is on your elder board? Whose theology shapes your preaching curriculum? Whose tradition is treated as the center and whose as the supplement? Whose discomfort is addressed when a sermon causes friction, and whose is absorbed?

These are not questions about intention. They are questions about structure. The structure tells the truth.

The path toward the eschatological vision is not a diversity initiative. It is a sustained, costly, generative process of shared power — the white church genuinely learning from the Black church, from the immigrant church, from the traditions it has historically treated as mission fields rather than teachers. The path requires being changed, not merely being expanded.

We are not there. Most of us are not even close. But the verdict has been clear since Ephesians 2, and the work required to approach it has not become optional because we have found it difficult. The wall came down at the cross. We have been rebuilding it with programs and good intentions for generations. The honest question is whether we are willing to stop.
