Begin with the table. Not the metaphor of the table — the actual table, the one where food arrives or does not arrive, where the question of who controls the conditions of that arrival is answered before the meal begins. The theology of the table in Christian tradition is rich and specific: the Eucharist, the eschatological banquet, the feeding of five thousand, the early church's common meals in Acts 2 that Luke reports with an attention to economic detail that most homiletical treatments quietly skip. That theology has mostly been read in one direction — upward, toward meaning, toward communion, toward the spiritual significance of breaking bread together. What it has rarely been pressed to address is the question underneath the meal: who decides what grows, where it grows, who can access it, and at what price?

Food sovereignty is the term that land rights and agrarian movements have used since at least the 1990s [cite — confirm] to name that question as a political one. It is the right of peoples to define their own food and agriculture systems — to determine what they grow, how they grow it, and how it is distributed — rather than having those systems imposed by the logic of global commodity markets and the interests of the corporations that dominate them. The concept emerged most prominently through Via Campesina, the international peasant movement, as a deliberate challenge to a food system that had been shaped by structural adjustment programs, free trade agreements, and agricultural policies that consistently benefited large producers at the expense of small farmers and subsistence communities in the Global South. [cite — confirm]

This history is not obscure. It is simply not taught in the contexts where most American pastors received their theological formation. And because it is not taught there, the church has largely missed that the question of food sovereignty is, at its root, a theological question about power, creation, and the image of God.

Start where the text starts. In Genesis 1, the first act of provision in the creation narrative is agricultural: "I have given you every plant yielding seed that is on the face of all the earth, and every tree with seed in its fruit. You shall have them for food" (Genesis 1:29). The earth's capacity to produce food is narrated as a gift — distributed without means test, without market mechanism, without the mediation of a corporation's supply chain. The gift is to "every living thing on the face of the earth" (Genesis 1:30). The distribution logic is abundance and universality, not scarcity and exchange.

The law codifies this logic in ways the church has largely treated as archaeological curiosity. The Mosaic legislation in Leviticus 19 commands that farmers not reap to the very edges of their fields and not strip the vineyard bare — the corners, the gleanings, the fallen fruit belong not to the farmer but to "the poor and for the sojourner" (Leviticus 19:9–10). This is not charity. It is law. It is a structural limitation on the farmer's property rights for the sake of the neighbor who has no field of their own. Ruth gleaning in Boaz's field (Ruth 2) is not a heartwarming exception to the normal operation of the agrarian economy. It is the normal operation of the agrarian economy, functioning as designed, in service of someone who would otherwise have starved.

The Jubilee provisions in Leviticus 25 go further still: every fifty years, land returns to its original family. Debts cancel. Slaves are freed. The logic is explicit — the land belongs to God, not to any human owner, and therefore the indefinite accumulation of land at the expense of the dispossessed is a form of theft from both the poor and from God himself. "The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine" (Leviticus 25:23). This is not a program. It is a cosmological claim: the earth is not a commodity. It is the Lord's.

Isaiah's indictment in Isaiah 5:8 — "Woe to those who join house to house, who add field to field, until there is no more room, and you are made to dwell alone in the midst of the land" — is addressed to the political economy of land concentration in eighth-century Judah, where the expansion of latifundia by wealthy elites was displacing small farmers from the land their families had worked for generations. The prophet is not making a point about spiritual pride. He is naming a specific economic practice as an offense against the covenant community's responsibility to the land and to each other. The woe is structural.

I have not preached that passage as a text about agricultural policy. Neither have most of us. We have spiritualized it into a warning against greed in general, which is safe enough to not offend anyone and specific enough to do nothing.

The New Testament does not retreat from this. The feeding of the five thousand in Mark 6 — the only miracle recorded in all four Gospels, which should itself be a signal that the tradition considered it important — does not end with a spiritual lesson about relying on God. It ends with twelve baskets left over after everyone has eaten (Mark 6:43). The abundance is material. The distribution is universal and egalitarian: everyone sitting on the grass (Mark 6:39–40). Jesus does not run a means test. He does not create a private market for the bread. He blesses, breaks, and distributes. The logic is Jubilee logic, incarnated in a hillside meal.

The early Jerusalem community in Acts 2:44–45 and 4:34–35 practiced a distribution so radical that Luke pauses twice to describe it: they sold possessions and distributed to any as had need, and "there was not a needy person among them." This is not a blueprint for mandatory communism. It is a description of what happens when people who take the Jubilee logic seriously inhabit a community together. The table creates the economy, not the other way around.

Against this background, the contemporary food system is not theologically neutral. A global food system that extracts grain from subsistence farming communities in sub-Saharan Africa to feed livestock for export markets in wealthier countries is doing something specific with the earth and with human bodies. A farm subsidy system that consistently advantages large commodity producers at the expense of small and mid-sized family farms is making a choice about who controls food production. A trade architecture that has repeatedly required developing nations to open their markets to subsidized agricultural imports while preventing those same nations from protecting their own farmers [cite — confirm] is an exercise of power over the most basic conditions of life — and it has a body count.

The church cannot be theologically serious about imago Dei — the full humanity of every person — and simultaneously indifferent to the conditions that determine whether that person can eat. The image of God is not a spiritual designation that floats above material existence. It is a claim about the full person, including the body that requires food, the family that requires land, the community that requires the capacity to feed itself.

The counter-argument is worth taking seriously. It goes like this: the church is not a political movement, and the complexity of global agricultural policy exceeds the competence of most pastoral communities. The risk of the church becoming an advocacy organization for particular agrarian movements is real. The gospel is not reducible to economic policy, and the pastor who turns every Sunday into a political education session has misunderstood his vocation.

This is not wrong. The church is not primarily a policy lobby, and the pastoral vocation is not coextensive with the advocacy organizations that work these issues with greater expertise and accountability. But the counter-argument proves too much if it is used to justify a silence so total that the congregation never hears that food is a justice issue, never encounters the biblical material on gleaning and Jubilee and the feeding of multitudes in anything other than its spiritualized form, never learns that the choices embedded in their food system have consequences for the bodies and livelihoods of people who bear the image of God in communities they will never visit.

Silence on this is not pastoral neutrality. It is pastoral formation by default — forming the congregation in the assumption that the food system has nothing to do with the gospel, which is itself a theological position, and a false one.

What does faithfulness look like, concretely? It looks like a congregation that understands the connection between the Lord's Table and the kitchen table of their neighbor. It looks like preaching that handles the gleaning laws and the Jubilee provisions as live texts with a claim on the present rather than as historical curiosities. It looks like congregations that support local food sovereignty efforts — community gardens, food co-ops, advocacy for policies that protect small farmers and increase food access in low-income neighborhoods — not because these are the full answer to global agricultural injustice but because they are the nearest expression of the conviction that the earth is the Lord's and its produce is gift, not commodity.

The table the church gathers around each week is a table where a body was broken so that life could be distributed without means test to anyone who comes hungry. The logic of that table runs in a specific direction.

The question is whether the congregation is being formed to follow it past the sanctuary door.

The earth yields. The question has always been: who decides for whom.
