New pastors often feel that being available to everyone at all times is the highest expression of pastoral love. In reality, it is a path to burnout that serves no one well. A pastor who has no margin has no depth — and depth is exactly what people need in a crisis.

That equation sounds simple. It is not. The pull toward total availability is not laziness or boundary confusion — it is something that feels, in the moment, like love. The phone rings at 10 p.m. and someone is struggling, and saying yes to the call feels like giving yourself away for the flock. It feels like the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to find the one (Luke 15:4). And it is not wrong to feel that pull. What is wrong is mistaking the feeling for a theology of ministry.

The shepherd in that parable comes back. He finds the lost sheep and he carries it home, and then there is a feast. The parable is not about a shepherd who spent every hour searching every hillside for every sheep simultaneously. It is about focused attention at a moment of acute need, followed by restoration, followed by community. There is a shape to it. The shape matters.

## The Structural Trap

The mistake most new pastors make is not moral. It is architectural. They build a ministry structure that has no design for rest, for preparation, for the pastor's own spiritual formation — and then they are surprised when the building collapses under its own weight. Eugene Peterson called this "the busy pastor" in his writing on pastoral ministry, and he was not gentle about it [cite — confirm]. He argued that busyness in a pastor is not a sign of dedication. It is a sign of disorder. The busy pastor has confused activity with faithfulness, and the confusion costs everyone, most of all the people who need genuine spiritual care.

What does total availability actually produce? Consider what it does to the sermon. A pastor who has given his full attention to the urgencies of the week arrives at Sunday morning depleted, with whatever he could scrape together in the hours that remained. The congregation receives something that was assembled under pressure. They do not know this. They receive it as care, because they trust the pastor. But what they are actually receiving is the residue of a man who has given everything to the immediate and had nothing left for the considered.

The congregation deserves the prepared pastor. Preparation requires time that is protected from interruption. That time is not selfishness. It is part of the pastor's job — arguably the most important part, the part that allows everything else to be done well.

## What Genuine Care Requires

Genuine pastoral care requires a pastor who has been nourished themselves. Setting boundaries on your availability is not selfishness — it is stewardship of the resource your congregation most needs: you, at your best.

The word stewardship applies here precisely because the pastor does not belong to himself. He belongs to the congregation and to God. And the congregation's long-term interest is not a pastor who is present at every moment but gradually hollowing out. Their long-term interest is a pastor who is present at the moments that matter most with something real to give.

I have made the other mistake. I have prided myself on being the pastor who always picks up the phone, who never says no to a hospital visit, who returns every message the same day. And I have stood in the pulpit on Sunday having given away everything that week and preached something that I am not proud of — not because I do not care about the text, but because I arrived at the text with nothing left. The congregation that week did not receive care. They received performance. The distinction matters and I did not always have the courage to admit it.

The counter-position deserves an honest hearing: is there not a risk that a pastor who schedules and protects his time will become inaccessible, distant, the kind of leader who cares more about his study than his sheep? Yes. That risk is real. It is the failure mode in the opposite direction. The pastor who has so fortified his calendar that crises feel like intrusions has mistaken order for ministry. He has confused a healthy structure with a spiritual discipline, and the people in his care will feel the coldness of it.

The answer is not to swing between the two errors. It is to develop the discernment that the structures are meant to serve. Protected time is not a rule to follow; it is a value to practice — the value of arriving at the work with depth enough to actually do it. When genuine urgency arises, the prepared pastor can give himself away freely because he is not already empty. The margin makes the interruption possible without becoming the pattern.

## The Theological Ground

There is a theological claim underneath all of this that is worth making explicit. The pastor who cannot receive care — who cannot name his own need, protect his own soul, or acknowledge that he too is a creature with limits — has subtly placed himself outside the Gospel he preaches. He has constructed a pastoral identity that is functionally self-sufficient, and self-sufficiency is not a Christian virtue. It is the fall's signature.

Jesus withdrew. Not once, as an exception, but repeatedly, as a pattern. He withdrew to pray (Luke 5:16). He withdrew after the feeding of the five thousand (Matthew 14:23). He withdrew before the crucifixion to Gethsemane, and the withdrawal was not escape — it was preparation, the night before the hardest hour. If the Son of God built withdrawal into his own ministry rhythm, the pastor who believes he cannot afford to is not being more faithful than Jesus. He is being less honest about his own nature.

Paul makes the same point structurally. He asks the Thessalonians to pray for him (1 Thessalonians 5:25). He asks the Romans to strive together with him in prayer (Romans 15:30). The apostle to the Gentiles, the man who planted churches across the Mediterranean world, publicly acknowledged his need for the prayers of the people he served. He did not perform self-sufficiency. He named a real dependence. The pastor who refuses to do the same has not surpassed Paul. He has simply closed himself off from something Paul was wise enough to receive.

## What to Build Instead

The practical shape of this is less romantic than the principle. It means a weekly sabbath that is actually observed — not a half-day of lighter work, but a genuine cessation. It means two or three hours in the week that are formally protected for study and prayer, marked on the calendar as the appointments they are. It means a decision, made in advance and not in the moment, about what hours are available for pastoral emergencies and what hours belong to the pastor's own formation and family.

None of this means ignoring genuine crises. A real emergency — the unexpected death, the acute breakdown, the marriage on the edge — calls for whatever is required. The pastor who has built margin into his week can give himself fully to the emergency precisely because the emergency is not competing with six other things he already gave himself to.

The congregation does not need a pastor who is available at all times. It needs a pastor who is present — genuinely, deeply, soulfully present — when presence matters. Presence requires preparation. Preparation requires limits. The limits are not the opposite of love. They are, in the long run, one of love's most necessary disciplines.

A pastor I respect once said that he could tell whether a visit was going to be useful within the first five minutes, and the difference was almost always whether he had prayed before he walked in the door. Not a quick prayer in the car, but actual prayer — real listening, real attentiveness to what God might have in this hour. That quality of presence was not something he could manufacture on the spot after a week of total availability. It was the fruit of a week that still had some silence in it.

The congregation deserves that fruit. So does the pastor. Build the structure that makes it possible, and defend it not because you matter more than the people you serve, but because the people you serve need you whole.

That is not a license for absence. It is a discipline for depth. The two things are not in tension. They are, rightly ordered, the same thing.

The pastor who builds this structure will face resistance — from the congregation that has come to expect total availability, from his own internalized sense that stepping back is a betrayal of the calling. Neither is right. The congregation's expectation was never a theological claim; it was a habit, and habits can be reformed. His internalized sense is the voice of a pastoral culture that confused giving everything with giving something real, and the culture is wrong. You cannot give from nothing. A well does not produce water it has not received. The pastor who has not been nourished — by silence, by study, by genuine rest, by the community of peers and mentors who give him what he gives everyone else — will eventually have nothing to give, and will discover that the congregation he was trying to serve deserved more than the residue of a man who ran out.
