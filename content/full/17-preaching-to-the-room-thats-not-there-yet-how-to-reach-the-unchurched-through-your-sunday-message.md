Carey Nieuwhof's 2025 church trend research surfaced a statistic that should stop every pastor cold: only one percent of pastors rate their church as very effective at reaching people who have no church background. One percent. After decades of outreach programming, evangelism training, church growth methodology, and cultural relevance conversations, the overwhelming majority of churches in America have a Sunday morning that is almost entirely inaccessible to the people who most need what happens there.

Part of this is structural — the church has always struggled to reach those who do not come. But part of it is homiletical. The way most preachers are trained to preach assumes a level of biblical literacy and theological familiarity that a growing percentage of the culture simply does not have. The room that is there — the one full of people who already know the language and the stories and the assumptions — gets fed. The room that is not there yet — the one full of people who would be confused, alienated, or talked past by the average Sunday message — never gets invited back.

## The Historical Roots of the Problem

The homiletical tradition most American pastors inherited was shaped primarily for Christian audiences. The great preachers of the church's history — Augustine, Chrysostom, Luther, Wesley, Spurgeon, Jonathan Edwards — were, by and large, preaching to communities in which the Christian frame was culturally assumed. Even the revivalists of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries preached to audiences with significant prior exposure to Scripture and church culture. The unchurched person at a Whitefield meeting had still grown up in a Protestant England where the King James Bible was the national book.

The cultural context has shifted dramatically and relatively quickly. Robert Bellah's analysis of American individualism in *Habits of the Heart* [cite — confirm] identified the declining force of biblical religion as a public moral language as early as 1985. Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age* describes the movement from a society in which belief in God was the default, the unchallenged background, to one in which belief is one option among many [cite — confirm]. The person walking into your church for the first time in 2025 does not share the background assumptions that were still common in the person walking into a church for the first time in 1975.

And yet the preaching has not changed proportionally. The sermon that assumes biblical literacy, that uses insider language without explanation, that makes its arguments from scriptural authority without establishing why scriptural authority should be accepted — that sermon is operating in 1975 while standing in a room full of people from 2025.

## Understanding Who Isn't in the Room

The unchurched person who might one day find their way into your church does not share your background assumptions about the authority of Scripture, the basic contours of the gospel, or the meaning of common religious language. They may have a vague spirituality that bears little resemblance to Christian theology. They may have been hurt by a church or a religious family member and carry that wound into the room. They may simply be curious — responding to an invitation from a friend, open to something but not knowing what — and completely without the cultural competence to decode what is happening around them.

They are not stupid. They are not hostile. They are not a problem to be solved. They are people made in the image of God, for whom Christ died, who are sitting in your church because something brought them there, and who will decide within the first few minutes of your sermon whether to come back based on whether what you are saying seems to have anything to do with their actual life.

The preacher who has never consciously imagined this person is preaching to a room that will never include them. The preacher who has genuinely imagined them — who has sat with their questions, who has felt the weight of their skepticism, who has asked what a person with no Christian background would hear in this sentence, this illustration, this appeal — is doing something that requires both homiletical skill and genuine pastoral empathy.

Tim Keller modeled this consistently in his years at Redeemer Presbyterian Church in Manhattan, where the congregation included a significant proportion of unchurched professionals who were intellectually serious and spiritually skeptical. His approach was not to simplify the gospel but to surface and answer the objections that his unchurched listeners would raise rather than assuming away the need for an apologetic [cite — confirm]. He took the questions of the room that was not yet there seriously enough to make them the starting point of his preaching.

## Preaching That Includes Without Compromising

Preaching to the unchurched does not mean abandoning theological substance or diluting the gospel to the point of inoffensiveness. The goal is not a message that does not challenge anyone. It is a message whose challenge is accessible, whose terms are defined, and whose assumptions are made explicit rather than assumed.

In practice, this means defining terms that feel obvious to the regular attender but are not. What is grace, exactly — not the theological definition that the congregation could recite, but the thing it actually refers to in human experience, the thing that makes it distinct from what the world offers in its place? What does "the cross" mean, and why does it matter — not to someone who grew up in Sunday school, but to someone who has spent their adult life constructing a moral framework that does not include it? What is the church, and what is it for — and how is the answer different from what the person sitting in that seat has assumed based on every interaction they have had with Christianity so far?

It means connecting the text to universal human experiences — longing, failure, grief, hope, the search for meaning — before connecting it to specific theological categories. The person who has no framework for "justification by faith" has a framework for the experience of being found acceptable, of being forgiven for something that felt unforgivable, of receiving something they did not earn. Start there. The theological category will follow if the human experience is genuinely entered first.

And it means adopting a posture in the pulpit that is genuinely curious and genuinely inviting rather than one that speaks from assumed authority to assumed agreement. The unchurched person is listening for contempt — for the preacher's implicit communication that people who do not already believe are less intelligent, less moral, or less worthy of being taken seriously. They have experienced enough of it to have an excellent detector for it. Remove it entirely from your preaching.

## The Pastoral Scene

A young woman named Sarah attended a church for the first time on Easter Sunday, brought by a friend who had been asking her to come for two years. She had no church background, had read exactly none of the Bible, and arrived with the specific expectation that the service would be irrelevant to her actual life and that she would be making a quiet exit as soon as it was socially acceptable.

The pastor's sermon that Sunday was on John 20 — Mary Magdalene at the empty tomb, the disciples running, the conversation between Mary and the risen Jesus. The pastor began not with the resurrection's theological significance but with what Mary was actually doing there: she had come to care for a body. She expected death. She was bringing what you bring when someone you love is irreversibly gone. The pastor spent four minutes describing what that felt like — the specific kind of grief that is the grief of the day after, when the shock has worn off and the finality is settling in.

Sarah told me later that those four minutes were why she came back the following Sunday. Not the theology. The recognition. The preacher had accurately described something she had felt at her mother's graveside three years earlier, and that accurate description created enough trust for her to stay for the rest.

The resurrection arrived in the sermon after the grief had been genuinely entered. She could receive it because she had been met first.

## What Happens to Your Regular Attenders

Here is what many pastors fear but should not: that preaching in a way that is accessible to the unchurched will bore or diminish the experience for the theologically formed regular attender. The opposite tends to be true.

Sermons that begin with genuine human reality, that take the time to surface and answer objections rather than assuming agreement, and that connect the gospel to the actual texture of ordinary life tend to be more engaging for everyone — including the person who has been in the church for decades. Depth and accessibility are not opposites. The best preaching achieves both, and does so not by splitting the difference between them but by refusing to accept the premise that they are in competition.

The regular attender who has heard the resurrection story fifty times has not heard the version of it that genuinely answers a thoughtful skeptic's best objection. Watching their pastor answer that objection with genuine intellectual honesty is often one of the most formation-producing experiences they have in the pew.

## The Verdict

One percent. That is the number to sit with. One pastor in a hundred rates his congregation as genuinely effective at reaching the people the church claims to be trying to reach.

The gap between what the church says it is for and what its Sunday morning actually produces is enormous, and the sermon is one of the primary places where that gap either narrows or widens.

The room that is not there yet is full of people who deserve a preacher who has imagined them before he arrived at the pulpit — who has asked what they would hear, what they would need, what would have to be true of the next sixty minutes for them to consider that coming here was worth their Sunday morning.

Ask that question before you preach. Let it shape the opening. Let it shape the terms you define and the objections you answer and the human reality you enter before the theological category you name.

The room that is not there yet will fill, or it will not. But the first step is preaching as if it could.
