On a given Sunday morning in the congregation I serve, the same sermon lands in a room containing a retired schoolteacher who has carried orthodox faith for fifty years and finds abstract theology comforting, a thirty-year-old man attending for the first time who would not identify himself as a Christian but came with his wife because their marriage is in trouble, a recent immigrant for whom the English language is still an obstacle course, and a teenager who is secretly losing her grip on everything her parents believe. The sermon is one sermon. These are four completely different situations. They are not the exception — they are the room.

The preacher who has not reckoned with the diversity of the room will do one of two things. He will aim for the center and hope the edges receive something. Or he will aim for one audience — usually the audience most like himself — and let everyone else work out how to translate it. Neither strategy is preaching. They are, at best, one variety of public address delivered to a roomful of people who deserved more than a variety.

The problem is older than the contemporary congregation. The New Testament itself is, in part, an extended argument about how the same gospel reaches people who are nothing alike. In Acts 2, Peter preaches to Jewish pilgrims steeped in the Hebrew prophets; the proclamation is saturated with Joel and David and the whole arc of covenant history. In Acts 17, Paul stands before Athenian philosophers at the Areopagus and begins not with Moses but with their own altar inscription, their own poets, the common ground of created humanity before God. Same gospel. Completely different approach. Paul is not changing the message. He is incarnating it — finding the point of entry that makes the same truth arrive in a different life.

The Incarnation is the theological principle behind all of this, and it deserves more weight than we usually give it. The Word becoming flesh (John 1:14) was not God's concession to human limitation. It was God's demonstration of how eternal truth reaches particular people: not by hovering above the particularity of human experience but by entering it, fully, without remainder. Jesus did not preach the Sermon on the Mount to an abstracted generic audience. He preached it to the poor, the mourning, the persecuted — people for whom the promise of a kingdom that worked differently was not an interesting philosophical proposition but a claim about whether their life could be otherwise. The specificity was not incidental to the message. The specificity was how the message arrived.

The preacher's work is to do what the Incarnation models: to speak the unchanging word into the specific situation. Not to change the gospel. To speak it with the kind of particularity that makes it arrive — that makes the man whose marriage is breaking apart hear not a general religious communication but a word addressed to him in the actual circumstances of his life. This is not manipulation. It is the ancient pastoral practice of preaching as a form of care, a craft the church largely understood until the industrial model of Sunday morning turned preaching into broadcast.

What does it require? First, it requires knowing the room. Not abstractly — not the demographic report — but knowing the specific people in it, their specific weights, what they carry when they walk in and what they need to carry out. This is the argument for pastoral visiting, for genuine congregational community, for the practice of listening throughout the week so that the Sunday sermon is already in dialogue with the actual lives of actual people before the first sentence is spoken.

Fred Craddock, one of the most careful thinkers about preaching in the last century, argued that the sermon must move — that it cannot simply state its conclusion and expect the listener to be formed by the statement [cite — confirm]. Formation requires the congregation to travel with the text, to arrive at the truth through a movement that mirrors the movement of genuine discovery. The congregation that is only told what to believe has been instructed. The congregation that is walked through the same territory the preacher walked — doubt, resistance, encounter, yielding — has been shaped by something. Craddock's insight is partly about homiletical method. But the deeper truth is that the movement of the sermon must match the movement of real lives, and real lives in the same room are moving from very different starting points.

Second, it requires theological generativity — the ability to ask, with genuine curiosity, what the same text yields when it is read from a different situation. The parable of the Prodigal Son (Luke 15) does not say the same thing to the waiting father as it says to the younger son as it says to the older brother who never left. All three of those characters are in the room on Sunday morning. The person who has never strayed and is quietly resentful of those who did and were forgiven anyway. The person who has strayed and cannot believe the welcome is real. The person who has been waiting and whose waiting has become a kind of hope he can barely sustain. The preacher who only addresses the returning prodigal — who knows only one way the text can land — has preached one third of the sermon.

There is a legitimate counter-position here, and it deserves honest engagement. The argument for simplicity goes like this: the attempt to address everyone equally produces a preacher so attentive to the diversity of his audience that he loses the nerve to say anything clear. The quest to offend no one produces sermons that say nothing sharply. Clarity requires commitment to what the text actually says, and that commitment must not be dissolved by the desire to be relevant to every conceivable situation.

This is true, and it must be heard. The preacher who has no center — who is so attentive to the variety of the room that he can never say the clear, costly thing — is not addressing the diversity of his congregation. He is avoiding his responsibility to all of them. Clarity and particularity are not opposites. The clear sentence that lands in a specific life is more powerful than the vague sentence that is inoffensive to everyone. The failure mode is not particularizing the gospel. The failure mode is using the particularity as a reason to never say the hard thing to anyone.

The resolution is this: preach with enough specificity that multiple different people recognize themselves in the same words. This is what Scripture itself does, across centuries and cultures. Paul writes to a first-century congregation in Rome, and his letter names something recognizable in twenty-first century Michigan. Not because he was trying to be timeless — he was writing to a specific church with specific problems — but because the human condition he addressed is genuinely shared, and the truth he proclaimed reaches it wherever it appears.

The preacher who knows his room will know which specific weights to name — and when he names them with enough precision, people who are carrying different weights will recognize in the naming something of their own. The man with the failing marriage and the woman with the fifty-year faith and the teenager losing her grip are not, at depth, in completely separate situations. They are all people asking whether the promises are real. The preacher who goes to that depth — who preaches from within the question rather than above it — will reach rooms he cannot see.

There is a craft dimension to this that requires naming. The difference between a generic sentence and a particular one is often a single concrete detail. Not "some of you are struggling" but "the conversation you did not have last night." Not "the world is broken" but "the phone call you are dreading on Monday." The detail does not limit the audience — it expands it. Because the man who did not have the conversation last night leans forward, and the woman who is dreading Tuesday's call leans forward, and the teenager who hasn't spoken honestly to her parents in months leans forward. The concrete detail becomes the door through which different people in genuinely different situations enter the same room.

Eugene Peterson spent years arguing that pastoral care and preaching are not two separate activities — that the preacher who visits, listens, and knows the particular lives of particular people brings that knowledge into the pulpit and it changes how the text arrives [cite — confirm]. This is why the minister who knows no one is a fundamentally different preacher than the minister who knows everyone. The former broadcasts. The latter speaks. The congregation can feel the difference. They have felt both, and they know which one is addressing them.

The gospel does not require translation. It requires incarnation. Every week, in every room, again.

This does not get easier. The room changes. People grow, suffer, leave, arrive. The preacher who knew the congregation well last year has to keep learning the congregation that exists now. This is not a problem to solve once. It is a pastoral discipline — the ongoing practice of paying attention, of reading the room not as a demographic but as a collection of actual people whose actual lives are the territory the sermon must enter.

What the congregation needs from the pulpit is not universal truth delivered to a generic person. They need the universal truth to arrive in their specific life, to find them where they actually are and speak to what they actually carry. That requires a preacher who has gone deep enough into the text to find the place where the text is addressing all of them — the human condition beneath the particulars — and who has gone deep enough into their lives to know how to say it in a way that arrives.

That is what preaching is for. Not performance. Not instruction. Arrival — the word finding the person, in their actual situation, with enough specificity to say: this is for you. Not because you are like everyone else. Because the God who speaks has found you here.

That is the work. It does not simplify. It demands everything the preacher has. But a congregation formed by preaching that takes that seriously will be different from a congregation formed by broadcast. They will carry the weight of the word into their Tuesday. That is what the Sunday hour is actually for.
