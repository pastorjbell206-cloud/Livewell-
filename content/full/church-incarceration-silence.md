The United States incarcerates more people per capita than any nation on earth. [cite — confirm current figures] The people held in its prisons and jails are disproportionately poor, disproportionately Black and Brown, and disproportionately drawn from communities the church claims to care about. Many of them are fathers. Many of them are sons. Some of them are members of congregations that have not spoken their names from the pulpit since they were arrested.

The church has been mostly quiet about this. That silence is not neutrality. It is a statement about whose suffering qualifies for prophetic attention.

## What the Texts Say

Jesus, in Matthew 25, names the incarcerated explicitly among those with whom he identifies: "I was in prison and you came to me." Not you visited the prison as a ministry. Not you donated to the prison chaplaincy budget. You came to me. The incarcerated person is, in Jesus's own account, the site of his presence. To visit is to encounter Christ. To ignore is to pass him.

This is not an incidental detail. Matthew 25:31-46 is one of the most structurally significant passages in the Gospels — the judgment scene where the nations are divided, where the basis of the division is not doctrinal fidelity or ritual observance but care for "the least of these." Hungry, thirsty, stranger, naked, sick, imprisoned — these six categories constitute Jesus's description of his own location among the vulnerable. He does not say he is spiritually present with the respectable. He says he is concretely present with the abandoned. The church that has ignored this passage's specific instruction about prisoners has not read it carefully.

The early church took this seriously. Prison epistles — Paul writing from Roman custody to the Philippians, to Philemon, to Timothy — form a significant portion of the New Testament. The early Christian community maintained contact with imprisoned members at considerable personal risk. [cite — confirm on personal risk of visiting prisoners in Rome] By the third century, care for the imprisoned was considered a standard obligation of the Christian community, not a specialized ministry for the particularly zealous. John Chrysostom in the fourth century preached repeatedly about the obligation to visit the imprisoned and named the failure to do so as a failure of love toward Christ himself. [cite — confirm Chrysostom reference]

The medieval church had prison ministry. The Reformers wrote from prison — Luther at the Wartburg, though under protective custody, not criminal confinement; Tyndale in a Vilvoorde cell before his execution; Bunyan in a Bedford jail where he wrote Pilgrim's Progress. The Quakers, in the eighteenth century, pioneered prison reform as a theological conviction — John Howard's work on prison conditions in England in the 1770s grew directly from Quaker commitments about human dignity. [cite — confirm Howard] Elizabeth Fry, a Quaker minister, organized systematic ministry to women in Newgate Prison in the early nineteenth century and became one of the most significant prison reformers in British history. [cite — confirm Fry details]

This history is not incidental. It is the record of what the church believed it was obligated to do, and it ran continuously until something changed.

## What Changed

The carceral expansion in the United States began in earnest in the 1970s and accelerated through the 1980s and 1990s. Policies that produced mandatory minimum sentences, three-strikes provisions, and the militarized prosecution of drug offenses produced an incarcerated population that grew from roughly 300,000 in 1975 to over two million by the early 2000s. [cite — confirm these figures] These were policy choices, not the inevitable response to rising crime. In many cases, crime fell while incarceration rose. The expansion was political.

The church was not absent from the politics of this era. White evangelical Christianity was, in many places, an enthusiastic constituency for the policies that produced the expansion. The language of law and order, the equation of toughness on crime with moral seriousness, the cultural positioning of the incarcerated as the deserving victims of their own choices — these were not theologically neutral positions. They were positions the church, in many cases, baptized.

This is not a comfortable sentence to write. I hold conservative convictions on many things. I believe in personal responsibility. I believe sin is real and that actions have consequences and that consequences are not always unjust. But the prophetic tradition does not permit selective application of those convictions. The same evangelical churches that affirmed the dignity of the unborn were, in many cases, simultaneously affirming policies that stripped dignity from people once they were born and convicted. The consistency the gospel requires was not consistently applied.

And the racial dimension cannot be separated from the theological analysis. Michelle Alexander's argument in *The New Jim Crow* — that mass incarceration functions as a system of racialized social control — is contested in its specifics but the underlying pattern is observable without any particular theoretical framework: Black Americans are incarcerated at approximately five times the rate of white Americans [cite — confirm], despite roughly comparable rates of drug use across racial groups. [cite — confirm] The system does not operate neutrally. The church that affirms the equal dignity of every human being while accepting a system that operates with such a dramatic racial disparity has affirmed a doctrine it is not actually applying.

## The Specific Forms of the Silence

The silence takes several shapes. There is the silence of the congregation that has no prison ministry, no reentry support, no relationship with the county jail, no advocacy for the people cycling through the system in its neighborhood — and has never named this as a gap. There is the silence of the preacher who addresses addiction, sexual sin, anger, and pride from the pulpit, and has never once named the racial disparities in drug prosecution that land a young Black man in a federal cell for a crime that earns a young white man a treatment referral. There is the silence of the denomination that has position papers on marriage and sexuality and medical ethics and has nothing systematic to say about an incarceration system that costs families generations.

These silences are choices. They are shaped by who is in the pews and who is not. The families of the incarcerated, the people who carry the stigma, the ones who can't get housing or employment because of a conviction from twenty years ago — they are less likely to be in the room when the elders decide what the church cares about. Absence from the room produces absence from the agenda. What the congregation cannot see, the church does not name. And what the church does not name, the congregation does not reckon with.

The incarcerated man who gave his life to Christ in a prison ministry service, who has been mentored and changed, who is released and wants a church home — this man faces a reception in most evangelical congregations that is considerably more complicated than the gospel he received promises it should be. The stigma travels. The background check travels. The restricted housing options travel. The employment barriers travel. The church that led him to faith is not typically the church that walks with him through the obstacles the state has erected around his reentry. That gap is a form of silence too.

## The Counter-Position

The serious objection: the church has limited resources and many legitimate needs, and prison ministry is genuinely difficult and genuinely specialized. Reentry support requires institutional relationships, legal knowledge, and persistent commitment that smaller congregations cannot easily develop. The silence may be less about indifference than about capacity.

There is something real in this. Ministry to the incarcerated and to people in reentry is hard, and the church should not be guilt-tripped into programs it cannot sustain. Better to do nothing than to start a prison ministry for the sermon series and then abandon it when the difficulty becomes apparent.

But the argument proves too much. The silence of most evangelical churches about mass incarceration is not primarily about program capacity. It is about whether the issue is on the moral map at all. Churches that have no prison ministry often also have no position, no preaching, no congregational awareness, no theological framework for thinking about the two million people in American cages and the seven million more under some form of correctional supervision. [cite — confirm figures] The absence is not logistical. It is categorial. The issue simply does not register as the kind of thing the church needs to reckon with.

That is the silence that requires naming.

## What Faithfulness Looks Like

Not silence. Not the silence of confusion or the silence of overextension or the silence of "we don't want to be political." The silence of the prophets was not a posture they adopted when the situation was complex. Jeremiah did not stay quiet about the conditions under which people were held captive and exploited. Isaiah 61:1, which Jesus stands and reads in the Nazareth synagogue when he announces his own ministry, is explicit: he has been anointed to "proclaim liberty to the captives and the opening of the prison to those who are bound." This is not metaphorical in its first-century context. Jesus was reading that text in a world where the Roman carceral system was real and present. He named liberation from imprisonment as part of the agenda of the kingdom of God.

Matthew 25 does not ask the church to have a carefully calibrated position on sentencing reform. It asks whether we visited. Whether we came. Whether the man in the cell knew that there were people outside it who had not forgotten him.

That is where it starts. The policy work is downstream from the visiting. The advocacy is downstream from the relationship. The reformation of an unjust system is downstream from the church deciding that the people inside it are not invisible.

They were never invisible to Jesus. We chose not to see them.

The silence was a choice. It can be unmade. The question is whether the cost of ending the silence — the discomfort of the sermon, the disruption of the congregation's self-understanding, the inconvenience of the visit — is something we are willing to bear in behalf of the people whose invisibility we have been sustaining with our quiet.

The man in the cell is not waiting for us to resolve the policy question. He is waiting for someone to come.
