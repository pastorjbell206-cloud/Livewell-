We have made the incarnation into a preface. It is the setup before the cross, the necessary precondition for the atonement, the means by which God positioned himself to accomplish what we really needed, which was the forgiveness of sins. Jesus had to be human in order to die for human sins. The incarnation served that purpose. Once the purpose was accomplished — once the cross was finished and the resurrection confirmed — we moved forward.

This is not wrong. It is a reduction that costs us more than we have calculated.

The incarnation is not a method God chose for a specific purpose. It is a disclosure. It is God showing us what God is, what humanity is, and what the relationship between them is meant to be. The eternal Son of God assumed human flesh — not temporarily, not as a disguise worn for thirty-three years and then removed. The resurrection is the resurrection of the body. The ascension is the ascension of the body. Jesus, at the right hand of the Father, is still embodied. He will return in the body. The permanent assumption of human flesh into the life of God is itself a theological statement, and we have mostly not thought about what it means.

## What Nicaea Was Protecting

The councils of the fourth and fifth centuries fought bitterly over the exact terms by which the incarnation should be understood, and the fighting was necessary because the alternatives were genuinely destructive. The Docetists, who were among the earliest heretics the church had to address, believed that Jesus only appeared to be human — that the eternal Word had clothed himself in the appearance of flesh without actually becoming flesh. Their error seemed pious: surely God could not be contaminated by material existence. But if Jesus only appeared to be human, then the suffering of the cross was an appearance, the death was an appearance, and the resurrection was a reversal of an appearance. Nothing real was at stake. Nothing real was accomplished.

John's prologue refuses this: "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). Not the Word appeared to be flesh. Not the Word clothed himself in the appearance of flesh. The Word became flesh. The Greek is not ambiguous. Sarx — flesh, the material human body in its full vulnerability and mortality — is what the Word became.

Athanasius understood why this mattered. In his treatise On the Incarnation [cite — confirm], he argued that only God could repair what humanity had lost, but only a human could stand in humanity's place. The one who becomes flesh and takes the consequence of what flesh had done must be both. The double requirement — truly God, truly human — is not a logical puzzle to be solved. It is the structure of the rescue operation. And the rescue operation, once completed, did not undo the becoming. The Word remains flesh.

The Council of Chalcedon in 451 [cite — confirm] articulated what the church had been working toward for over a century: one person, two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation. This is not abstract philosophy. These four negatives are guards against four specific errors, each of which carries pastoral and ethical consequences. Without confusion: the human and divine are genuinely distinct — God did not dissolve into humanity, erasing its particularity. Without change: the divine was not altered by the becoming — God was not diminished. Without division: the two natures do not alternate or compartmentalize — Jesus does not shift between being human and being divine. Without separation: the union is permanent — there is no future state in which the incarnation is undone.

## What the Body Means After the Incarnation

Because God became human and remains human, the material world carries a dignity it could not have had otherwise. The body is not a spiritual embarrassment, a temporary housing for the real self, a liability to be overcome on the way to eternal disembodiment. The body is what God chose to inhabit and continues to inhabit. That changes everything about how we think about bodies — sick bodies, aging bodies, bodies in poverty, bodies with disabilities, bodies that are hungry or tired or in pain.

Paul's theology of the body flows directly from incarnation. "Do you not know that your bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit?" (1 Corinthians 6:19). Not metaphorically. As a matter of theological fact, the body that the Spirit inhabits has been given a dignity it could not claim by virtue of its material nature alone. The same Spirit who descended at the Jordan, who raised Jesus from the dead (Romans 8:11), inhabits the physical body of the believer. What is done to that body matters. What is done to the bodies of others matters. The incarnation makes bodily harm a theological category.

This is why the incarnation changes the ethics of poverty, of illness, of incarceration, of the treatment of the aging. When Jesus in Matthew 25 says that whatever is done to the least of these is done to him, the statement has weight only because the incarnation has established that God is genuinely present in the bodily suffering of human beings. It is not a rhetorical move. It is a consistent extension of what the incarnation already claims.

Irenaeus of Lyon, writing in the second century, offered a phrase that has endured precisely because it carries so much weight: "The glory of God is a human being fully alive" [cite — confirm]. He meant it as a statement about what the incarnation reveals — that the living human person is where God's glory is most fully displayed in the created order. The body is not incidental to that display. The body is the site of it. Irenaeus was answering the Gnostics who believed the body was a cage. He was saying: the cage is the temple. You have misread what you are looking at.

## What We Have Missed

The Gnostic instinct runs deep in American Christianity. The soul is what matters. The body is temporary. The spiritual experience is what we really come to church for, and the body is the address where the spiritual experience lives until the spiritual experience can be fully released from it.

The incarnation will not allow this. The resurrection will not allow this. The ascended, embodied Jesus at the right hand of the Father will not allow this.

If God became a body and still is a body, then bodies matter. The body of the person suffering with chronic pain matters. The body of the homeless person sleeping outside matters. The body of the prisoner matters. The body of the child born into poverty matters. They matter not merely as souls trapped in temporarily inconvenient circumstances, but as bodies that bear an image that God himself has inhabited.

I have preached incarnation for years and lived as if it were mostly about the soul's eternal destination. I have moved quickly past the bodily suffering of people I encountered, not because I did not care, but because I had not fully reckoned with what caring actually requires when you believe God has permanently inhabited flesh. The leap from theology to ethics is not a leap at all. It is a direct line. What you believe about the body of Jesus determines what you owe the body of your neighbor.

## The Counter-Argument That Has to Be Taken Seriously

The objection worth engaging honestly is this: you can take the incarnation seriously as a theological claim and still hold that the priority of Christian ministry is spiritual — the proclamation of the gospel, the conversion of souls, the formation of people toward eternal life. The body will pass. The soul endures. If you have finite time and resources, surely the soul is where the investment should be concentrated.

I understand this argument. I have made versions of it. But the incarnation itself refuses the clean separation it relies on. Jesus spent his ministry healing bodies. He fed crowds. He touched lepers — people who were not merely sick but ritually excluded, socially severed from community. He wept at a grave. The Logos, through whom all things were made (John 1:3), spent his human ministry in persistent, direct engagement with bodies in distress. The witness of the incarnation is not that bodies are secondary to souls. The witness is that the distinction between bodily and spiritual need is itself a construction the incarnation refuses to honor.

The care of bodies is not a distraction from spiritual ministry. It is spiritual ministry — the specific form of it that becomes available when the infinite God has chosen to enter and remain in the material order.

## The Permanent Weight of What God Did

Revelation 21 does not describe a purely spiritual final state. It describes the New Jerusalem coming down from heaven to earth — the renewal of the material, not its elimination. "God will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (Revelation 21:4). Tears are a bodily event. The final act of divine comfort is tactile. It involves God and bodies and the specific sorrows that bodies carry.

Taking the incarnation seriously is the single most demanding move Christian ethics can make. It requires us to care about bodies — all of them — in ways that cost something. It is far more comfortable to care about souls. Souls you can pray for without changing your budget. Bodies require something more: presence, money, political will, the willingness to sit in uncomfortable places with people whose material suffering makes claims on the life you have arranged for yourself.

God chose a body. The choosing was permanent. The body God chose was poor, born outside the structures of power, dependent on the charity of others before he was old enough to speak. He spent thirty years in a village whose name was a regional insult. He ate with people the respectable classes avoided. He died the death reserved for those whom Rome considered expendable.

The incarnation is not a story about God becoming human in order to solve a theological problem and then moving back to a more comfortable mode of existence. It is a story about God permanently joining the material order from inside it — and doing so at the precise point in the social structure where the material order's cost is most visible.

We should act accordingly. The theology demands it. The body of Christ, permanent and ascended, still bearing the wounds of what it cost, is the warrant for what it means to live as if God became human and stayed that way.

That is not a preface. That is the whole argument.
