The word "missionary" carries a specific image in the American evangelical imagination: a Western Christian, usually white, who leaves a comfortable life in the developed world to carry the gospel to a people who do not yet have it. That image is not entirely wrong. It describes something that has happened, at real cost, for centuries — from William Carey wading into Bengal in 1793 to the thousands of cross-cultural workers who serve today in difficult conditions. The sacrifice is real. The fruit has been real.

The image is also, increasingly, an incomplete account of where the missionary movement actually lives.

---

The center of global Christianity has shifted, and the shift is not subtle. The largest Christian populations are now in sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and parts of Asia [cite — confirm]. The fastest-growing churches in the world are in Nigeria, Brazil, Ethiopia, China, and South Korea. The countries that were once "mission fields" receiving workers from the West now send more missionaries, in raw numbers, than many Western nations. The Korean church has become one of the world's most significant missionary-sending communities, dispatching thousands of cross-cultural workers to contexts that Western churches cannot access [cite — confirm]. The Ethiopian church, the Nigerian church, the Brazilian Pentecostal movement — these are not young communities being nurtured along by Western missionaries. They are mature, rooted communities producing their own theologians, their own seminaries, their own pastors who go to places the Western church cannot follow.

This is not a story that the American church has fully absorbed. We continue, in many of our denominational structures and mission agency models, to operate on the assumptions of the earlier era: that the resources, the expertise, and the theological formation flow from the West outward, and that the primary task of the global church is to receive what the Western church sends.

Those assumptions are wrong. And continuing to act on them has consequences — not just for the effectiveness of global mission, but for the integrity of the Western church's own theological formation.

---

In 1907, Roland Allen published *Missionary Methods: St. Paul's or Ours?* [cite — confirm], and the question he asked has not aged. Paul's missionary method was to establish communities, trust those communities to the Holy Spirit, and leave — not return every year to oversee the community's theological development, not require Western organizational structures as the condition of legitimacy, not place the local leader in a subordinate relationship to the sending organization in perpetuity. The Western missionary movement, by contrast, developed institutional patterns that reproduced the colonial relationship even after the explicit colonial era ended: the national pastor as the local partner of the Western mission agency, accountable to the agency, dependent on the agency's funding, and therefore operating within the agency's assumptions about what the church should look like.

This is not a charge against the sincerity of the missionaries who worked within those patterns. Many of them gave their lives for the communities they served. The critique is structural, not personal. The structures reproduced a relationship of dependency that Paul did not establish and that the New Testament does not require.

The national pastor working in rural India or urban Lagos or the mountains of Ethiopia does not need the American church to send him a church planter. He may need the American church to send resources, to advocate for his community with the American government when that advocacy matters, to learn from his theological insights — particularly about how to follow Christ under pressure, which the comfortable Western church has very little experience doing — and to partner with him in the specific mission he is already executing with a depth of cultural competence that no outsider can replicate.

---

That reorientation requires genuine humility from the American church, and humility about mission is not one of our strongest institutional instincts.

We are accustomed to being the ones who send. The machinery of American missionary giving — the mission agencies, the support letters, the deputation circuits, the annual reports showing converts and church plants — is built around a donor who feels generous and a recipient who receives. That relationship is not morally neutral. It creates a power asymmetry that the gospel does not endorse, and it can produce, in both parties, a distorted picture of what the body of Christ actually looks like.

The donor who gives to send a missionary to the mission field has done something real. Giving is a spiritual practice that shapes the giver. But if the giving supports a structure in which the Western church always occupies the sending position and the global church always occupies the receiving position, the structure is lying about the body of Christ. It is implying a hierarchy that the New Testament does not recognize.

Paul did not write to the Philippians as a superior addressing subordinates. He wrote as a partner in the gospel, gratefully acknowledging their contribution to his work and his contribution to theirs (Philippians 1:3-7). The reciprocity was real and was named. That is the model the American church needs to rebuild its missionary imagination around.

---

The national pastor working in a context of persecution has things to teach the American church about faith under pressure that our comfort has prevented us from learning any other way. The Nigerian pastor whose congregation prays with a desperation that is not performance but necessity — because the alternative is despair, because the circumstances are genuinely dire — has things to teach the American congregation whose prayer life is anemic not from lack of theology but from lack of felt need. The Chinese house church pastor who has maintained a congregation for decades without buildings, budgets, or legal protection has things to teach the American pastor who defines the church by its facilities.

Lamin Sanneh, the Gambian-born scholar who spent decades at Yale, argued that the indigenization of Christianity — the capacity of the gospel to be received in every culture and translated into every language without losing its essential character — was itself a theological claim about the nature of God. [cite — confirm] The God of the Bible is not the God of the Western church. He is the God of Abraham, who was not Western; the God of Moses, who was African-born; the God who became incarnate in a Middle Eastern peasant community. The fact that the gospel has taken root in every cultural soil it has touched is not an interesting sociological phenomenon. It is the demonstration, in history, of the claim that God is the God of all peoples.

The American church that has not learned from the global church is not merely missing out on interesting perspectives. It is cutting itself off from a significant portion of what the Spirit has been doing in the world — and in the process impoverishing its own theological imagination.

---

There is a practical implication for the American pastor who leads a congregation that supports missionaries and mission agencies.

The question is not simply: how much money is going to mission? The question is: what kind of relationship are we funding? Are we funding a structure in which the national pastor is a subordinate partner, or are we funding genuine collaboration in which the national pastor's vision, leadership, and theological judgment are primary? Are we sending resources in a way that creates dependency, or in a way that builds indigenous capacity? Are we learning from the global church, or only teaching it?

These questions require actual relationships, which require actual investment of time and attention rather than money alone. The pastor who has a genuine friendship with a national pastor in a different part of the world — who knows his name, his family, his congregation's struggles, who has visited and been visited — is far more likely to ask these questions honestly than the pastor who writes a check to a mission agency and receives a quarterly newsletter.

The PCN network has the infrastructure for these relationships. The question is whether we are using it.

---

The gospel traveled from Jerusalem to Antioch to Rome. It did not stop there. It reached North Africa before Europe, and North Africa produced Tertullian, Origen, and Augustine — three of the most significant theological minds in the history of the church. [cite — confirm] The Western church has been, for much of its history, the inheritor of African theological labor.

The gospel has been traveling ever since — through the Syriac church into Persia and India, through the Celtic church into northern Europe, through the Korean church into the persecuted communities of North Asia. It has traveled in ways that the Western church did not organize and in some cases did not know about. The Spirit does not require our organizational structures as a precondition of movement.

What the Spirit does require is the humility to recognize movement that happens outside the frameworks we built. To receive the gospel as it has been received and shaped by communities that have lived inside it differently than we have. To be the body of Christ rather than the center of it.

The missionary movement of the twenty-first century is a conversation between the global church and the church in the West. The American pastor who has not yet begun listening to his end of that conversation is missing something essential — not just for the effectiveness of mission but for the integrity of his own faith.

It has been traveling ever since. Often without our help. Always without our permission.
