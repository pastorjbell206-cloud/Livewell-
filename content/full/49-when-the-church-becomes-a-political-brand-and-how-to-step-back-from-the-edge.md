It happens gradually, and then suddenly. The church that began with a genuine theological identity — a specific understanding of the gospel, a commitment to Scripture, a particular tradition of worship and community — finds itself, over the course of several years, more recognizable by its political tribe than by its theological one. The culture of the congregation has shifted. The sermon illustrations draw more from current events than from Scripture. The pastoral vision is increasingly framed in terms of cultural battle rather than the advance of the kingdom. And the people who are most engaged — who give the most, who volunteer the most, who show up to every event — are the ones whose primary motivation is political and cultural rather than deeply theological.

This is one of the most significant and least-discussed dangers facing American churches in the current moment, and it cuts across the political spectrum. The church that has been captured by the political right and the church that has been captured by the political left are both exhibiting the same underlying pathology, even when they would each define the other as the problem.

## How It Happens

Political capture of a church rarely happens through deliberate decision. It happens through a thousand small accommodations, each of which seems reasonable in the moment. The pastor makes a comment from the pulpit that resonates with one political tribe and generates enthusiastic response — and learns that this kind of response is easier to produce than the response to careful expository preaching. The church takes a public position on a social issue and discovers that the position, rather than the gospel, becomes the thing it is known for in the community. The congregation's leadership structure gradually populates with people who are primarily motivated by the cultural-political project rather than the theological one.

By the time the drift is visible, the congregation's culture has already shifted. The people who were there for the gospel have quietly departed. The people who remain are increasingly there for the political identity. And the pastor — who usually did not intend this outcome — is now leading a congregation that would be significantly disrupted by a return to genuine theological focus.

## The Historical Precedent

This is not a new danger. The church has always faced the temptation to collapse its theological identity into the political and cultural frameworks of the surrounding world.

In Germany in the 1930s, the German Christian movement — the Deutsche Christen — attempted to synthesize Protestant Christianity with National Socialist ideology. Pastors preached sermons that aligned the church with the aims of the regime. The swastika and the cross appeared together in sanctuaries. By the time the Confessing Church, led by figures like Dietrich Bonhoeffer and Karl Barth, declared through the Barmen Declaration in 1934 that the church's Lord was Jesus Christ and not any other authority, the institutional church in Germany had largely already made its accommodation [cite — confirm].

The Barmen Declaration's first thesis is a direct repudiation of political capture: "Jesus Christ, as he is attested for us in Holy Scripture, is the one Word of God which we have to hear and which we have to trust and obey in life and in death... We reject the false doctrine that the church could and should recognize as a source of its proclamation, beyond and besides this one Word of God, yet other events, powers, historical figures, and truths as God's revelation [cite — confirm]."

The political circumstances of twenty-first-century America are not Germany in 1934. The comparison should not be made glibly. But the structural dynamic — the pressure on the church to align its identity with a political project, the gradual accommodation that makes the crossing of each successive line seem less significant, the eventual loss of the theological independence that makes the church capable of prophetic speech at all — is recognizable in both contexts.

Bonhoeffer paid for his theological independence with his life. The cost in the American church is different. But it is real.

## Signs Worth Taking Seriously

Several warning signs indicate that a church may be drifting toward political capture. First: the pastor's most resonant sermons are about cultural or political topics rather than the core content of the gospel. Second: the church is more known in the community for its political positions than for its care of the poor, its welcome of the stranger, or its genuine discipleship. Third: members who hold different political views — even minor variations — do not feel genuinely welcome. Fourth: the primary driver of new attendance is political identity rather than genuine spiritual need.

A fifth, subtler sign: the pastor's reading life is increasingly drawn from political and cultural commentary rather than theology, Scripture scholarship, and the classical sources of the tradition. The pastor who is better versed in the current political moment than in the history of Christian thought is being formed by the wrong sources, and what they are being formed toward is not the gospel.

I have caught myself drifting in this direction. The pull is real because the cultural moment is genuinely urgent, because the congregation's anxiety is genuinely political, because responding to what feels most urgent in the room is a form of pastoral attentiveness that can be mistaken for faithfulness. But the pastor's primary formation should not be the news cycle. It should be the Scripture, the tradition, and the specific formation of prayer and theology that makes it possible to speak to the current moment from a position that is genuinely outside it.

## The Counter-Position

The counterargument is that the church's retreat from political engagement is itself a political act — that the claim to theological transcendence has historically been used to protect the comfortable from the prophetic, to silence the voices of those harmed by specific political decisions by insisting that the church is above the fray. The pastor who refuses to name specific injustices in the name of political neutrality has, in effect, aligned the church with the powerful by refusing to speak for those without power.

This is a serious argument and it deserves a serious answer. The response is not to return to spurious neutrality but to distinguish between prophetic speech and partisan alignment. The prophets of Israel — Amos, Micah, Isaiah — were not politically neutral. They named specific injustices with specific language and directed specific accusations at specific rulers. But they were not the prophets of any political party. Their authority came from the covenant between God and his people, and they were as willing to condemn the failures of Israel's religious establishment as the failures of foreign powers.

The church that has been captured by a political brand has lost that independence. It can only speak the prophetic word that its political tribe is comfortable hearing, which means it is not the prophetic word. Genuine prophetic speech makes everyone uncomfortable, including the speaker. That discomfort is not incidental. It is how you know you are speaking from outside the tribal framework rather than within it.

## The Path Back

Stepping back from the edge of political capture requires deliberate, sustained, and sometimes costly pastoral leadership. It means reclaiming the pulpit for genuinely theological content — not avoiding the intersection of faith and public life, but insisting that the Scriptures shape the conversation rather than the political moment. It means preaching through books of the Bible rather than through the current news cycle, letting the text set the agenda rather than the anxiety of the congregation.

It means explicitly naming the danger of political identity displacing theological identity, from the pulpit, with enough pastoral care to be heard rather than merely defended against. This naming should be done without partisan edge — without signaling to one side of the congregation that they are the target and the other side is the ally. The idolatry of political identity is symmetrical. The indictment runs in both directions.

It will almost certainly mean losing some people. The ones who are there primarily for the political project, who will not stay when the project is no longer the primary focus — their departure is painful and should not be minimized. But the alternative is a congregation that has become a political brand wearing a cross as its logo. That is a more profound kind of loss, and one that is much harder to recover from.

## The Weight of the Office

The pastor who steps back from the edge of political capture is not retreating from engagement with the world. They are reclaiming the specific kind of engagement that the pastoral office is meant to produce: the engagement that comes from a community whose primary loyalty is to Jesus Christ and whose witness in the world flows from that loyalty rather than from the priorities of any political faction.

That reclaiming requires the pastor to be genuinely, personally formed by the things that make them different from a political commentator: Scripture, prayer, the tradition of theological reflection, the specific practices of pastoral care that keep them tethered to the actual suffering and actual joy of the people they lead. The pastor who is formed primarily by political commentary will produce a politically formed congregation. The pastor who is formed primarily by the gospel will, over time, produce a gospelformed one.

The congregation that cannot be fully claimed by any political party — that keeps the gathering place of worship free from the tribal markings that make it feel like a rally — is not politically disengaged. It is performing the specific political act that the church is called to perform: the embodied demonstration that there is a loyalty that transcends every human political project, and a community formed by that loyalty rather than by any other.

That community is what the church exists to be. Recovering it, when it has been lost, is the most important pastoral work of the moment.

It is also the most costly. The cost is worth it.
