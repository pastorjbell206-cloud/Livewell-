Walter Brueggemann spent a career arguing that the most radical thing the church can do is imagine differently. His 1978 work *The Prophetic Imagination* made a claim that most American Christianity has never fully absorbed: the task of the prophet is not primarily to predict the future or even to critique the present. The task of the prophet is to offer an alternative to the dominant consciousness — to refuse the world as it is and to practice, in word and in community, the world as God intends it to be.

The dominant consciousness, in Brueggemann's reading of Israel's history, was royal consciousness — the ideology of Solomon's court, which offered affluence, oppressive social policy, and the static religion of a God whose primary function was to legitimize the existing order. The prophets were not conservative reformers trying to tweak the system. They were people who had been seized by a vision of the world that could not coexist with the world they were looking at, and who said so plainly and persistently at significant personal cost.

Jeremiah serves time in a cistern. Amos is expelled from Bethel. Isaiah walks naked through Jerusalem for three years as a sign against the political alliances he is protesting (Isaiah 20:2-3). The prophets were not comfortable members of the religious establishment who raised pointed questions about policy. They were people whose imagination had been captured by God and who could not stop speaking what they saw, regardless of where that speaking took them.

This is the tradition the church claims to stand in. The question is whether the contemporary church has retained the prophetic imagination — the capacity to see differently and to say what it sees — or whether it has become, like Solomon's court, an institution whose primary function is to legitimize the existing order.

The evidence is mixed, and not primarily in the direction of the prophetic tradition.

The prophetic imagination begins with grief. This is Brueggemann's central claim, and it is one the contemporary church has largely failed to practice. The prophet does not begin by offering solutions. The prophet begins by naming the death — by refusing the denial and the numbness that the dominant culture depends on to keep going. "Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by?" asks Lamentations 1:12. The prophetic voice is the voice that will not let the pain be smoothed over, will not permit the convenient explanation, will not allow the community to congratulate itself on its good intentions while the suffering continues.

The American church has become skilled at moving quickly past grief into encouragement. The worship service is designed to take people from their week into an experience of uplift. The sermon is designed to send people out with something they can use. Lament — sustained, public, communal acknowledgment that things are broken and that the brokenness is wrong — is not a standard feature of the evangelical liturgy. Which means the prophetic imagination cannot take root, because the prophetic imagination begins in the willingness to grieve what is.

The second element of prophetic imagination is the capacity for what Brueggemann calls energizing — the presentation of an alternative future that makes the present intolerable and the future possible. Isaiah 40-55, written to a people in exile who had concluded that their situation was permanent, is the greatest sustained act of prophetic energizing in the canon. "Do not remember the former things, or consider the things of old. I am about to do a new thing; now it springs forth, do you not perceive it?" (Isaiah 43:18-19). The prophet does not offer a program. The prophet offers a vision — a picture of the world as God intends it, concrete enough to be believed and different enough to require transformation.

This is where the church is most urgently needed and least consistently present. The dominant culture offers its own visions of the future — technological progress, economic growth, national strength — and those visions are powerful not because they are true but because they are specific and vivid. The church counters with generalities about heaven and spiritual flourishing that are not specific enough to interrupt the dominant vision, not concrete enough to compete with it, not costly enough to constitute a genuine alternative.

The prophetic imagination is the imagination trained to see the specific ways in which the kingdom of God contradicts the kingdoms of this age, and to name that contradiction in terms concrete enough to act on. Not "justice matters" but "the people working in your city's chicken processing plant are being paid wages that cannot sustain a family, and the imago Dei in them makes that a theological crisis." Not "we should care for the poor" but "the gap between the resources represented in your congregation and the poverty three miles from this building is not incidental to the gospel — it is the question the gospel is addressing."

The steel-man of the conservative evangelical response is worth hearing honestly. That tradition argues that the prophetic imagination has been captured by left-leaning politics and that what is presented as prophetic vision is actually a particular political program dressed in theological language. The prophets are invoked to legitimate preferred policies. The result is not genuine prophetic witness but the left-wing version of the same royal consciousness Brueggemann critiques — a God who legitimizes a particular political arrangement, who endorses particular economic policies, who is invoked in the service of partisan agendas rather than the genuine alternative of the kingdom.

This critique has real force. The prophetic tradition can be and has been co-opted. The solution is not to retreat from prophetic imagination but to deepen it — to be more faithful to the actual prophets, who were not partisan and not safe, who afflicted the comfortable and comforted the afflicted without regard to which side those categories put them on politically. Amos condemned both the wealthy women of Samaria (Amos 4:1) and the corrupt judges who perverted justice in the gate (Amos 5:10-12). Isaiah condemned both the political alliances that substituted for trust in God and the religion that substituted liturgy for justice (Isaiah 1:13-17). The prophets are available for co-option because they are sharp and uncomfortable — but their sharpness cuts in all directions, and the prophetic community that takes them seriously will find its own practices challenged before it turns to anyone else's.

The cultivation of prophetic imagination in a congregation begins with specific practices. The reading of lament psalms in corporate worship. The naming, from the pulpit, of specific conditions of injustice that affect specific people connected to this community. The regular encounter with people whose lives contradict the comfortable assumptions of the congregation — inviting those people to speak, not as illustrations, but as members of the same body with something the congregation needs to hear.

It requires the pastor who is willing to be wrong in public — who will name what he sees and say what he thinks it requires and then be corrected by the community and by deeper reading of the text and by the evidence of what actually happens. The prophets were not always right about the details of what God was doing. They were faithful to the vision they had been given, and faithfulness to a genuine vision is more valuable to the community than competence at delivering comfortable presentations.

The prophetic imagination, finally, is not pessimistic. This is the misunderstanding that most consistently neutralizes it. The prophetic tradition is not the tradition of the people who see everything wrong. It is the tradition of the people who see so clearly what is possible that what is current becomes intolerable. Grief is the beginning, not the end. The end is what Isaiah 65 describes — no infant who lives only a few days, no old person who does not live out their years, people building houses and inhabiting them, planting vineyards and eating their fruit (Isaiah 65:20-21). The vision is material, specific, embodied. It is a world where the basic conditions of human flourishing are not denied to anyone by the arrangements of the powerful.

That world has not arrived. It is arriving. The church that holds both of those things at once — the grief that it has not yet arrived and the conviction that it is coming — is the church that lives prophetically.

That church is dangerous to the dominant consciousness.

That is exactly what it ought to be.

The prophetic imagination is not a temperament. Some people are more naturally inclined toward it than others, but Jeremiah, who wept through his entire prophetic career, was not a naturally combative personality. He did not want the calling he received. He tried to refuse it (Jeremiah 1:6). The imagination that seized him was not his own preference. It was the consequence of genuine encounter with the God whose vision for the world cannot coexist with the world's complacent self-assessment.

The church that cultivates prophetic imagination is the church that puts itself regularly in the presence of that God — not the God who endorses the status quo, not the God who is happy with our current arrangements, but the God who asks Ezekiel's valley of dry bones whether those bones can live (Ezekiel 37:3) and means the question seriously. Can these institutions live? Can these relationships live? Can this congregation be more than a gathering of people who happen to share a liturgy?

The prophetic answer is: yes. But not as they are. Not without the breath of God. Not without someone willing to prophesy over the dry bones until the breath enters them.

That is the pastor's work. It is not comfortable. It is necessary.
