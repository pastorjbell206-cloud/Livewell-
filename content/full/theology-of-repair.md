The Hebrew prophets did not speak primarily about preventing future wrongdoing. They spoke about reckoning with what had already been done. Amos does not say: let us create conditions where injustice is less likely. He says: let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream (Amos 5:24). The image is not a committee. It is a flood.

We have reduced justice to prevention when the tradition demands repair.

The word the prophets use most often for what God requires is not "reform." It is restoration — the return of what was taken, the healing of what was broken, the re-establishment of relationship where relationship was severed. This distinction matters because prevention and repair are not the same moral claim. Prevention says: do not do this again. Repair says: account for what was done. The first lets the past remain in the past. The second refuses to let the past be past until it has been addressed.

## The Covenant Structure of Repair

The Jubilee laws in Leviticus 25 are perhaps the most radical economic legislation in the ancient world, and they are built around the logic of repair rather than prevention. Every fifty years, land returned to its original families (Leviticus 25:13). Debt slaves were released (Leviticus 25:40–41). Accumulated economic advantage was reset. The text is explicit about the theological basis: "The land is mine," God says, "and you are but aliens and my tenants" (Leviticus 25:23). No permanent dispossession is possible in a system built on that premise, because the foundation of ownership is always conditional on the covenant, and the covenant insists that what the poor need cannot simply become permanent wealth in the hands of those who took it from them.

The Jubilee was not aspirational. It was calendrical — written into the liturgical structure of Israel's life, meant to recur with the same regularity as Sabbath and harvest festivals. Whether it was ever fully practiced is a matter of historical debate [cite — confirm]. That it was commanded — that it was inscribed in the covenant as the baseline expectation of a faithful community — is the theological claim. The God who commands the Jubilee is not satisfied with individuals who are personally generous toward the poor. He requires systems that do not permanently concentrate advantage. He requires that the damage of dispossession not become permanent simply because it becomes old.

The prophets understood this. When Micah condemns those who "covet fields and seize them, and houses and take them away" (Micah 2:2), he is not primarily warning about future coveting. He is indicting the accumulated result of past coveting — the parcels already seized, the families already displaced, the ancestral inheritance already in someone else's possession. The indictment points backward. The required remedy points forward but must account for what has happened.

## What Augustine and Aquinas Knew

The Christian theological tradition has resources here that it has largely failed to deploy. Augustine's doctrine of sin includes not just the acts we commit but the disorder those acts create in the world — a disorder that does not resolve on its own because sin damages the structures in which human life is embedded, not merely the individual hearts that chose to sin [cite — confirm]. The world is disordered by our sin. The sin can be forgiven without the disorder being repaired, and a forgiveness that only addresses the sin while leaving the disorder intact is not the full restoration the gospel envisions.

Thomas Aquinas argued, in his discussion of commutative justice in the *Summa Theologiae* [cite — confirm], that restitution is a precondition of genuine justice — that having wronged someone creates an obligation to repair the wrong, not merely to stop doing it, and that this obligation is not discharged by repentance alone. The grace that forgives the sinner does not automatically restore what the sinner took. These are related but distinct. Forgiveness addresses the relationship between the wrongdoer and God. Restitution addresses the material damage done to the wronged person. To conflate them is to offer forgiveness as a substitute for repair rather than as the foundation of it.

Luther preached grace freely given, and grace is freely given — nothing I am arguing here disputes that. But Luther's tradition also produced in the same century a rigorous Lutheran social ethic under figures like Johannes Bugenhagen [cite — confirm], who worked to establish systematic care for the poor in Lutheran cities precisely because the forgiveness of individual sins did not automatically resolve the social conditions that left the poor poor. The cross forgives the sinner. The community of the cross is supposed to repair the damage.

## The American Church's Evasion

I have preached about justice for fifteen years, and most of what I preached was about stopping harm. I preached against racism and I preached against poverty and I did not preach about what is owed to those who have already suffered under both for generations. I named the injustice. I did not name the debt. Those are not the same sermon.

The American church has repeatedly confused forgiveness with repair. We have told survivors of abuse: God forgives your abuser, and closed the door on the question of what the abuser owes. We have told communities fractured by decades of redlining and deliberate economic exclusion that God loves everyone equally, and then wondered why that message lands as an insult. It lands as an insult because it is incomplete. Divine love without divine restitution is not the full gospel. It is half of it, strategically selected to comfort the people who have the most to lose from full accountability.

The history is specific. Between 1934 and 1968, the Federal Housing Administration's redlining policies systematically denied mortgage loans in Black and minority neighborhoods while subsidizing homeownership in white suburbs [cite — confirm]. The effect was to prevent an entire generation of Black families from building the home equity that constitutes the primary form of wealth accumulation for American households. The families who were excluded did not simply fail to accumulate wealth. Wealth was structurally withheld from them by government policy, often with the institutional cooperation of banks, real estate agencies, and communities — including many churches that blessed the white flight and the segregated suburbs as God's provision. The wealth gap between white and Black American families that persists today [cite — confirm] is not a natural fact. It is the accumulated compound interest on that structural theft.

To say that God loves everyone equally in response to that history is to offer a truth that, in isolation, functions as a mechanism for avoiding the harder truth: that love without repair is not the love the prophets described.

## The Parable as Economic Narrative

The story of Zacchaeus is instructive here (Luke 19:1–10). When Jesus arrives at his house and the people grumble about him entering the home of a sinner, Zacchaeus stands and makes a declaration: half of what he owns he will give to the poor, and anyone he has defrauded he will repay fourfold. Jesus does not generate this response by delivering a lecture about structural injustice. He generates it by presence — by arriving at the door of the man everyone had agreed to exclude. And what emerges from that encounter is not simply a changed heart. It is a changed ledger.

Salvation came to this house, Jesus says. Not that Zacchaeus understood correct doctrine about the atonement. That salvation came. And salvation, in this house, looked like math. It looked like fourfold restitution to anyone who had been defrauded. The specific number is significant: it is the number the Mosaic law required for the theft of livestock (Exodus 22:1). Zacchaeus is applying the Torah's logic of repair to his own life, and Jesus names it as salvation. Not as a supplement to salvation. As salvation.

## What Repair Actually Requires

The theology of repair is not primarily a political program, and I am not arguing for a single policy position. I am arguing that the church must have the theological vocabulary for this conversation — that when we arrive at the hard questions of what repair looks like, we arrive with the full tradition, not just the convenient half.

The truth-and-reconciliation process that South Africa attempted after the end of apartheid, beginning in 1995, was not perfect [cite — confirm]. Many of its limitations were the result of insufficient commitment to the repair dimension — the process produced testimony and some limited amnesty but did not produce the structural economic repair that the full tradition demands. Archbishop Desmond Tutu, who chaired the commission, was clear that reconciliation without the restoration of dignity and material loss was an inadequate substitute for justice [cite — confirm]. His clarity came from a theological tradition shaped by the prophets who knew the difference between stopping harm and repairing it.

The God who requires mishpat and tsedaqah is not satisfied with gestures. He is satisfied with justice, which is to say: with the world made more fully right than it was. That is a higher bar than we have typically set for ourselves. It is also the bar the full tradition sets, and there is no credible way to preach the prophets while quietly setting it aside.

I have preached about the damage. I have not always preached about the debt. That difference is not small. It is the difference between a gospel that confronts the powerful and a gospel that consoles them.

The damage is older than we are. That does not make it ours to ignore.

We are the generation standing in the rubble. The question is not whether we caused all of it. The question is what we do now that we are here.
