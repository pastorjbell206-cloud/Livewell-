The staff member every pastor worries about is easy to identify. The one with the bad attitude, the one who complains in the parking lot, the one whose theology is drifting, the one whose behavior is already a problem. These people are visible. They can be addressed directly, or if necessary, they can be released. They are not the most dangerous person on a church staff.

The most dangerous person is someone else entirely.

The most dangerous person is the one who is visibly gifted, who the congregation loves, who has been there long enough to carry institutional memory, and who has quietly understood that their longevity and popularity make them difficult to hold accountable. This person does not act out. They do not create scenes. They simply do not receive correction. They deflect it, contextualize it, agree with it in the room and ignore it outside the room. And because they are gifted and beloved, the pastor learns over time that pushing the accountability creates more disruption than the original problem — so he stops pushing. The behavior continues. The culture absorbs it as normal. And over time, the message sent to every other staff member is that there are people here who are not held to the same standard as everyone else.

That message is corrosive. It poisons everything it touches.

---

Samuel's sons Joel and Abijah are a minor reference in the Old Testament narrative — two verses in 1 Samuel 8:3 — but the description is striking: they were appointed to judge Israel and they perverted justice for personal gain. Their position gave them cover. The institution that elevated them also, for a time, protected them from the consequences of what they were doing. The damage accumulated quietly until it could no longer be ignored — until the elders of Israel came to Samuel and said, "Your sons do not walk in your ways" (1 Samuel 8:5), and the request for a king was, in part, a response to the failure of unaccountable leadership that had been allowed to continue for too long.

What happened to Samuel's sons is not primarily about theological failure. It is about structural failure. They had been placed in a role that came with authority and no accountability. The position itself had made them dangerous.

This is the structural diagnosis that most pastoral conversations about difficult staff members miss. We look for the bad actor — the person who entered the staff with wrong motives, whose character was always going to be a problem. Sometimes that person exists. More often, what we are looking at is a person whose character was shaped, over time, by the accountability-free zone they were allowed to inhabit. People grow into the conditions their environments create. A person who is never corrected comes to believe, eventually, that correction does not apply to them. That is not original sin in an unusual form. That is original sin responding normally to an abnormal environment.

---

I have made this error. I kept a person in a role too long because their gifts were real and the congregation valued what they produced, and I kept telling myself that the accountability issues were manageable — that the culture wasn't really being damaged, that I was reading it correctly, that the cost of action was higher than the cost of inaction. I was wrong on every count.

The cost of inaction always arrives eventually. It arrives in the staff member who finally says out loud what everyone has known for two years: that there are two sets of rules here. It arrives in the quiet departure of people who were paying attention and drew the correct conclusion about what the culture actually values. It arrives in the leader who realizes, too late, that the culture he wanted is not the culture he actually built — that the vision statement is one thing and the daily practice is another, and the people on the ground have been watching the practice, not the statement.

The gifted, unaccountable staff member is dangerous not because of what they do directly, but because of what their presence communicates. They are a living announcement that the accountability structure has exceptions. And once that exception is established, everyone on the staff adjusts their behavior to account for it — either by quietly resenting it or by quietly testing whether they too can find the edges of accountability.

---

David Augsburger, in his work on pastoral care across cultural contexts, notes that one of the most powerful teachers in any community is not the stated value but the tolerated behavior [cite — confirm]. What a community actually teaches is not what it prints in its value statements. It is what it accepts without comment when the chips are on the table. The staff culture that tolerates exceptional behavior in gifted people is teaching, daily, that giftedness is the trump card. That what you can do matters more than how you do it. That the community's stated values are aspirational for the ordinary members and optional for the exceptional ones.

This is the teaching that damages the culture most deeply. Not any individual behavior, but the structure it reveals: that accountability is for the people who need it, and the people who produce enough results can operate outside it.

Jesus addressed a version of this among the disciples themselves. When James and John's mother asked for her sons to sit at Jesus's right and left hand (Matthew 20:21), she was asking for the exceptional position — the role that puts the gifted performers above the normal accountability structures of the community. Jesus's response was not primarily about organizational structure. It was theological: "Whoever would be great among you must be your servant, and whoever would be first among you must be your slave, even as the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." (Matthew 20:26-28) The measure of greatness in the community of Jesus is not performance. It is service. And service is subject to accountability — it is, by definition, oriented toward others rather than above others.

The community that has organized itself around the exceptional performer has, often without knowing it, inverted this. It has placed performance above service, productivity above character, gift above accountability.

---

The counter-position deserves honest engagement. It goes like this: exceptional gifts require unusual latitude. The pastor who holds an unusually gifted staff member to the same standards as an average performer will eventually drive that person away, and the cost of their departure will be real and visible to the congregation. Ministry is not a bureaucracy. The rules that govern ordinary institutions cannot be applied uniformly to communities of calling where some people are genuinely operating at a different level. Some people need more room to move.

There is something to this. The creative, prophetically gifted, unusually productive person often does require more latitude than a person of ordinary gifts. Their workflows are different. Their timelines are different. Their ways of processing accountability may be different. Rigid enforcement of uniform standards can suppress exactly the gifts that make a ministry exceptional.

But there is a line between latitude and exception. Latitude means adjusting how accountability is applied. Exception means suspending whether it applies. Latitude means working with a gifted person to find the form of accountability that actually shapes them rather than the form that simply passes the institution's documentation requirements. Exception means concluding that the person's gifts are so valuable that the accountability conversation itself is not worth having.

The first produces a healthy relationship with a gifted person. The second produces the most dangerous person on the staff.

---

The path forward is not punitive. It is not a campaign to identify and remove the people who have been allowed to operate above accountability. Most of the time, the person who has been allowed to operate this way did not set out to do so — they were simply responding to the conditions that the pastoral leadership created.

The path forward begins with the pastor examining what he has allowed and why. Which relationships on the staff are actually characterized by accountability, and which are characterized by the appearance of accountability? Where has he avoided the hard conversation because the gifted person's production is more immediately valued than the culture's health? What has that avoidance communicated to everyone who was watching?

Those questions have answers. They are uncomfortable ones. I have sat with them. The answers do not make you feel better about yourself as a leader. They make you more honest, and more honest is the only direction that produces real change.

Paul writes to Timothy that the elders who persist in sin should be rebuked "in the presence of all, so that the rest may stand in fear" (1 Timothy 5:20). The pastoral letter is not recommending public humiliation. It is recommending public accountability — the kind that makes visible what accountability actually looks like in the community, so that the exception does not become the standard.

The most dangerous person on a church staff is not the obvious problem. It is the unaccountable high performer. Every staff culture will eventually be shaped by what it tolerates in its most protected members.

Hold everyone to the same standard. The gifted especially.

The congregation is watching whether you believe your own values.

They already know whether you do.
