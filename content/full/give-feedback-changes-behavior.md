Most feedback conversations accomplish one thing: they make the person giving the feedback feel that they have discharged an obligation. The conversation happens. The words are spoken. The file is documented. And the person receiving the feedback leaves the meeting confused, defensive, or privately unconvinced — and changes nothing.

I have given feedback this way. I have sat across from a staff member or a volunteer leader and said the right things in the right sequence and watched the person nod in all the right places and known, even as it was happening, that nothing would change. Not because the person was resistant, but because I had said what I needed to say rather than what they needed to hear.

This is not a small distinction. Most pastoral leaders are better at the former than the latter, for reasons that go deeper than skill.

The pastoral tradition has a complicated relationship with correction. On one side is the prophetic heritage — Amos confronting Israel's injustice, Nathan confronting David, Paul writing to the Corinthians that he will come with a rod if they do not deal with the man living with his father's wife (1 Corinthians 5:3-5). The prophetic voice does not equivocate. It names the thing. On the other side is a pastoral temperament that bends toward mercy — toward wanting to protect people from pain, toward softening the hard edge, toward a kind of relational warmth that sometimes prevents rather than enables honest speech. Most pastors are, by constitution, more comfortable with the second than the first. The result is feedback that keeps the peace and changes nothing.

The church inherited its understanding of correction primarily from Paul, who modeled a complexity that is hard to imitate. When Paul writes to the Corinthians in 2 Corinthians 7:8-9, he says he does not regret the painful letter he wrote them, because it produced genuine repentance. The goal was not to wound. The goal was metanoia — the turning that leads to life. Paul's standard for whether correction was successful was not whether the person felt better afterward. It was whether they changed. That is a harder standard than most of us hold ourselves to.

The first failure of ineffective feedback is that it is abstract. "Your communication needs to improve." "You are not as present in meetings as you should be." These statements may be accurate. They do not tell the person what to actually do differently on Tuesday morning. Specific behavior is the only addressable unit of change. The feedback that changes anything is feedback that is concrete enough to act on: "When you send the meeting agenda the night before, people come unprepared. Send it by Thursday afternoon and watch what changes." The pastor who cannot name a specific, recent, observable behavior when giving feedback is not giving feedback. He is giving a general impression, and general impressions produce defensiveness, not growth.

The second failure is that it arrives too late. Feedback delivered six weeks after the event it is responding to is not feedback. It is a record. The person being addressed has no access to the emotional and contextual reality that the feedback is responding to — it has faded, reinterpreted itself, been processed in a dozen different directions. The feedback that has the best chance of changing behavior is feedback given close enough to the event that the person can re-enter it, can access the specific situation, can actually imagine doing it differently.

Proverbs has a great deal to say about the relationship between words and their timing. "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in a setting of silver" (Proverbs 25:11). The word fitly spoken is the right word in the right moment — not just accurate in content but appropriate in timing and delivery. Timing feedback well requires the pastor to name what he observed in something close to real time — not necessarily in the moment, because some observations require reflection before they are ready to be shared, but close enough that the moment is still alive.

The third failure, and the one most costly to correct, is that the feedback lacks genuine care for the person receiving it. Feedback delivered as organizational maintenance — you are not meeting the job description, these are the requirements, this is being documented — communicates that the person is a function, not a person. Human beings do not change their behavior significantly in response to being treated as functions. They comply minimally and protect themselves. Behavior changes in response to someone who clearly cares about their actual growth and is willing to tell them hard things precisely because they believe those hard things will help.

Paul's counsel in Galatians 6:1 — "Brothers and sisters, if someone is caught in a sin, you who live by the Spirit should restore that person gently" — does not describe performance management. It describes pastoral intervention. The word Paul uses for *restore* — *katartizō* — is the word used for setting a broken bone or repairing a torn net. Painful, but aimed at making the thing whole and functional again. The gentleness is not softness. It is the posture of someone who is in the work for the other person's sake, not for the sake of institutional compliance.

There is a steel-man worth hearing here. Some pastoral leaders, taking the above seriously, make the opposite error: they become so focused on care and relationship that they cannot be direct. They circle the issue endlessly. They soften every hard truth until it no longer carries any edge. They confuse pastoral warmth with the avoidance of anything painful. This is not care — it is its counterfeit. The person who leaves the meeting not knowing that their leader has a serious concern about their performance has not been cared for. They have been managed. And they will eventually discover the problem, under worse circumstances, with less time to respond.

Direct and caring are not opposites. The most effective feedback I have ever witnessed held both without compromising either. It named the specific behavior, named its specific impact, named the expectation going forward, and then asked the person what support they needed to meet it. The care was not in the softening of the correction. The care was in treating the person as someone capable of change and worth the investment of honest speech.

The pastoral leader who wants to give feedback that actually changes behavior must practice three things.

First, the discipline of specificity. After every difficult observation, before the conversation: what specifically did you see, when did you see it, what specifically would different look like? If you cannot answer those questions concretely, you are not ready to speak.

Second, the discipline of proximity. Do not accumulate. Deal with what you observe in something close to real time. The conversation held thirty days after the event is helping no one. The conversation held forty-eight hours after the event, after you have slept on it and thought clearly about what you want to say, can change something.

Third, the discipline of investment. Ask yourself honestly: do I actually want this person to succeed? If the answer is yes, that investment will be audible. If the answer is no — if you are feeding a paper trail toward a termination decision rather than genuinely investing in someone's growth — then what you are doing is not feedback. It is documentation. Documentation has its place. It is not pastoral work.

The hardest feedback I have ever had to give was to someone I genuinely loved and whose failure was going to cost both of us something real. I gave it badly the first time — too much care, not enough directness. The message did not land. I gave it again, more plainly. The person heard it that time. It was not comfortable. It was not designed to be comfortable.

The goal was not comfort. The goal was restoration.

That is usually how genuine pastoral correction works. You do not get credit for the attempt. You do not get credit for your intentions. You get the result, or you do not. And whether you get the result depends on whether you were willing to be both direct enough to actually name the thing and caring enough that the person knew you were on their side.

Most of us find one of those easier than the other. The work is to find both in the same conversation.

There is a fourth failure that sits underneath the other three and rarely gets named: the leader who gives feedback primarily to protect himself. He speaks so that later he can say he spoke. He documents so that the termination is defensible. He delivers the message at the time and in the form that is most comfortable for him, not most useful for the person receiving it. This is not pastoral leadership. This is institutional self-protection dressed in the language of accountability.

The pastoral leader who wants to be taken seriously on this issue has to examine his own motives before he opens his mouth. Is the goal this person's growth? Or is the goal your record? Both may be legitimate in the end, but they require different conversations. The conversation aimed at growth treats the person as someone with capacity and worth and a future. The conversation aimed at documentation treats the person as a risk to be managed.

People know the difference. They have always known the difference. The conversation that treats them as capable of growth will be received as hard but caring. The conversation that treats them as a liability problem will be received as the preliminary to a firing, whether that is true or not.

Begin with the assumption that the person can change. Deliver the feedback accordingly. Then see what happens.
