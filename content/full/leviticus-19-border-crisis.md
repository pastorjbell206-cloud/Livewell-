The text is not ambiguous. It never was.

Leviticus 19:33-34: "When a stranger sojourns with you in your land, you shall not do him wrong. You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt: I am the Lord your God."

That last phrase — I am the Lord your God — is not ornamental. It appears throughout Leviticus 19 as a divine signature, an assertion of authority. When the Lord signs a command with his own name, he is not offering a suggestion. He is binding the covenant community to a standard inseparable from who he is. The treatment of the stranger is not a secondary application of the law. It is a direct expression of the character of the God who gave it.

The church that has spent recent years debating the ethics of immigration while treating Leviticus 19 as a historical curiosity has made a theological error of the first order.

## The Memory at the Center

The command does not stand alone. It is rooted in a specific historical memory: you were strangers in the land of Egypt. This is not analogy. This is the mechanism of the command. Israel is commanded to protect the stranger because Israel knows from inside what it means to be a stranger — to be powerless, to be subject to the decisions of a host society that can exploit or expel at will, to have no legal standing, no advocate, no protection except whatever the powerful choose to extend.

God took note of what Egypt did to Israel in that condition. God intervened. The entire narrative of Exodus — the deliverance, the wilderness, the law at Sinai — flows from that intervention. And then God commands: because you know what that is, because you were in that place, you will not be Egypt to the stranger in your land.

The Hebrew word for stranger in these texts is *ger* — a person living outside their native land, resident among a foreign people. The ger had no tribal belonging, no family network, no inherited claim on the land's resources. The law protected the ger explicitly and repeatedly because the ger was structurally without protection. Leviticus 19, Deuteronomy 10, Deuteronomy 24, Exodus 22 — the command recurs so often that its repetition is itself a theological statement. God keeps returning to it because Israel kept needing to be reminded. The pressure to treat the stranger as less than the native was always present. The command against it was equally persistent.

The American church exists in a country whose founding mythology borrowed heavily from the Exodus. Puritan settlers identified themselves explicitly with Israel fleeing Egypt, with the Atlantic as their Red Sea, with the New World as Canaan. That metaphor has been deployed again and again in American religious history. But if the church has borrowed that metaphor, it has also inherited the command at its center. You do not get to be Israel without being obligated by what it means to have been a stranger.

## What the Law Encoded

Leviticus 19 is remarkable for how much it encodes in one chapter. Within forty-six verses, God commands the leaving of field edges for the poor and stranger (verse 9-10), honest weights and measures (verse 35-36), love of neighbor as oneself (verse 18), and love of the stranger as oneself (verse 33-34). The chapter treats care for the vulnerable not as a supplement to religious practice but as its core substance. The commands come interspersed with the divine signature, binding each to God's own identity.

The prophets understood this. Jeremiah 22:3 summarizes the prophetic demand: "Do justice and righteousness, and deliver from the hand of the oppressor him who has been robbed. And do no wrong or violence to the resident alien, the fatherless, and the widow." The resident alien appears again. He always appears. In Ezekiel 22, among the catalog of Jerusalem's sins that have brought judgment, one of the charges is: "The stranger living among you suffers extortion" (verse 29). Not an afterthought. Evidence for the prosecution.

Zechariah 7:10, Malachi 3:5, Isaiah 1:17 — the prophetic witness is consistent. The treatment of the stranger is not one justice concern among many equal concerns. It is a recurring, foregrounded test of covenant faithfulness. God cares about this with the intensity of someone who was once in the stranger's position and has not forgotten it.

## What Every Crisis Reveals

Every border crisis — and this one is not the first — reveals what the church actually believes about the stranger, as opposed to what it preaches about him.

The debate around immigration policy involves genuine complexity. Nations have responsibilities for order and security that are not illegitimate. A careful reader can hold those concerns alongside the commands of Leviticus 19 and does not have to choose between them entirely. I am not arguing that open borders are the obvious and only faithful policy position, or that every element of border enforcement is categorically wrong.

What is not defensible is the silence. What is not defensible is the church that has weeks of internal discussion about worship style, budget priorities, and children's programming, and has had no serious conversation about the families separated at the border, the asylum seekers in detention, the undocumented worker in the congregation who is afraid of what the next executive order means for her children. The silence is a position. It is just a position we have not been honest enough to name.

I have been part of congregations that practiced that silence. I have contributed to it. The deacon meeting where someone raised the question and the room went very quiet, and we moved on to the next agenda item — I was in that room. That silence is not neutrality. It is a choice about whose discomfort matters more.

## Two Evasions That Fail

I have watched the American evangelical church, in recent years, perform elaborate theological gymnastics to avoid the plain reading of texts like Leviticus 19. The gymnastics usually take one of two forms.

The first is dispensationalism applied to ethics: that was Israel's theocratic law, it does not apply to a secular nation-state, we live in a different covenant era. This move fails for at least two reasons. First, the New Testament does not abandon the logic of Leviticus 19. Jesus quotes Leviticus 19:18 — "love your neighbor as yourself" — as one of the two greatest commandments (Matthew 22:39). The parable of the Good Samaritan expands the definition of neighbor to include precisely the person the religious establishment had decided to walk past. The stranger is still in the text. The command has not been retired. Second, the principle underlying the command — you were once powerless, therefore you will not exploit the powerless — is not a theocratic novelty. It is a moral logic rooted in the character of God that runs from Genesis to Revelation.

The second evasion is the separation of justice and politics: I care about the immigrant as a person, but immigration policy is a political question and the church should not be political. This fails because the prophets were relentlessly political in the specific sense that they named what political and economic systems were doing to the poor and the stranger. Avoiding politics was not an option available to Amos or Isaiah or Jeremiah. When Amos denounced the merchants who could not wait for the Sabbath to end so they could get back to cheating the poor with false scales (Amos 8:5), he was making a political claim about economic practice. When Isaiah condemned those who "join house to house" and "add field to field" until they alone inhabit the land (Isaiah 5:8), he was addressing a policy of land consolidation. The prophets did not have a category for prophetic witness that was non-political in its application.

The church that separates its love for the immigrant person from its engagement with the systems that determine how that person is treated has made a theological choice to operate at the level of charity rather than justice. Charity is good. It is insufficient.

## The Word That Remains

Love him as yourself. Not love him as you would a distant beneficiary of your charitable giving. Not love him as you would a mission project with clear boundaries and funding cycles. As yourself.

That standard is demanding. It requires asking what I would want, what I would need, what I would be owed, if I were in his position. It means asking those questions about the man who fled violence in Central America with his family, who crossed a border because the legal channels had been closed or made inaccessible, who is now living in my city, working, attending my church or one near it, afraid every time a vehicle moves slowly past. What would I want, in that position?

The answer to that question does not resolve every immigration policy debate. Reasonable people applying the same Scripture can disagree about the mechanisms of border management, the structure of asylum processes, the pathways to legal status. But it tells me where to start. It tells me that silence in the face of his vulnerability is not an option Leviticus 19 leaves open. It tells me that the community God gave his name to protect him has an obligation that precedes and exceeds every policy preference.

I grew up without a father to advocate for me. I know something about what it means to be without protection in a social context that is organized around the assumption of protection you do not have. I did not have to cross a border to know that. I grew up speaking English in Michigan. The man at the border has less than I had. The command reaches him. It reaches me.

We were strangers once. We were not forgotten.

The command is: do not forget.
