The word the New Testament uses for the church — *ekklesia* — means "those called out." Not called out into withdrawal. Called out from a previous set of allegiances, a previous way of organizing your life, a previous set of answers to the question of what you ultimately serve.

That calling is not primarily about behavior modification, though it produces behavioral changes. It is about a reordering of loves — what Augustine called the *ordo amoris*, the right ordering of what you love most. When that ordering shifts, everything else shifts with it. You no longer make decisions from the same center. You no longer evaluate the same things as ultimate. The culture around you will notice.

And when it notices, you have a choice.

The pressure to conform is not new. The Christians in Rome in the first century lived in a culture saturated with imperial theology — the claim that Caesar was lord, that the empire's flourishing was the measure of all things, that participation in the civic religion was the price of belonging. The early church's refusal of that participation was not experienced as mere religious preference. It was experienced as subversion. "Christians to the lions" was not a cultural slogan against people with unusual private spiritual beliefs. It was a slogan against people whose private spiritual beliefs produced public non-conformity, and the public non-conformity was felt as a threat.

The threat was accurate. The gospel does threaten. Not with violence — the early church did not wield that. But with an alternative account of reality that, if believed, reorganizes everything the surrounding culture depends on remaining unquestioned.

The Roman economy depended on the moral legitimacy of slavery. The early church was proclaiming that in Christ there is neither slave nor free (Galatians 3:28) — a claim that did not immediately abolish slavery but that, carried consistently, would make it impossible to treat the slave as less than a full member of the human community. The Roman social order depended on the absolute authority of the *paterfamilias*, the male head of household whose power over wife, children, and slaves was virtually unlimited. The early church was preaching that husbands should love their wives as Christ loved the church — self-giving rather than dominating (Ephesians 5:25), a genuinely counter-cultural instruction in that context. The Roman imperial cult required at minimum the performance of acknowledgment that Caesar was lord. The church said: there is one Lord, and it is not Caesar.

These were not minor theological quibbles. They were challenges to the organizing principles of the social order, and the social order responded accordingly.

Rodney Stark's analysis of early Christianity's growth argues that much of it was driven precisely by the church's counter-cultural practices — the care for the sick and dying during urban epidemics, the refusal to expose unwanted infants, the welcome of women and slaves into full community membership [cite — confirm]. People noticed. Some of what they noticed, they found attractive, even subversive in a way that appealed to those the social order had positioned at the bottom. Some of what they noticed, they found threatening. Both responses were appropriate. The gospel was producing both effects because it was genuinely different.

This is where the church in post-Christian America faces a particular temptation. We inherited the social form of cultural Christianity — the assumptions, the language, the institutional presence — without always inheriting the content that made it distinctive. We kept the form. The form is now dissolving. And the question is whether, in the dissolution, we will rediscover the content that actually gives us something to say, or whether we will spend our remaining social capital trying to restore the form.

The temptation is to make the gospel acceptable. To translate it into a register that the surrounding culture finds agreeable — therapeutic, affirming, life-improving, broadly compatible with the values of upper-middle-class American aspiration. A Christianity that improves your marriage without asking you to lay down your preferences. A Christianity that offers community without asking you to carry anyone's burden. A Christianity that speaks about purpose without speaking about the cross.

I have been that pastor. For years I wanted a Christianity that could be presented as continuous with the aspirations of mainstream American culture. A Christianity that did not ask too much. What I discovered, slowly, was that a Christianity that does not ask too much also does not offer too much. It offers adjustment, not liberation. It offers improvement, not resurrection. It helps people become better versions of themselves rather than something genuinely new.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer called it "cheap grace" — the preaching of forgiveness without repentance, the proclamation of community without the cost of community, the announcement of salvation that asks nothing and produces nothing [cite — confirm]. Cheap grace is not a minor error. Bonhoeffer understood it as the deadly enemy of the church precisely because it inoculates against the real thing. The person who has received cheap grace is protected against the recognition that she needs something more costly.

The counter-position worth taking seriously: the church that constantly emphasizes its distinctiveness risks becoming self-congratulatory — a community whose identity is organized around being not-like-the-world, which is a form of self-reference rather than a witness to God. The Pharisees were distinct. They wore it publicly. Jesus was not impressed. Distinctiveness is not, in itself, a virtue. The question is what you are being different *for*, not merely different *from*.

This is exactly right. The difference is not the point. The difference is the consequence. When you actually believe what Christianity claims — that the Lord of history was executed and raised and that this changes what is possible for every human being — you will end up different from the ambient culture not because you tried to be, but because the gospel does not leave you where it found you.

The content — the actual, costly, countercultural claims of the gospel — is what the world around us has no reason to expect and every reason to be surprised by. A community that holds its possessions loosely in a culture of accumulation. A community that welcomes people the world discards, in a culture of performance and productivity. A community where rank is expressed as service rather than as privilege, in a culture where status is the primary currency. A community where forgiveness is not naive but is offered precisely because we have received something we did not earn, in a culture where vengeance is experienced as justice.

These practices are strange. They are meant to be strange. They are not strategies for growth. They are the logical outworking of believing what the gospel actually says.

James K.A. Smith argues that the practices of the church form its members in ways that run deeper than intellectual conviction — that the Sabbath and the table and the prayer and the community shape the desires and instincts of those who inhabit them [cite — confirm]. The courage the gospel requires is not primarily the courage to confront culture publicly, though it sometimes includes that. It is first the courage to be formed differently — to let those practices actually shape you into someone whose instincts no longer match the default settings. That formation is slow. It is unglamorous. It produces people who are genuinely different, not by effort but by nature, and the difference is its own kind of witness.

The church that looks exactly like the culture has nothing to offer the culture. It can only reflect back what was already there.

The church that has been formed differently — that carries the strangeness of the gospel in its actual common life, that surprises people who encounter it, that does things the world cannot explain by reference to any other motive — becomes a sign. An evidence that the world as it is does not have to be the world as it must be.

That sign costs something to bear. It always has. The first-century church paid with its social standing and, in some cases, its members' lives. The contemporary American church will likely pay with its cultural acceptability and its capacity to be broadly agreeable to everyone.

That is a smaller cost. It is still a cost.

The question is whether we love what the gospel promises enough to bear what the gospel requires. The answer is not something we can decide once in a moment of clarity. It is something we decide again every time the comfort of conformity presents itself as the sensible option.

We were called out. The calling does not expire.

The early church did not survive three centuries of intermittent persecution by organizing better campaigns or developing more persuasive arguments. It survived because it was genuinely different in ways that could not be explained by ordinary social dynamics — because it crossed ethnic lines that the Roman world treated as permanent, because it included people the social order had discarded, because it practiced a forgiveness and a generosity that had no parallel in the ambient culture. Tertullian reports the pagans saying of Christians: "See how they love one another" [cite — confirm]. That observation was not a compliment to a good marketing strategy. It was bewilderment at something that had no precedent in the world they knew.

That bewilderment is still available. It is still the most effective witness the church possesses. Not the cleverness of the argument, but the strangeness of the life — the community that actually lives what it says, that surprises people, that cannot be explained by reference to any other organizing principle.

It costs something to produce that kind of bewilderment. It always costs the same thing: the comfort of being broadly agreeable, the ease of looking like everyone else, the social reward of fitting in with the dominant culture. These are real costs. The prosperity gospel promises to eliminate them by promising that faithfulness produces social reward. The actual gospel promises something different: that the one who loses his life for the sake of the kingdom will find it (Matthew 16:25). That is not a promise about social capital. It is a promise about something the social order cannot give and cannot take away.

The courage to be different is, in the end, the courage to believe that what God promises is more valuable than what the culture offers. Not as an abstract theological conviction. As an operative, practical, daily, visible commitment that organizes your actual choices.

The question is not whether we are capable of this. The question is whether we believe it is worth it.
