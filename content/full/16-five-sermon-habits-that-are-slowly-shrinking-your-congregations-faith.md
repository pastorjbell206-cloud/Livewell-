Preaching that genuinely forms disciples — that actually changes how people think, pray, love, and engage the world — is one of the hardest things in ministry to do and one of the easiest to only appear to do. The sermon that sounds good, that generates positive feedback, that leaves the congregation feeling inspired and affirmed, is not necessarily the sermon that is doing the deep work of spiritual formation. And the habits that produce inspiring-but-shallow sermons are often deeply embedded in the preacher's practice long before anyone notices the formation gap.

This article names five of those habits directly — not to condemn but to surface, because the first step toward better preaching is honest recognition of what the current preaching is actually doing.

---

**Habit 1: Preaching to the Felt Need Without the Deeper Need**

Congregations have felt needs — the things they know they want help with. Parenting, marriage, finances, purpose, anxiety. These are real. Preaching that addresses them has genuine pastoral value, and a preacher who is tone-deaf to the actual burdens people carry is not serving the congregation, however theologically sophisticated the sermons may be.

But preaching that consistently *begins and ends* with felt needs tends to produce a congregation that knows how to apply biblical principles to the problems on the surface of their lives while having little formation at depth. The deeper need — for genuine encounter with a holy God, for the transformation of desire itself, for the capacity to suffer faithfully and love generously in the absence of visible reward — is rarely felt acutely until it is urgently needed. And by then, a diet of felt-need preaching has not prepared the congregation for it.

The person who has spent years receiving practical-application sermons about felt needs comes to the crisis of their life — the diagnosis, the grief, the slow collapse of the thing they had built — and finds that their faith, while real, is thin at the places where weight is now being applied. They were formed to handle the problems they expected. They were not formed to handle the problems that come without warning and do not yield to the principles.

The text often begins with felt needs and moves through them to something else — something that reorders the felt need by placing it inside a larger reality. Jesus does not simply address the anxiety of the crowd about food. He uses it to reveal who he is (John 6). Paul does not simply address the Corinthians' conflicts. He moves through them to the mystery of the cross as the subversion of every human hierarchy of wisdom and power (1 Corinthians 1-2). The felt need is the entrance. The preacher's job is to take the congregation where the text actually goes.

---

**Habit 2: Always Resolving the Tension**

Great preaching creates tension before it resolves it. The text creates a problem — about God, about human nature, about the gap between what is and what should be — and the sermon holds that tension long enough for it to do real work before offering resolution.

Many preachers rush to resolution because sitting in homiletical tension is uncomfortable and generates less positive feedback than comfort does. The sermon that leaves the congregation unsettled, that names the problem without offering an easy way through, will receive more ambivalent responses than the sermon that moves efficiently from problem to solution. The preacher who is managing their feedback will develop a systematic bias toward early resolution.

But the sermons that stay with people — that genuinely reshape them — are often the ones that held them in discomfort long enough for something to shift. The lament psalms do not resolve quickly. Psalm 88 does not resolve at all. Job holds the tension for forty-one chapters before God speaks, and when God speaks he does not explain the suffering — he describes himself in terms that make explanation beside the point (Job 38-41). The resolution comes, but it comes after the full weight of the problem has been named and carried, and it is a different kind of resolution than the one Job was asking for.

The congregation that has been trained, week after week, to expect early resolution loses the capacity to hold tension. They become consumers of spiritual comfort rather than people capable of sustained theological engagement. Monday comes, and the comfort of Sunday dissipates, and the discomfort of the actual life reasserts itself, and there is no interior formation to meet it — because the preaching never trained them to sit in it long enough for it to become ground.

---

**Habit 3: Illustrating Everything With Success Stories**

The consistent use of success stories as sermon illustrations — here is someone who applied the principle and it worked out beautifully — creates a systematic distortion in the congregation's theological imagination. It implies that faithful application of biblical truth reliably produces good outcomes in visible, timely ways.

This is not the testimony of Scripture. Abel offered an acceptable sacrifice and was murdered (Genesis 4:8). Jeremiah preached faithfully for decades and watched his nation be destroyed. Stephen preached with full theological authority and was stoned immediately afterward (Acts 7:60). Paul applied the principles of the gospel with extraordinary faithfulness and ended in prison, in chains, and ultimately in execution.

The person in the pew who is applying the principles faithfully and not seeing the promised results begins to wonder what is wrong with them — rather than questioning the implied framework the preaching has built. That is the formation damage. Not doubt in God but doubt in themselves, created by a preaching habit that built an implied prosperity framework one success story at a time.

Include the laments. Include the failures. Include the stories where the faithful thing was done and the outcome was not what was hoped for. That is where most of the congregation actually lives, and they need to know that the gospel has something to say to them there — not as a consolation prize but as the deep thing itself.

---

**Habit 4: Never Preaching Anything That Costs You Something**

The safest sermon to preach is the one everyone agrees with in advance. The most dangerous is the one that names what the congregation would prefer not to name, confronts what they would prefer to leave unconfronted, and calls them to something that genuinely costs.

Most preachers have a mental list of the topics they systematically avoid — the things that would generate pushback, that would make certain powerful members uncomfortable, that would require the harder conversation on Monday. Those avoidances are costing the congregation more than the feedback they spare the preacher.

Ezekiel was told: "Son of man, I have made you a watchman for the house of Israel. Whenever you hear a word from my mouth, you shall give them warning from me. If I say to the wicked, 'You shall surely die,' and you give him no warning… his blood I will require at your hand" (Ezekiel 3:17-18). The watchman's job is not to say what the city wants to hear. It is to say what the city needs to hear, at the cost of the watchman's comfort. That is the office. The preacher who systematically avoids the costly sermon is not being pastoral. They are being negligent — and they have confused the negative feedback they are avoiding with the thing it would have served.

The congregation, whatever they say on Sunday mornings, knows when the pastor is flinching. They can feel the avoidances as surely as they can feel the text being engaged honestly. The flinching builds, over time, a gap between the pulpit and the pew — a sense that the preacher is managing the congregation rather than serving them. The costly sermon, preached from genuine conviction, does more for pastoral trust in one Sunday than a year of comfortable sermons can undo.

---

**Habit 5: Ending Every Sermon With the Same Application Structure**

If your sermons consistently end with three practical steps, or a salvation invitation, or a call to join a small group — regardless of which text was preached — you are training your congregation to anticipate the landing rather than be genuinely surprised by where the text takes them.

The text does not always end in three steps. The Psalms end in lament and doxology and raw, unresolved anguish. The prophets end in judgment and hope and the kind of ambiguity that requires the reader to keep living inside it. Lamentations ends with a question God does not answer (Lamentations 5:22). A preacher who imposes the same application structure on every text has decided, in advance, what shape the sermon should have — and that decision is made before the text is consulted.

The congregation that is trained on consistent structure loses the capacity to receive the sermon that does not fit it. They need to know what to do next. But the formation that actually sustains a life of faith is not the knowledge of what to do next. It is the deep interior capacity — the resilience, the capacity for lament, the ability to sit in unresolved tension and remain faithful — that comes from years of genuine encounter with the full texture of Scripture.

Great preaching is formally responsive to the specific passage. It ends where the text ends. It applies what the text applies. It demands what the text demands. And it trusts the congregation to carry what cannot be neatly resolved — which is most of what the life of faith actually requires.

---

These five habits are not exotic. They are present in most preaching at some level, including mine. The formation gap they create is not dramatic or visible from one Sunday to the next. It accumulates quietly, over years, in the gap between what the congregation knows theologically and what they have been formed to hold in the actual conditions of their lives.

The Pastors Connection Network exists, in part, for exactly this kind of honest conversation — not critique from above but the collegial honesty that good preaching requires and pastoral isolation prevents. These habits are surfaceable. They are changeable. But only after they are seen.

That is what this naming is for. Not condemnation. Recognition. The work that follows it is the preacher's own.
