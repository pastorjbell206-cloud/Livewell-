The crisis facing American Christianity is not primarily numerical. It is not about attendance decline or shrinking affiliation, though both are real. The data matters. But the data is downstream from something older and more theologically precise.

American Christianity is not being persecuted. It is being seduced.

And seduction is far more dangerous than persecution.

Persecution clarifies allegiance. It forces a choice. It strips away ambiguity and reveals who the church actually belongs to, because when the empire demands worship, the Christian who refuses has to know — really know — what they believe. The early church grew under persecution. Tertullian was not wrong: the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the church. [cite — confirm source and exact phrase.] Persecution produces the thing it intends to eliminate.

Seduction does the opposite. Seduction confuses allegiance. Seduction tells you that you can keep Jesus and keep power, keep worship and keep control, keep the cross and keep the sword. Seduction does not ask you to deny Christ. It asks you to bring him along — to make him useful, to let him bless the arrangement, to let the arrangement define what he blesses. It offers the church a seat at the table and calls the seat an opportunity.

We are not watching the church get crushed by the state. We are watching parts of the church crawl into bed with it.

---

The prophets had a word for this. In the book of Hosea, God accuses Israel not of atheism but of adultery. Israel still worshiped. They still sacrificed. They still gathered. The liturgical life was intact. But when fear rose, when Assyria threatened, when political uncertainty came, they ran to foreign alliances for security. They looked to treaties and chariots and kings to secure what God had promised to secure. The Hebrew word Hosea uses is *zanah* — prostitution, the selling of oneself for what one already had but did not trust. God's accusation is not that they stopped believing in him. It is that they stopped trusting him — that they kept the religious language while making the political arrangement, that they said *Lord, Lord* and then sent the envoys to Assyria.

The prophets called that unfaithfulness. The precise term matters. This was not doubt. This was not honest struggle. This was the calculation that God was not enough — that the covenant needed supplementing, that the worship needed backing by something with actual chariots.

Israel did not become Assyrian. It became something worse: a people who carried God's name and Assyria's logic simultaneously, who had lost the capacity to see the difference, and who therefore could not repent of what they could no longer recognize as sin.

---

There is a scene in 1 Kings 18 that has lodged in me for years.

Elijah on Mount Carmel. The prophets of Baal on one side, the people of Israel — who had not converted to Baal worship, who still considered themselves Yahweh's people — standing to watch. Elijah's challenge is sharp: "How long will you go limping between two different opinions? If the LORD is God, follow him; but if Baal, then follow Baal" (1 Kings 18:21). The text notes the response of the people: "The people did not answer him a word."

Not hostility. Not denial. Silence. The silence of people who had convinced themselves that the two loyalties were compatible, that they could manage both, that the question itself was too blunt — that surely the truth was somewhere in the middle of it, that surely God understood the political realities they were navigating.

God was not interested in the middle. Elijah was not interested in the middle. The fire came down, and the middle position was the one thing it did not vindicate.

---

The seduction takes specific forms in American Christianity, and naming them plainly is the point.

It takes the form of the prosperity gospel, which is the oldest temptation dressed in contemporary clothes. The prosperity gospel does not deny Christ. It recruits him — makes him the divine guarantor of the health and wealth the market was already promising, gives religious sanction to the acquisitiveness that the culture had already normalized. It is not hostile to Christianity. It is Christianity made useful to an arrangement Christianity should be interrogating. The people in those pews are not being persecuted for their faith. They are being assured by it — assured that God wants them comfortable, that the present arrangement is what faithfulness looks like, that the sermon should tell them they are doing well.

It takes the form of political identification so complete that the faith becomes a mascot for a movement. When the symbols of Christian faith appear primarily as markers of partisan identity — when the congregation's politics are the predictable output of their zip code and their media diet rather than the counter-cultural demand of the Sermon on the Mount — the seduction is complete. The church is not being dragged away from Christ. Christ is being dragged along as a brand element.

It takes the form of consumer Christianity, in which the congregation is understood as an audience that needs to be satisfied, the pastor as a performer who needs to be engaging, and the Sunday gathering as a product competing for market share against better-produced alternatives. No persecution required. The market is simply more honest about what it is selling than the church has been about what it is supposed to be.

Each of these is seduction, not persecution. Each offers something the human heart already wants — comfort, power, belonging, entertainment — and labels it Christian. None of them asks the believer to lose anything. And that is the tell.

---

Jesus was explicit about this at the outset. "If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me" (Matthew 16:24). Not: follow me and I will supplement your existing life. Not: follow me and the arrangement will work out in your favor. Deny yourself. The Greek word — *aparneomai* — means to disown, to renounce, to say *this is no longer mine*. The self-arrangement. The self-protection. The plan that keeps me at the center.

The cross, in the Roman world, was not an inspirational image. It was a death instrument and an instrument of public shame. Jesus was not saying: carry a burden. He was saying: be willing to be found on the wrong side of the power arrangement, willing to lose the cultural standing, willing to be numbered with the ones the empire disposes of. That is the terms of discipleship. Not aspiration — terms.

The seduced church has not rejected this. It has edited it. It has kept the language of the cross while systematically removing the content — the cost, the shame, the willingness to be wrong by the world's ledger. It has produced a Christianity that makes you feel better, that gives you community, that provides moral frameworks for your children, that positions you on the right side of whatever social conversation is current — while the fundamental demand of Jesus, which is the surrender of the self-arrangement, remains untouched.

---

The steel-manned counter-position deserves a fair hearing.

The argument runs: the church has always existed within culture, and cultural accommodation is not the same as betrayal. Paul became all things to all people (1 Corinthians 9:22). The church that refuses to engage its culture on terms the culture can receive will not have the opportunity to be prophetic, because it will have no audience. The Constantinian settlement had real costs, but it also produced Christendom — centuries of art, philosophy, education, and social infrastructure built on Christian foundations. The politically engaged church may be making accommodation, but accommodation at least keeps a chair in the room, and from that chair, some witness is possible.

That argument has more force than its critics usually grant. Blanket condemnation of cultural engagement proves too much — it would indict every theologian who ever engaged secular philosophy, every Christian who ever held political office, every church that ever accepted tax-exempt status. The question is not whether to engage culture but how, and at what price, and who sets the terms.

But here is where the argument breaks down. The seduction does not announce itself as seduction. It arrives wearing the language of opportunity, of influence, of salt and light — the very biblical metaphors for engagement. The question the church in every age must ask is not *are we engaging culture* but *who is forming whom*. Is the church bringing to the culture a word that culture cannot generate on its own? Or is the church adopting the culture's categories — power, comfort, tribal identity, therapeutic validation — and baptizing them?

When the political arrangement tells the church what to say, and the church says it and calls it prophecy, the formation runs the wrong direction. When the market tells the church what its members want, and the church delivers it and calls it ministry, the formation runs the wrong direction. The salt has lost its saltiness. Jesus was not gentle about what happens then (Matthew 5:13).

---

The recovery does not look like withdrawal. It does not look like the church circling its wagons and waiting for the culture to come to its senses. It looks like what Dietrich Bonhoeffer called — in a Germany where the seduction was catastrophic in scale — "costly grace": the grace that actually demands something, that does not leave the receiver unchanged, that cannot be purchased with the coin of cultural alignment. [cite — confirm Bonhoeffer, Cost of Discipleship, relevant passage.]

"Cheap grace," Bonhoeffer wrote, "is grace without discipleship, grace without the cross, grace without Jesus Christ, living and incarnate." [cite — confirm exact quote.] The seduced church is the church of cheap grace. It is not the church of no grace. It keeps the language, the liturgy, the programs. What it has surrendered is the cost — the specific cost that comes from belonging to a Lord whose kingdom runs by different logic than every kingdom on offer.

That cost is not abstract. It shows up in the sermon that tells the powerful they are wrong. It shows up in the congregation that welcomes the one the neighborhood would rather not see. It shows up in the pastor who says the thing that will lose him the donor who has been most generous. It shows up, sometimes, as the simple refusal to let the partisan framework be the last word on what faithfulness requires.

---

I am not outside this. I have felt the pull. I have softened the sermon to protect the relationship. I have let the cultural moment define the angle of approach when the text demanded something less convenient. I have, at times, let the arrangement be more determinative than the call.

Hosea's accusation is not aimed at people who deliberately turned away. It is aimed at people who made reasonable adjustments until the adjustments became a different allegiance entirely — who looked up one day and found they had been faithless without a moment when they had chosen faithlessness.

That is the danger of seduction. It does not ask for a decisive betrayal. It asks for a series of small ones, each of which is defensible, each of which makes sense in the moment, each of which moves the center a little further from where it was.

The fire will come down again. The question it will answer is not whether we believed the right things. It is whether we trusted — whether, when the chariots were available and the fear was real and the political arrangement was offering a seat at the table, we knew who we belonged to.

The people of Israel did not answer Elijah a word.

That silence is still the one that kills.
