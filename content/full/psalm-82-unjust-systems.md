There is a psalm that most congregations have never heard preached. It is eight verses long. It opens in a courtroom where the defendant is God — or rather, where God is the judge and the defendants are gods, divine beings who have been given authority over the nations and who have exercised that authority corruptly. It is a strange, unsettling text, and its strangeness is precisely why it matters.

Psalm 82 opens: "God has taken his place in the divine council; in the midst of the gods he holds judgment" (Psalm 82:1). The Hebrew is explicit: God stands in the *'adat-'el*, the assembly of the divine beings, and renders a verdict. The beings assembled are not metaphors for human rulers, though many interpreters have tried to make them so. They are the *elohim* who have been assigned governance of the nations — a cosmology attested elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible (Deuteronomy 32:8–9 in the Dead Sea Scrolls tradition; Daniel 10) and in the wider ancient Near Eastern understanding of how divine powers administered human affairs. [cite — confirm] The psalm is working with that cosmology and exploding it from within.

The indictment is specific. These divine governors have failed at their governing function, and the failure is named in terms of who has been harmed: "How long will you judge unjustly and show partiality to the wicked? Give justice to the weak and the fatherless; maintain the right of the afflicted and the destitute. Rescue the weak and the needy; deliver them from the hand of the wicked" (Psalm 82:2–4).

The list is worth sitting with. The weak. The fatherless. The afflicted. The destitute. The needy. These are not incidental groupings. They are the specific population that appears repeatedly throughout the Hebrew prophetic tradition as the measure of a society's justice — the people whose vulnerability reveals whether the governing powers are operating in alignment with the character of the God who made them. The divine council is indicted not for failing in some abstract governance task but for failing the specific people whose protection is the defining purpose of just governance.

I grew up without a father. I do not read "the fatherless" in that list as a rhetorical category. I read it as a description of a specific form of social vulnerability that governance structures either protect or exploit. The psalm names the exploitation of the fatherless as cosmic treason.

The verdict follows the indictment: "They have neither knowledge nor understanding, they walk about in darkness; all the foundations of the earth are shaken" (Psalm 82:5). This is the causal logic the psalm is pressing. When the governing powers fail to protect the vulnerable, the foundations shake. Not as metaphor. As structural reality. The stability of the created order depends on the just treatment of the weakest members of the community. The corruption of governance toward the wicked and away from the poor does not merely harm individuals. It destabilizes the architecture of the world. The cosmos is not neutral about injustice.

The sentence is then passed: "You are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you; nevertheless, like men you shall die, and fall like any prince" (Psalm 82:6–7). The authority delegated to these powers is revoked. The tenure of divine governance that failed the weak is ended. And the psalm closes with a petition that is, given what precedes it, explosive: "Arise, O God, judge the earth; for you shall inherit all the nations" (Psalm 82:8).

This is not a prayer for divine intervention in a private matter. It is a political declaration: the failed governors have forfeited their authority, and the God who stands in defense of the weak and the fatherless is now claimed as the rightful ruler of every nation.

Jesus quotes Psalm 82 in John 10:34 in a context that New Testament scholarship has increasingly recognized as a claim to divine identity — he is not merely citing the text as precedent but positioning himself as the one the psalm's closing petition had requested. [cite — confirm] The one who rises to judge the earth, who inherits the nations, who takes governance from the powers that have walked in darkness — that one has arrived. This changes the pastoral weight of Psalm 82. It is not only a text about ancient Near Eastern cosmology or a historical artifact of Israelite religion. It is a text that the New Testament reads as inaugurated. The justice it demands has been launched in the person of the one who said in Luke 4 that he had been anointed "to proclaim good news to the poor" and "liberty to the oppressed."

Which means the church that reads this text cannot do so from a neutral position. The powers named in Psalm 82 are generic enough to name any governing structure — any system of economic, political, or social authority — that consistently delivers for the powerful and consistently fails the weak. The logic of the indictment does not require the governor to be a supernatural being. It requires the governor to have authority and to exercise it in ways that walk in darkness — that show partiality to the wicked, that neglect the fatherless and the destitute, that leave the needy in the hand of those who would exploit them.

The application to contemporary systemic injustice is not a liberal innovation or a conservative threat. It is the plain reading of the text.

The counter-argument from those who resist systemic analysis of injustice goes like this: the church should focus on individual sin and individual transformation, not on systems. Systems are too abstract. Social change is too slow and too politically entangled. The gospel transforms persons, and transformed persons change cultures from within. There is truth in this — genuine truth, worth holding. Individual repentance is not optional. Transformed hearts do change communities. The reduction of justice work to political organizing without the accompanying call to personal metanoia is a real theological error.

But Psalm 82 does not give us the luxury of that reduction in the other direction. The psalm is not addressed to the hearts of individuals. It is addressed to governing powers — to the structures and systems through which authority is exercised. The indictment is systemic: the entire council has failed in its governing function. The solution is not merely the conversion of individual council members. It is the replacement of the corrupt governance structure with the direct rule of the God who defends the weak. The psalm is thinking categorically, structurally, about how power is organized and for whose benefit.

The prophetic tradition that preceded and followed Psalm 82 shares this structural analysis. Amos 5 does not merely indict greedy individuals. It indicts the system that "trample the head of the poor into the dust of the earth and turn aside the way of the afflicted" (Amos 2:7). Micah 6:8 does not call for personal spiritual disciplines as an alternative to justice. It names justice — *mishpat* — as the required disposition alongside mercy and humility. Isaiah 10 pronounces woe on those "who decree iniquitous decrees, and the writers who keep writing oppression, to turn aside the needy from justice and to rob the poor of my people of their right" (Isaiah 10:1–2). The decrees are the problem. The legislative structures are the subject of the indictment. The scribes who write the policies are named alongside the powerful who benefit from them.

The church that inherits this tradition and then refuses to apply it to contemporary governing systems because that would be "getting political" has performed an act of hermeneutic cowardice that the tradition itself does not support.

What Psalm 82 requires is not that the church become an arm of a particular political party. Both major parties in the American system have at various points exercised the kind of governance the psalm indicts — showing partiality to the powerful, failing the fatherless, leaving the needy in the hands of those who exploit them. The psalm's standard is not partisan. It is covenantal. It measures governance by one question: is the system protecting and providing for the weakest members of the community, or is it not?

That question can and should be applied by a congregation across the full spectrum of policies that affect the vulnerable — tax policy, housing policy, immigration enforcement, child welfare, criminal justice, food access, medical care. The congregation that learns to ask the psalm's question will not become a single-issue voter bloc. It will become a community capable of prophetic discernment that is accountable to something older and more demanding than party loyalty.

The foundations of the earth are at stake when the weak are abandoned. That is not rhetoric. It is the psalm's own structural claim.

The God who arises to judge the earth judges the church too. We have been entrusted with the same governing responsibility — not over nations, but over our own practices of power, our own institutional decisions about who receives our care and whose suffering we acknowledge and whose we ignore. The council assembled in Psalm 82 walked in darkness because they did not know, did not understand. Ignorance does not protect them from the verdict.

We know. The question is whether knowing has changed what we do.

Arise, O God. Judge the earth.

And let the church that prays that prayer be ready to be judged with it.
