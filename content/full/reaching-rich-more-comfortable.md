There is a congregation in nearly every American city that understands itself to be doing the work of the kingdom by bringing wealthy, educated, culturally influential people into the church. The strategic logic is coherent: if you can reach the people with resources and influence, they will fund the mission and shape the culture. Leaders matter. Resources matter. A church that influences the powerful has, in theory, outsized kingdom leverage.

I have heard this argument from serious people who genuinely love God. I have made versions of it myself. It contains real truth, and the truth in it has made it possible to ignore the theological problem at its center for most of American church history.

The theological problem is Jesus.

---

He was not subtle about this.

"It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for someone who is rich to enter the kingdom of God" (Matthew 19:24). The disciples, who shared the common cultural assumption that wealth was a sign of God's blessing, were "greatly astonished" and asked who, then, could be saved. Jesus did not retract the statement. He deepened it: "With man this is impossible, but with God all things are possible" — which is not a soft landing for the wealthy. It is an acknowledgment that the barrier is real and formidable.

Luke records that Jesus read from Isaiah at the synagogue in Nazareth to announce what his ministry would be: "good news to the poor," "freedom for the prisoners," "recovery of sight for the blind," "freedom for the oppressed" (Luke 4:18). He did not say "good news to the influential, which will eventually improve conditions for everyone." He named the poor first, specifically, as the primary recipients of the kingdom announcement.

The Beatitudes in Luke are more explicit than Matthew's version: "Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God" (Luke 6:20). And then: "Woe to you who are rich, for you have already received your comfort" (Luke 6:24). These are not metaphors. They are social and economic categories, and the reversals they announce are genuine reversals. The kingdom puts these categories upside down.

The comfortable church has never quite known what to do with any of this.

---

The American church's orientation toward wealth has historical roots that predate the contemporary megachurch. The Protestant work ethic, as Max Weber [cite — confirm] traced it, created a cultural logic in which material success was read as a sign of election — God's favor made visible in earthly prosperity. This was always a theological distortion, but it was an extraordinarily useful one for the emerging commercial culture of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. It sanctified accumulation. It made the successful man spiritually respectable and the poor man spiritually suspect.

The late-nineteenth and early-twentieth century saw this logic reach full flower in the Gospel of Wealth — most explicitly articulated by Andrew Carnegie [cite — confirm] — and in the parallel phenomenon of wealthy industrialists becoming prominent figures in and donors to the mainline Protestant churches. The church that was home to the Carnegie and Rockefeller families was not in a strong institutional position to prophesy against Gilded Age exploitation of labor. It was too dependent on the same.

Historian Richard Hofstadter [cite — confirm] and others have documented the degree to which American Protestant Christianity was shaped by the commercial class that dominated it financially. When the money in the room comes from a particular direction, the preaching tends to follow. This is not a conspiracy. It is an institutional logic that operates below the level of conscious decision: the pastor who offends his major donors loses his major donors, and the institution that depends on them cannot survive that loss. The financial pressure produces doctrinal outcomes over time.

The twentieth-century megachurch replicated and amplified this pattern. The churches that grew most dramatically in the suburban expansion of the postwar decades were churches that spoke the language and addressed the concerns of the middle and upper-middle class — the nuclear family, professional success, personal fulfillment, therapeutic wellbeing. The people who came to these churches in the largest numbers were the people for whom this message was most immediately relevant. The people for whom it was least relevant — the poor, the economically marginal, the incarcerated, the homeless — were not, structurally, the target.

---

The logic of targeting the wealthy for kingdom impact has a steelman worth addressing honestly.

Billy Graham's ministry reached presidents and cultural leaders because he understood that influence multiplies through networks — that a converted executive shapes more lives than a converted laborer, not because the executive's soul is worth more, but because his reach is wider [cite — confirm]. The argument is pragmatic and not, in its pure form, theologically corrupt. The New Testament does not exclude the wealthy from the kingdom. Zacchaeus was a tax collector and wealthy, and Jesus sought him out. Nicodemus was a member of the ruling council, and Jesus engaged him at length. Joseph of Arimathea, who provided the tomb, was described as a rich man (Matthew 27:57). The church has had influential, wealthy members since its earliest days.

The problem is not that the wealthy are welcome in the church. The problem is the organizational logic that makes reaching the wealthy the church's *primary* strategic orientation — that positions the poor as a ministry category rather than the theological center, that measures kingdom success in donor networks and cultural influence rather than in proximity to the people Jesus named first when he described his mission.

The difference between including the wealthy in a community organized around the poor and the marginalized, versus organizing the community around the wealthy and adding a charity program for the poor — that difference is not trivial. It determines who sits at the center of the church's life, whose concerns shape the preaching, whose presence defines the culture. It determines whether the poor are guests or neighbors. It determines whether the church's relationship to wealth is prophetic or dependent.

---

There is a passage in Amos that most wealthy congregations have not heard preached in its proper context. Chapter 6 opens: "Woe to you who are complacent in Zion, and to you who feel secure on Mount Samaria, you notable men of the foremost nation, to whom the people of Israel come!" (Amos 6:1). The notable men. The foremost nation. The people come to them. This is the church that has influence, that is respected, that sits at the tables of cultural power. And Amos's word to them is *woe* — because their comfort has made them indifferent to the suffering around them: "You do not grieve over the ruin of Joseph" (Amos 6:6). The ruin is happening. The notable men are not grieving it. They are lying on beds of ivory and singing songs to the sound of the harp.

The church organized around reaching the powerful is always in danger of becoming the church that does not grieve the ruin.

---

I have not been immune to this. I have felt the appeal of the growing church, the influential church, the church with the capacity to project its voice into the culture. I have felt the pull of a budget that allows real ministry to happen, funded by the generosity of people who have the resources to fund it. This is not entirely wrong. Ministry requires resources, and resources require givers, and the church that refuses to welcome givers because they are successful has made an error in the other direction.

But I have also sat across tables from pastors of small, underfunded, predominantly poor congregations whose theological clarity about the kingdom was sharper than anything I encountered in the places with the larger budgets and the more impressive reach. They knew something about the kingdom that the megachurch pastor does not easily know, because they are living in closer proximity to the people Jesus named first.

The institutional suspicion that greets the pastor who orients his church around the poor is real. It comes from boards who wonder about sustainability. It comes from denominational structures that measure success in attendance and giving. It comes from peer culture among pastors who define effectiveness in the metrics that wealthy churches produce. The pastor who turns toward the poor, in the American church environment, is not rowing with the current.

He is rowing against the current. The current is money.

---

The question is not whether the church should exclude the wealthy. The question is whether the church has allowed wealth and its presence to become the organizing principle of its life and mission — whether the arrangement of the congregation, the tone of the preaching, the strategic choices about location and programming, have all been calibrated around the comfort and retention of people with resources, while the people Jesus named first remain somewhere over there, in the mercy ministry budget, not quite at the center.

Luke 14 contains the dinner party passage where Jesus instructs the host: "When you give a luncheon or dinner, do not invite your friends, your brothers or sisters, your relatives, or your rich neighbors; if you do, they may invite you back and so you will be repaid. But when you give a banquet, invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind, and you will be blessed" (Luke 14:12-14).

He did not say refuse the rich. He said orient the table differently. He said the metric of kingdom faithfulness is who you invite when there is nothing to be gained.

The church that gathers only people who can give back to it has understood giving as transaction. The church that invites the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind — that church is describing something else. It is describing the kingdom of God.

That table is available. The question is who we are building our guest list around.
