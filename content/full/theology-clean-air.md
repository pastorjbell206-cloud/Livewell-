Breathe.

You just did something that Genesis 2:7 traces to the act of God. "Then the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature." The Hebrew word is *neshama* — breath, soul, the animating force of a life. Every breath connects the living creature to the Creator who breathed it into existence. Respiration is, in this sense, a theological act. The air you breathe is not a resource. It is a gift. And the gift is being poisoned.

That is not a political claim. It is a statement about what is happening and who bears the cost.

---

The body of research on air pollution and its effects on human health is not contested science. Fine particulate matter — PM2.5, the particles small enough to penetrate deep into lung tissue — is associated with increased rates of cardiovascular disease, stroke, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, lung cancer, and premature death. The World Health Organization estimates that air pollution causes approximately seven million premature deaths per year globally [cite — confirm]. In the United States, communities near highways, industrial facilities, and petrochemical plants bear the highest exposure to particulate matter, ground-level ozone, and chemical emissions. Those communities are disproportionately Black, Latino, and low-income. [cite — confirm]

This is not coincidence. The siting of industrial facilities and highways in communities of color follows patterns established by the same kinds of policy decisions that produced redlining — decisions about whose neighborhoods were valuable enough to protect and whose were available for uses that the protected neighborhoods would not tolerate. The result is that the air quality in a given zip code correlates strongly with the racial composition of that zip code. Children in those communities have higher rates of asthma. Adults have shorter lifespans. The health burden of dirty air is distributed along lines that should be recognizable to anyone who has spent time reading the prophets.

---

Amos 4:1 addresses the women of Bashan who crushed the poor and demanded more from their husbands. The oppression Amos named was economic and structural — the accumulation of luxury by those whose comfort depended on the suffering of the poor. The mechanism was trade, lending, taxation. In 2026, the mechanism includes the siting of refineries and chemical plants in communities that lack the political power to prevent it, the failure to regulate particulate emissions from truck traffic concentrated on highways that were built through communities of color, and the persistence of lead-contaminated infrastructure in cities where the replacement cost is treated as excessive.

The prophetic vocabulary does not change when the mechanism changes. The people bearing the cost are still the poor. The people setting the conditions are still the powerful. The question the prophet asks is still: what does the LORD require?

Micah 6:8 is the most compressed answer Scripture gives: do justice, love mercy, walk humbly with your God. The justice Micah has in mind is not a general disposition of fairness. It is *mishpat* — the active establishment of right order in relationships and institutions, the restoration of those who have been damaged by the corruption of that order. To do mishpat is to name the specific injustice and to act against it. The child in Flint, Michigan whose development was damaged by lead-contaminated water — damaged not by accident but by official decision to use the Flint River as a cost-saving measure and to ignore the evidence that the water was unsafe [cite — confirm] — that child is a mishpat claim. The church in Flint that did not raise its voice in that child's defense was not being apolitical. It was failing to do justice.

---

The theological case for clean air as a justice concern begins with imago Dei — the affirmation of Genesis 1:26-27 that every human being is made in the image of God. The imago Dei is not primarily a statement about human cognition or spiritual capacity. It is a statement about dignity — the inherent worth of every person that cannot be reduced to their utility, their productivity, or their economic value to the people around them. To damage a person's health through preventable environmental exposure is to treat the imago Dei as expendable when it lives in a body that lacks political protection.

The specific targets of the prophetic witness — the widow, the orphan, the stranger, the poor — are not simply people who need charity. They are the people the social order has most thoroughly failed, the people whose imago Dei is most systematically denied by the structures of the world they live in. They are the people who live near the fence lines of chemical plants. They are the people who breathe the air that the rest of us made dirty and then arranged not to live near.

The New Testament does not abandon this concern. Paul's letter to the Romans includes the famous declaration that "creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption" (Romans 8:21) — that the redemption God is working toward is not only the salvation of individual souls but the liberation of the whole created order from the decay that sin introduced. The present condition of creation is described as groaning (Romans 8:22) — a labor pain that points toward a birth rather than toward a death. The church that reads this text and concludes that the material world is dispensable has misread it.

---

There is a pastoral objection worth addressing directly: the charge that environmental justice is a political cause that the church has no business endorsing.

This charge contains a real concern. The church has been weaponized by political causes in both directions, and the pastor who cannot distinguish prophetic faithfulness from partisan alignment has lost the ability to speak to either. The warning is legitimate.

But the charge proves too much. The question is not whether the church should endorse a policy platform. The question is whether the church should care about the child who cannot breathe. That question has an answer that does not require a party registration. Jesus placed a child in the center of the disciples' argument about greatness (Matthew 18:1-4). He said that whoever receives a child in his name receives him, and whoever causes a child to stumble would be better off drowned (Matthew 18:5-6). The child with asthma in the neighborhood near the highway is not a political abstraction. She is a child. The church that will not speak for her has confused neutrality with faithfulness.

The steel-man version of the objection goes further: perhaps clean air is a genuine concern, but the church should pursue it through prayer and charity rather than through policy engagement, because the church's proper sphere is the spiritual rather than the political. This view has a long pedigree in American Christianity — the pietist tradition that locates the church's primary work in the transformation of individual souls, trusting that individual transformation will eventually produce social change.

That tradition has genuine insight. The transformation of individual hearts is irreplaceable. No policy can do what genuine conversion does. But the prophets did not make the distinction. Amos and Micah and Isaiah addressed the political and economic structures of their day with the same authority they addressed individual sin. They did not have a category for spiritual concerns that were separate from the conditions under which the poor lived. The God who breathed life into the human creature through the breath is not indifferent to the condition of the air that creature now breathes. The two are not separable.

---

The environmental justice movement has deep roots in communities of faith. The term "environmental justice" itself was shaped significantly by the work of Black churches in Warren County, North Carolina in 1982 [cite — confirm], when those communities led a protest against the siting of a PCB landfill in a majority-Black county. The connection between environmental harm and racial injustice was named by people of faith because they had the theological vocabulary for it: the poor, the vulnerable, the people the system treats as expendable.

The church does not need to adopt an environmental justice framework from the secular left. It has a better one — older, more comprehensive, more theologically grounded. It is the framework of the whole biblical canon, from the creation mandate of Genesis 2 to the restored creation of Revelation 22, in which the tree of life stands beside the river of the water of life and its leaves are for "the healing of the nations" (Revelation 22:2).

The tree is still there. The nations still need healing. The air they breathe still matters to the God who breathed life into the first human being and has not stopped breathing.

---

What does the theology of clean air ask of the church?

At minimum, it asks for the willingness to see — to look at the map of air quality in your city, to notice where it is worst, to ask who lives there and why. It asks for the courage to name the connection between environmental harm and racial and economic injustice, to call it what the prophets would have called it.

It asks for the willingness to stand alongside the communities that bear the worst of it — not as paternalistic helpers but as members of the same body, whose health is bound up with theirs, whose flourishing depends on theirs, because the body of Christ is one body and when one part suffers, the whole suffers (1 Corinthians 12:26).

Every breath you take is a gift. The child whose breath comes harder than yours is a daughter of the same Creator.

What are we going to do about that?
