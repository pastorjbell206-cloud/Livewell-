Something happened to the word "justice" in American evangelical Christianity somewhere in the first decade of this century, and the effect has been severe. The word began to be heard — in many pulpits, in many denominational conversations, in many board meetings — as a political signal rather than a theological claim. A pastor who preached on justice was assumed to be announcing a partisan alignment. A congregation that pursued justice ministries was suspected of departing from the gospel. The word itself became so thoroughly coded that many serious, faithful church leaders stopped using it, not because they had abandoned the concept but because they could not use the concept without triggering a response that had nothing to do with theology.

This inversion is one of the more serious theological errors of our time. And I say "of our time" without apology, because the error is specific to our cultural moment, not latent in the tradition.

The Hebrew word mishpat appears over two hundred times in the Old Testament. [cite — confirm count] It is most commonly translated "justice," and it describes something that is not primarily a political arrangement but a moral and theological reality — the establishment of right order between persons and communities, the giving to each what each is owed, the protection of the weak from the predations of the powerful. When the prophets use mishpat, they are not calling for a particular policy platform. They are naming a characteristic of God himself. "The LORD is a God of justice" (Isaiah 30:18). Not a God who endorses a justice agenda. A God whose own character is just, who acts in history according to his own character, and who requires of his people a corresponding character.

The companion word is tsedaqah, typically translated "righteousness," though in prophetic usage it carries more than the English word "righteousness" suggests — it points toward right relationship, toward the restoration of the conditions under which human beings can flourish together. Mishpat and tsedaqah appear together throughout the prophetic literature as a pair that describes the full scope of what God requires and what God embodies. Amos 5:24 — "But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" — is not a political slogan. It is a theological description of what God's character looks like when it becomes visible in the social order.

That theology did not begin with Amos. It began at Sinai.

The Law given to Israel was, among other things, a social architecture — a set of arrangements designed to produce a community that reflected the character of its God. The gleaning laws in Leviticus 19 required landowners to leave the edges of their fields unharvested for the poor and the stranger. The Jubilee provisions in Leviticus 25 mandated a periodic redistribution of land that had accumulated through economic transaction, returning it to the families from whom it had passed — a structural correction to the tendency of market economies to concentrate wealth. The laws governing debt, wages, and treatment of workers — "Do not hold back the wages of a hired worker overnight" (Leviticus 19:13) — were not suggestions about personal virtue. They were legal requirements embedded in the covenantal identity of the people. A people who treated their workers unjustly had not merely failed in private virtue. They had violated the covenant. They had become something other than what God had called them to be.

The prophets were, among other things, enforcement officers for the covenant. When Amos condemned Israel for "trampling on the heads of the poor" and "pushing the afflicted out of the way" (Amos 2:7), he was not introducing a new concern. He was measuring Israel against the covenant standard they already possessed and finding them disqualified. When Micah asked "what does the LORD require of you but to act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8), he was not offering a new program. He was reminding Israel of the content of their existing obligations. The prophets do not innovate. They recall. They hold the community to the measure it has already agreed to.

Jesus did not abandon this framework. He intensified it.

The Beatitudes in Matthew 5 are not spiritual encouragements for the emotionally humble. They are reversals — announcements that the order of things as the powerful have arranged them is not the order of things as God has arranged them, and that the one does not predict the other. "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 5:3). "Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they will be filled" (Matthew 5:6). The word in verse 6 — *dikaiosyne* — is the Greek equivalent of tsedaqah. It means right relationship. The ones who are unsatisfied with the current arrangements and long for the conditions under which human beings can live together in God's design — those are the ones Jesus calls blessed.

In Luke's version of the Beatitudes, the language is even less metaphorical: "Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God. Blessed are you who are hungry now, for you will be satisfied" (Luke 6:20–21). The poor. The hungry. The ones the social order has assigned to the margins. Jesus identifies with them not as a political alignment but as a theological claim about where God stands in human history.

The strong objection — and it deserves honest engagement — goes like this: justice is not primarily about social structures but about the heart. The root cause of injustice is individual sin, and the gospel addresses individual sin through conversion and regeneration. A church that focuses on structural justice has confused the secondary effects of sin with sin itself, and has substituted sociological analysis for the gospel. Better to change hearts than to rearrange structures that sinful hearts will simply corrupt again.

This argument has real theological content. It is not wrong that individual transformation is necessary. It is not wrong that structural change without heart change tends to produce different configurations of the same fundamental problems. It is not wrong that the gospel addresses the person, not merely the system.

But the argument commits a false opposition. The prophets were not confused about individual sin. Amos condemned specific individuals — the merchants who falsified their scales (Amos 8:5), the wealthy who sold the righteous for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals (Amos 2:6). The Mosaic law regulated individual conduct directly. The prophetic tradition does not choose between individual and structural — it insists that the two are always implicated in each other. Systems are made of people, and people are made partly by systems. A person formed by an economic arrangement that makes exploitation profitable will find it easier to exploit and harder to resist exploiting. A community whose laws protect the vulnerable creates conditions under which the vulnerable can actually live. These are not competing concerns. They are the same concern at different levels.

What the evangelical retreat from justice language has actually produced is not a purer gospel. It has produced a gospel with a missing half — one that can address the individual soul with genuine power but has very little to say to the systems within which souls live and are formed and are destroyed. That half-gospel is easily accommodated by the powers it does not address. A church that is willing to preach against personal sin but unwilling to name the structural arrangements that make certain sins profitable and certain virtues nearly impossible has made itself available to be used by those arrangements as a mechanism of social stability.

The prophets were expelled from the temple not because they were bad theologians. They were expelled because they were good ones — because their theology had consequences for the powerful, and the powerful preferred a theology without consequences.

We have arrived at a moment where a generation of younger Christians, raised in the evangelical tradition, is walking away from it partly because the tradition seems to have nothing to say about the world they can actually see — the economic anxiety, the housing crisis, the racial divisions, the ways in which geography and family income and zip code determine life outcomes before anyone has made a single moral choice. They are not asking the church to become a political party. They are asking whether the God the church preaches has any claim on the conditions under which they actually live. When the church's answer is that those conditions are political questions outside theology's scope, the questioner has been told, functionally, that theology has no reach into the part of life where they most need it.

Justice is the word the prophets used for what God requires and what God does. It is a theological word before it is a political one, and it belongs to the tradition before it belongs to any party platform. The church that cedes this word to the political sphere has ceded something that is not politics' to claim.

Mishpat. Tsedaqah. Two Hebrew words, ancient in origin, whose content was established not in a party platform but in the covenant between God and a people he was forming in his own image. That is where the word comes from. That is where it still belongs.

Reclaiming it is not a political project. It is a theological recovery.
