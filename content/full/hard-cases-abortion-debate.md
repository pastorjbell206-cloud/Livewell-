The people who make abortion debates most uncomfortable are not the ideologues on either side. They are the women in the waiting rooms — the ones whose stories do not fit cleanly into anyone's framework.

The woman who is fourteen weeks pregnant and has just received a diagnosis that her child has no chance of survival outside the womb. The woman who is pregnant as a result of rape and is not sure what faithfulness looks like now. The woman whose continued pregnancy is a medical threat to her life. The couple who already have three children and are one financial crisis from losing everything, who are trying to figure out what love actually requires in the specific circumstances of their specific lives.

The easy answers do not reach these cases. And when pastors pretend they do — when confident theological declarations are handed to people whose situations the declarations were never designed to address — the people in those situations know immediately that they are not being seen.

## The Pharisees Had Clear Positions

The comparison is not flattering, and I offer it with awareness of what it costs. The Pharisees were not cynical or corrupt in their commitment to the Law. They were serious about it. They had worked out its implications with considerable care, and they were prepared to apply those implications with precision.

And Jesus, again and again, had time for the people whose situations embarrassed the positions. The woman with the bleeding disorder who had spent twelve years in the company of physicians — and who reached through the crowd and touched the hem of his garment in what was technically a violation of purity law (Mark 5:25-34). The man with the withered hand, whose healing Jesus performed on the Sabbath in full view of those monitoring his compliance with the rules (Mark 3:1-6). The sinful woman in the home of Simon the Pharisee, who wept on Jesus's feet while the host calculated the theological cost of permitting the display (Luke 7:36-50).

Jesus was not indifferent to the Law. He said he came to fulfill it, not abolish it (Matthew 5:17). What he was indifferent to was the use of the Law as a barrier against the encounter with persons. The Law was made for human beings, he said, not human beings for the Law (Mark 2:27).

That principle has direct bearing on how the church handles the hard cases. The theological position exists to serve the person, not the other way around.

## What "Hard Cases" Actually Means

The hard cases are not hard because they present a challenge to the general pro-life position that cannot be answered. Many careful theologians have worked through the arguments about rape, incest, fetal abnormality, and maternal health risk with real rigor and have offered positions that deserve serious engagement.

The hard cases are hard because they require you to hold genuine moral complexity with real people attached to it. They are hard because the woman in front of you is not a hypothetical designed to test your position — she is a person who did not choose the circumstances she is in, who is being asked to bear a cost that is real and immediate, and who needs more than a verdict handed down from outside her situation.

The Greek word for mercy — eleos — is used in the Septuagint as the translation of the Hebrew hesed: covenantal faithfulness, loyal love, the kind of commitment that does not dissolve when the situation becomes complicated. Hesed in the Old Testament is the word used for God's commitment to Israel even when Israel has failed. It is the covenant love that does not evaporate when the conditions of the covenant are difficult to meet.

A church that is quick with a position and slow with eleos has the ratio backwards. The position may be correct. The timing is wrong. Presence before answers. Honest engagement with complexity before confident declaration. The humility to know that proximity to real suffering changes what you want to say.

## A Specific Scene

She came into my office after a service in which I had spoken about the sanctity of unborn life with the kind of clarity I thought the congregation needed. She was twenty-two years old and twelve weeks pregnant, and three weeks earlier she had learned that the pregnancy had resulted from an assault she had not told her family about.

She did not ask me what the church's position was. She knew what it was. She had sat in the third row and heard it. What she asked me — the question she managed to get out before she stopped being able to speak clearly — was whether God was still there with her. Whether he was still there in this.

I had prepared a sermon. I had not prepared for her. The gap between those two things is the gap this article is trying to name.

I said what I could. I do not know if it was enough. What I know is that the sermon I had preached twenty minutes earlier was not the thing she needed in that office, and that if I had led with the sermon, I would have lost her.

## The Counter-Position Deserves a Real Answer

The counter-position is this: hard cases are used rhetorically to undermine the general position. In reality, hard cases represent a very small percentage of abortions. Most abortions are not the result of rape or fetal abnormality; they are the result of an unwanted pregnancy in circumstances that could be addressed with genuine support. By focusing pastoral attention on the dramatic edge cases, you risk eroding the general conviction that protects the much larger number of unborn children whose situations are less dramatic.

This argument has statistical weight. The percentage of abortions that occur in the cases most frequently cited as hard — rape, incest, fetal abnormality, direct threat to maternal life — is genuinely small [cite — confirm]. The rhetorical deployment of hard cases to undermine the general principle is a real phenomenon, and the honest pastor needs to see it.

But the argument fails pastorally for two reasons. First, it treats the small number of hard cases as if the smallness of the percentage reduces the weight of the individual person's situation. The woman who conceived in rape is not a statistical edge case. She is a person. Her situation demands serious engagement regardless of how many people share it. Second, the argument assumes that pastoral engagement with the hard cases constitutes a compromise of the general principle — when in fact the ability to engage the hard cases honestly, with real presence and real care, is what earns the pastoral credibility to speak into any of the cases at all.

The church has often been willing to do the easy version of the pro-life commitment — the political declaration, the legislative position, the statement of principle — and very reluctant to do the costly version, which requires sitting with specific women in specific circumstances and offering something other than a verdict.

## What the Hard Cases Require

Hard cases require presence before answers. They require the willingness to say "I am here" before the willingness to say "here is what I believe." They require the kind of pastoral self-knowledge that acknowledges: my position may be correct and still be harmful if delivered at the wrong moment in the wrong way without the relationship to carry it.

They require a community that has built the infrastructure of care before the crisis arrives — the relationships of trust, the culture of honest conversation, the network of practical support — so that when the woman in the hard case needs something, the church is not scrambling to construct a response from scratch.

They require training — not in debate, but in listening. The pastor who walks into the hard case having rehearsed his position is not prepared for what the person in front of him actually needs. The pastor who walks in having practiced the discipline of hearing without immediately constructing a response is beginning to be useful.

They require the theological humility to acknowledge that being in the middle of a situation and looking at it from outside are not the same epistemic position. I know what I believe about the sanctity of unborn life. I do not know what I would decide if I were the woman in the waiting room. That asymmetry is worth naming, not as a reason to abandon the conviction, but as a reason to hold it with the kind of care and humility that makes it possible to offer it to a real person in a real crisis.

The hardest thing about the hard cases is not that they are theologically unresolvable. It is that resolving them at a distance is easy, and sitting with someone in the middle of one is not. The church has often been very willing to do the first thing and very reluctant to do the second.

That is the problem worth naming — and then, harder still, worth doing something about.

The weight remains. It does not resolve itself by being named. It only becomes more demanding.
