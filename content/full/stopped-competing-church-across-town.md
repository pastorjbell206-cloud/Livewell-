I will tell you something I am not proud of. For years I kept a private scoreboard. The church across town grew, and something in me tightened. Their building project went up, and I found a reason it was unwise. Their pastor was kind to me at the lunch, and I went home and explained to myself why his kindness was a strategy. I would not have called it competition. I called it discernment. It was envy wearing a collar.

The competitive spirit in ministry is quiet, and that is what makes it dangerous. It does not announce itself. It hides inside legitimate things — a desire to reach people, a longing to see the work flourish, a healthy attention to whether you are being faithful. It borrows the language of zeal and it means something closer to fear. Fear that there is not enough — not enough people, not enough room, not enough significance — and that the brother down the road is taking your share.

That fear has a long history in the church, and naming the history helps.

The American church inherited a model of congregational success shaped less by the New Testament than by the market logic of the frontier. When circuit riders competed for converts in the early nineteenth century, the theological framework was revivalism and the practical metric was decisions. Charles Finney's "new measures" — the anxious bench, the protracted meeting, the direct appeal for public commitment — were innovations designed, consciously or not, to be more effective than what the next denomination was doing [cite — confirm]. The competition was dressed in the language of harvest, but the underlying logic was institutional. More converts was better. More converts than the Methodists was better still.

That logic did not disappear when the frontier closed. It migrated into the church growth movement of the late twentieth century, which imported the language of market strategy and demographic analysis and consumer preference into the work of building congregations. The result was a pastoral culture shaped by competitive growth metrics — attendance, giving units, square footage, platform visibility — and a generation of pastors whose sense of vocational health was indexed to those metrics more than they knew. I was one of those pastors. The scoreboard I was keeping was not an aberration. It was the ecosystem.

Here is the theology underneath the competition: a functional belief that the kingdom of God is a zero-sum market. That the family who joins the church across town is a family that cannot join mine. That the pastor whose platform grows at my city's expense has taken something from me. That the town is not large enough for both of us to be faithful.

It is a strange thing to believe while preaching the gospel I preach. The kingdom of God is not a market. The other church is not my competitor; it is, if the creed I recite on Sundays is true, the same body, serving the same Lord, advancing the same kingdom in the same town. When they reach a family I never could, the kingdom gains. When their doors stay open through a hard year, the gospel has a witness on a corner where mine does not stand. I knew all of that. I preached all of that. And I kept the scoreboard anyway, because the heart can hold a doctrine and disobey it at the same time, and a pastor's heart is not exempt.

What broke it was not an argument. It was a funeral.

A pastor I had quietly measured myself against lost a child, and I sat in his sanctuary, in the back, and watched a man I had been competing with stand at the front of the worst day of his life. There was no scoreboard in that room. There was a brother, and grief, and the same Lord we both served holding both of us up. I drove home and I could not find the rivalry anymore. It had never been real. I had built it out of my own fear and then lived inside it as though it were a fact.

Grief has a way of stripping the constructed things down to what actually holds. What held in that room was not institutional. It was the unity that Paul describes in Ephesians 4 — one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father (Ephesians 4:4-6). The oneness is not aspirational. It is the already-given reality that we are called to *keep*, to guard, to preserve — not to create. The competition was a denial of something that was already true. I was fighting over territory in a kingdom that was never divided.

Here is what the competition costs, and it is more than character. It costs you the brothers.

A pastor's loneliness is real and well-documented, and one of its quiet engines is this: the men who could most understand your work are the very men you have decided to compete with. You isolate yourself from your only true peers and then wonder why no one understands. The pastoral work is not fully legible to the congregation — they experience it but do not see behind it. The pastoral work is not fully legible to the denomination — they track metrics but not the interior. The pastoral work is legible to other pastors, because they are doing it too, because they have felt the same weight on the same kinds of Sundays. And if you have made them rivals, you have cut off the relationship that could most honestly hold you.

The Pastoral Connection Network was built on a recognition of exactly this: that the pastor's isolation is not an inevitable feature of the role but a structural problem that requires a structural solution. Men who should be brothers have been made strangers by competition, geography, and the habits of professional self-protection. The network is an attempt to reverse that — to build, deliberately and with some effort, the kind of relationship where the scoreboard is not allowed. I know what the network can produce, because I have been inside it and I have watched men find, in middle age, the pastoral friendships that the competition kept them from building in their thirties.

The steel-man for the competitive approach goes like this: healthy competition between churches raises the quality of ministry across the board. When churches compete for the same population, each is incentivized to be more effective, more relevant, more excellent in its programming and preaching and pastoral care. The market produces quality. The congregation benefits.

The argument has a partial truth in it. The churches I have been inside that were most serious about excellence in preaching were often led by pastors who had excellent peers they were measuring themselves against. Excellence does not emerge in isolation. The standard matters.

But the competitive framework corrupts the goal. When the goal of the excellent sermon is to attract more families than the excellent sermon down the road, the sermon is no longer primarily an address from God to the congregation. It is a competitive product. The pastoral care that is driven by retention strategy is no longer primarily about the flourishing of the souls being cared for. It is institutional maintenance dressed in pastoral language. The corruption is not obvious, but it is real, and it shows up in what the pastor actually worries about when he lies awake at three in the morning.

So I stopped, as best a man can stop a thing that grows back. I started praying for the church across town by name, and meaning it, which is harder than it sounds. I started telling the truth at the pastors' lunch instead of managing my image at it. When their work flourished, I made myself say, out loud to God, *good* — and kept saying it until some of it became true in me.

I started sending families to that church when it was clear they belonged there better than they belonged with me. This was the hardest part. Not the prayer and not the honesty — those were interior changes that cost something but cost it privately. Sending a family across town is a public act with a public cost. The congregation notices the departure. The scoreboard takes a hit. But the family needed what the other church could give them, and my commitment was to them, not to the number. The commission to make disciples does not include a territorial clause.

The town does not need two pastors quietly at war for the same hundred families. It needs a few who have laid the scoreboard down — who can stand in each other's sanctuaries on the worst days, who can send a family across town when that church can serve them better, who measure their ministries by faithfulness and not by the comparison that was always going to leave them empty.

Jesus prayed that his people would be one, so that the world would believe (John 17:21). He tied our unity to the gospel's credibility. The watching town is not most persuaded by the church that wins. It is persuaded, if it is persuaded at all, by the unlikely sight of churches that could compete and refuse to. The unity is the witness. The competition is its undoing.

I still notice the scoreboard. I would be lying if I said otherwise. The machine was built over years and it does not dismantle in a season. But I notice it now as a symptom rather than a fact — a flag that tells me fear is operating, that the kingdom logic has been temporarily displaced by market logic, that I need to pray for the brother whose success triggered the tightening.

Lay it down. The brother across town was never the rival. The fear was. And the fear was lying to you the whole time.
