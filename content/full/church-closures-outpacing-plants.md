The data on American church decline is not new. Researchers have been tracking it for decades — the drop in weekly attendance, the rise of the religiously unaffiliated, the acceleration of closures among small and mid-sized congregations that were already operating on margins too thin to survive the disruptions of the early 2020s [cite — confirm]. What is new is the scale and the pace. The closure rate now significantly outpaces the planting rate. The American church is contracting [cite — confirm].

That fact invites two responses, and most of the church has chosen the wrong one.

The wrong response is panic dressed as strategy. It looks like church leaders spending extraordinary energy on programs designed to attract people back to a model that was already in trouble before they left — better production values, more relevant sermon series, more sophisticated platforms, the latest conference circuit's prescription for congregational renewal. These are not bad things. They are answers to the wrong question.

---

The right response begins with a harder question: not "How do we reverse the numbers?" but "What do the numbers reveal?" Because the closure of a congregation is not first a marketing failure. It is a diagnostic. It tells us something about the health of what was there, about the formation that did or did not happen, about whether the people who sat in those pews were equipped to be the church outside those pews when the building was no longer available as the organizing center of their faith.

This is not a kind question. But it is the necessary one.

The most important number in this conversation is not the closure rate. It is the post-closure discipleship rate — the percentage of people who were members of a church that closed who remained active in another congregation within two years [cite — confirm]. If that number is low, and there is reason to believe it is, then what we are witnessing is not the rearrangement of Christians between institutions but the loss of people from the practicing community of faith altogether. That is a different crisis than the institutional one.

And it points to a different failure.

---

The church that produces disciples who are dependent on the institution for their practice of faith — who require a building, a program, a professional pastor, a Sunday morning structure to sustain their connection to God and the community of faith — has not, in fact, made disciples. It has made consumers. And consumers stop consuming when the product changes or becomes unavailable.

This is the formational crisis underneath the institutional one. It has been accumulating for generations, in the decades when American Christianity's metrics of success were primarily institutional — attendance, giving, programs, staff headcount, campuses. These metrics are not worthless. They track real things. But they do not track formation. They do not measure whether the person in the seat on Sunday has been shaped by their participation in the community in ways that will survive the community's absence.

The German-American theologian Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote, from inside a church that was watching the institutional Christianity of the Reich capitulate to political pressure, about the difference between cheap grace and costly grace [cite — confirm]. Cheap grace, he argued, is the grace that is dispensed without discipleship — the forgiveness that asks nothing, the community that requires nothing, the church that offers comfort without calling anyone to the cross. Cheap grace produces a kind of Christianity that is exactly as deep as the cultural support for it, and no deeper. Remove the cultural support — remove the building, the program, the Sunday morning social structure — and there is nothing beneath it.

Costly grace produces something different. It produces the person who has actually taken on the yoke of Jesus (Matthew 11:29), who has actually counted the cost (Luke 14:28), who has actually been changed in ways that persist when the institutional scaffolding comes down. That person is not dependent on the institution's continued existence for their practice of faith, because their practice of faith is not primarily institutional. It is relational, disciplined, rooted in the text, habituated by prayer and mutual accountability.

---

The disciple that Paul describes in his letters does not require institutional infrastructure to function. He meets in homes (Romans 16:5), he sustains the community through mutual burden-bearing (Galatians 6:2), he is accountable to brothers and sisters whose names he knows rather than to an anonymous congregation whose members nod at him in the lobby. The earliest Christian communities met in the spaces available to them — workshops, private homes, occasionally rented halls — without the institutional apparatus that most contemporary American pastors treat as the baseline for viable ministry. That kind of faith is portable. It does not close when the church building locks its doors for the last time.

This does not mean institutions are the problem. The church has always needed institutional forms — gathering places, sustained communities, shared resources, trained leaders, doctrinal accountability. The history of church institutions is not simply a history of co-optation and decline. Institutions are how movements survive across generations. They are how the tradition is maintained, how the community is formed, how the mission is coordinated.

The problem is not institution. The problem is institution that does not produce the formation it exists to produce — institution that has become an end rather than a means, whose continuation has become the goal rather than the mission it was created to serve. That kind of institution will close, and the people it contained will scatter, and no one will be equipped to gather them again because the institution never taught them how to gather themselves.

---

There is a counter-position worth engaging honestly. It says: the closure of small, theologically marginal, or culturally isolated congregations is not a crisis. It is a natural pruning. The denominations and church forms that are declining are often declining because they failed to maintain theological integrity or cultural relevance. Their closure makes room for new forms that are better suited to the current moment. The Spirit is always doing something new, and the anxiety about institutional decline is often the anxiety of people who have confused the form of the church with the church itself.

This has some truth to it. Not every closure is a tragedy. Some churches should close because they have become obstacles to the mission rather than servants of it — because the cultural form they embody is a barrier to the Gospel reaching the people in their immediate vicinity, or because the internal dynamics have become so corrupted that the community is producing damage rather than formation.

But the argument from pruning can become a way of avoiding the harder question. If the closures are primarily producing pruning — removing forms that have outlived their usefulness — then the communities that survive should be demonstrating stronger formation, deeper discipleship, more missionally capable congregants. The evidence for that is not strong. What the numbers suggest is not a healthy pruning but a broad institutional contraction in which the people exiting are not relocating to better-formed communities. They are simply exiting.

---

This means the pastoral response to the closure crisis is not primarily structural. It is formational. The pastor who wants to matter to the mission of the church in America in the next generation will not be measured by his attendance numbers. He will be measured by whether the people he shaped can still pray when there is no one to lead them, still gather when there is no building to gather in, still preach the Gospel when there is no pulpit to stand behind.

That formation is slow. It resists the metrics we use. It does not show up in the church growth report. The pastor who shifts his attention from institutional metrics to formational ones will spend years doing work that he cannot easily defend to a board looking at attendance trends.

But it is the only work that actually addresses the crisis rather than managing its surface.

I am not certain I have done this. I am not certain the people in my congregation could sustain their faith and their community without the infrastructure I provide. That uncertainty matters. It is the question that should be driving my pastoral decisions this year, not the question of how to grow the attendance numbers.

The church that is serious about the mission must become serious about the question: What are we actually forming?

Are we forming consumers who need our product, or disciples who carry the Gospel?

The answer to that question will determine whether the institution closes and the mission dies with it, or whether the institution closes — as institutions eventually do — and the mission continues because it was never actually located in the institution.

---

There is a pastoral practice that grows out of this reckoning, and it is deceptively simple: start treating the small gathering as the unit of formation rather than the large gathering. The Sunday morning service is visible and measurable and satisfying to institutional metrics. The small gathering — the dinner table, the accountability group, the two or three gathered in Jesus's name (Matthew 18:20) — is not. It does not appear in the annual report. It does not justify the building's square footage. It does not impress the denomination's regional leadership.

But it is where people are actually formed. It is where the text meets the specific life of a specific person, where accountability has a face and a name, where the mutual burden-bearing that Paul describes in Galatians 6:2 can actually happen between people who know each other's actual burdens. The large gathering is the occasion; the small gathering is the formation. The church that invests its pastoral energy in the large gathering and treats the small gathering as an optional supplement is investing backward.

The pastor who wants to matter when the institutions are contracting must spend his energy building the thing that survives the contraction. That thing is not the program. It is not the building. It is the community of people who are accountable to each other in Christ — who know how to pray together without him, who know how to read the text together without a professional's guidance, who know how to carry grief together and hope together and sustain each other's faith when the institutional scaffolding comes down.

This is the form of Christian community that has survived every persecution, every institutional collapse, every cultural shift that made public Christianity costly or unavailable. It is the form that met in the catacombs, in the fields, in the house churches of the first century and the house churches of the persecuted church today. It does not close. It cannot be closed. It is the thing that cannot be taken by the mechanisms that take buildings.

I am not certain I have built it. I am certain I need to.

The church that is serious about the mission must become serious about the question: What are we actually forming?

Are we forming consumers who need our product, or disciples who carry the Gospel?

The answer to that question will determine whether the institution closes and the mission dies with it, or whether the institution closes — as institutions eventually do — and the mission continues because it was never actually located in the institution.

Everything else is maintenance.

And maintenance ends.
