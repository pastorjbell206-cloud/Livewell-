The neighborhood your church occupies was made. Not by accident, not by natural aggregation, not by the invisible hand of preference and proximity. It was made by federal policy, by real estate covenants, by lending practices, by zoning laws, and — in many cases — by the silence or active participation of the American church. The segregated geography of American cities is not a neutral feature of the terrain. It is the residue of decisions made by human beings who could have decided differently. Some of those human beings sat in pews, and some of them stood behind pulpits.

That is where we have to start.

---

The history is specific enough to require specificity. Between 1934 and 1968, the Federal Housing Administration implemented a set of mortgage insurance policies that effectively excluded Black Americans and other minorities from the government-backed homeownership programs that built the American middle class. [cite — confirm] The Home Owners' Loan Corporation created "residential security maps" — color-coded assessments of credit risk in American cities — in which neighborhoods with significant Black populations were colored red, indicating minimal credit-worthiness. [cite — confirm] The maps were used by banks and the FHA to deny mortgage insurance to properties in those neighborhoods, regardless of the individual borrower's financial standing.

The effects compounded across generations. White families who purchased homes in the 1940s and 1950s with FHA-backed mortgages built wealth in ways that were explicitly unavailable to their Black neighbors. The Federal Highway System, authorized in 1956, was routed through urban neighborhoods with the explicit or tacit goal of displacing Black communities and creating physical barriers between them and whiter, wealthier areas. [cite — confirm] Urban renewal programs in the same decades destroyed functional Black neighborhoods — Tulsa's Greenwood District had already been burned in 1921; cities across the country accomplished the same destruction with bulldozers and the language of progress. [cite — confirm]

The result, by the late 1960s, was a geography that looked natural but was designed. The white suburb. The Black inner city. The structural gap between them in property values, school funding, municipal services, environmental quality, and access to capital. That geography persists in most American cities with remarkable fidelity to the redlined maps drawn nearly a century ago.

---

What was the church doing during these decades?

Some churches were complicit in explicit ways. Racially restrictive covenants — legal agreements that prohibited the sale of property to Black buyers — were commonly supported by white religious communities protecting their neighborhoods. Some congregations actively organized to exclude Black residents from their neighborhoods and then preached the gospel on Sunday morning without apparent awareness of the contradiction. Some denominational bodies offered theological cover for segregation — the Southern Baptist Convention was founded in 1845 on a defense of slaveholding, [cite — confirm] and its successor institutions carried the weight of that founding well into the twentieth century.

Other churches were complicit in the quieter way. They did not draft the covenants or lobby for the FHA policies. They simply organized their congregational life around the segregated geography the policies produced. The white church in the white suburb did not ask how the suburb came to be. The Black church in the redlined neighborhood carried the community through decades of disinvestment and maintained the social fabric of a neighborhood that every institutional force was abandoning. That asymmetry — the Black church bearing the cost of segregation while the white church benefited from it — is not a detail. It is the structure of the situation.

There were exceptions. The Civil Rights Movement drew on the prophetic tradition of the Black church and produced — in moments — a witness from some white congregations that moved against the tide. The courage of those exceptions should be named. It should not be used to obscure the weight of the general pattern.

I am a white pastor. I serve in a denomination that has a particular history with these questions. I do not stand outside this indictment. I stand inside it.

---

The theological problem with redlining is not only that it was unjust — though it was, flagrantly and deliberately so. The theological problem is that it produced a geography that has shaped the church's imagination about what the community of God's people looks like. When the church's default image of its own congregation is racially homogeneous, and when that homogeneity was produced by policies designed to produce it, the church is not simply reflecting culture. It is embodying the fruit of injustice and calling it normal.

The New Testament vision of the ekklesia is not racially homogeneous. Paul's argument in Ephesians 2 is precisely that the wall of hostility between Jew and Gentile has been torn down by the cross — that the community constituted by Christ is a new humanity, not a collection of existing human categories. The reconciliation between groups that history has divided is not a secondary implication of the gospel. It is, for Paul, one of its primary evidences. The community that refuses that reconciliation is not a minor version of the church. It is a contradiction of what Paul says the church is.

This has structural implications that are uncomfortable to sit with. If the segregated congregation is, at least in part, the product of segregated policy — if the all-white church in the suburb exists where it is and is who it is because of federal lending practices and racial covenants — then the church's continued comfort with that segregation is not merely cultural passivity. It is ongoing participation in the fruit of injustice, even if no one in the current congregation endorsed the policy that produced it.

The beneficiaries of an injustice are implicated in it even if they did not author it. That is a hard sentence. I am trying to say it honestly rather than soften it into something easier to hear.

---

The counter-position deserves direct engagement. Some will argue: the church did not make those policies, and the church cannot undo them by reorganizing its congregational demographics. Structural problems require structural solutions, and the church is not primarily a political institution. The call to congregational integration does not address the underlying economic and policy failures. This is partly right. The church cannot solve redlining by diversifying its Sunday attendance, and the performance of diversity without actual redistribution of power and resources is its own kind of failure. The point is not that the church should add some Black members to its programs and declare the work done. That is what Robert Woodson has called "diversity tourism." [cite — confirm]

But the argument proves too much if it is used as permission to do nothing. The church that acknowledges the structural problem and uses that acknowledgment as cover for continuing its own comfortable segregation has achieved sophistication without repentance. The acknowledgment of structural causes does not release the individual from the obligation to respond.

What does an honest response look like? It begins with the history. Not the general acknowledgment that racism is bad, but the specific reckoning: this church is here because of these policies. These neighborhoods are as they are because of these decisions. The wealth in this building was accumulated by families who had access to programs explicitly denied to their Black neighbors. This is not guilt as an end in itself. This is diagnosis as the prerequisite for repair.

---

Repair — the Hebrew concept of *tikkun* — is not a one-time act. It is a posture that requires ongoing institutional commitment. For the church, it might look like deliberate partnership with Black-led congregations that have been doing the work the white church avoided — not the token alliance, but the kind of relationship that requires presence, listening, and submitting to the leadership of people who know the neighborhood from the inside. It might look like the redirection of charitable giving toward organizations rooted in the neighborhoods that redlining devastated — organizations led by people who live in those neighborhoods, not organizations that serve them from a safe distance. It might look like advocacy for the housing and lending policies that continue to perpetuate the geography redlining created. It might look like examining who holds power within the church itself — on the board, in the paid staff, in the decision-making rooms — and whether that distribution reflects the theology the church claims.

It looks, at minimum, like honesty. Like the congregation that knows its own history well enough to name what was done and to ask what faithfulness requires in light of it.

Jeremiah told the exiles to pray for the city they were in. But you cannot pray honestly for a city you do not know. And you cannot know a city if you have organized your life so completely around one corner of it — one carefully constructed corner — that the rest of it remains invisible to you.

The neighborhood your church occupies was made. The question is what you are going to do with the knowledge.

That question has not yet been answered by most of us. It is still waiting.
