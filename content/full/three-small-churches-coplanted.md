The conventional wisdom about church planting runs in a single direction: a sending church, large enough to absorb the cost, releases a team and a planter and carries the financial weight of the first several years. That model works. It requires a church large enough to give away tens of thousands of dollars annually while absorbing the departure of key families and volunteer leaders. It requires a pastor secure enough in his own ecclesiological identity to raise up a leader he is willing to release. And it is unavailable to the vast majority of churches in America, which are under two hundred people, run close to the financial edge already, and led by pastors who are doing everything alone.

What happens when three churches that cannot afford to plant alone decide to plant together?

That is not a theoretical question. The Pastors Connection Network has seen versions of this arrangement take shape in communities across the Midwest, and what those arrangements reveal about the nature of the church is at least as interesting as what they reveal about church planting strategy.

The first thing that happens when small churches attempt a collaborative plant is that every assumption about ownership has to be renegotiated. A single sending church knows who is in charge. The lead pastor of the sending church carries the authority, and the planter is accountable to him. Three churches sharing a plant means three pastors, three governing structures, three sets of expectations about what success looks like — and no obvious single authority. The negotiation of that shared governance is difficult and revealing. It is also, ironically, precisely the kind of difficulty that the church needs to practice.

Paul's description of the body in 1 Corinthians 12 is not a description of how organisms work under a single organizational head. It is a description of how differently gifted communities need each other — how the eye cannot say to the hand, "I have no need of you" (1 Corinthians 12:21). Three small churches bringing different strengths to a shared plant begin to practice that interdependence in ways that the single sending church never has to. The practice is uncomfortable. It is also formative.

Consider what typically differentiates small churches from each other in the same city. One church brings financial stability but no young families — it is aging in place, giving faithfully, and aware that it does not have the demographics to sustain itself indefinitely. One church brings young families but no financial margin — it is growing but scraping. One church brings a neighborhood presence in exactly the place where the plant needs to take root but no capacity for anything beyond its own Sunday morning. Together they have something. Separately they have nothing but good intentions and a common problem.

The plant that emerges from that collaboration carries a different genetic material than the plant that emerges from a single strong sender. It is accountable to multiple bodies. Its planter navigates three sets of pastoral relationships instead of one. The governance is more complicated. The decision-making is slower. And the congregation that forms — if the collaboration holds — has been planted into a posture of inter-church dependence from its origin. That posture is very hard to teach to a church that has never practiced it. It is the native disposition of a church born from a collaborative act.

There is also something the collaborative plant does to the sending churches themselves. The church that releases a family or a tithe-unit or a key volunteer toward a shared plant has made a theological statement about its own purpose. It has said, with its budget and its people, that the gospel advancing in this city matters more than its own institutional stability. That is not a small thing. Most small churches are functioning in a moderate degree of self-protective anxiety — wondering whether they have enough, whether they will survive another decade, whether the next pastor will stay. The church that gives away something it cannot easily afford in order to participate in a shared mission has reoriented itself. The anxiety does not disappear, but it has been interrupted by an act of genuine faith.

This is not metaphorical. It is practical and observable. The churches that participate in collaborative plants tend to describe a renewal of their own sense of purpose that neither could generate alone. Being part of something larger than yourself is not just a spiritual principle. It is a sociological one — communities that are organized around something beyond their own survival tend to be healthier than communities that are organized around their own continuation.

The steel-man of the conventional sending model is legitimate and should be heard. Clear authority produces clear accountability. When one church sends and one pastor is in charge, decisions get made cleanly, conflicts get resolved through a single chain of authority, and the planter knows exactly who he answers to. Three-way governance produces real friction — three sets of expectations about worship style, about theological emphasis, about ministry priorities, about what "success" looks like in year three. That friction can be generative, but it can also be paralyzing. The collaborations that fail tend to fail along exactly these lines: a governance vacuum that nobody wanted to fill, theological differences that were papered over in the early enthusiasm, the gradual calcification of the partnership into a transactional arrangement where the churches are contributors rather than co-laborers.

What makes these collaborations survive is harder to name precisely. The arrangements that hold seem to share one characteristic: the pastors involved have submitted to something larger than their own church's brand. They have decided, at some level, that the plant succeeds if the gospel advances — not if their particular congregation gets credit for it. That posture is not natural to most pastoral leaders, who have significant ego invested in their own congregation's identity and are doing ministry in contexts where comparative success is visible and constantly evaluated.

The posture requires friendship. Not professional collegiality — actual friendship between pastors, the kind that requires regular conversation, shared meals, honest disagreement, and the willingness to say the hard things that institutional partners do not say to each other. The Pastors Connection Network exists, in part, because that kind of friendship between pastors does not happen by accident. It requires structure — a regular gathering, a committed group, the expectation that real things will be said rather than managed presentations of pastoral competence. Collaborative church planting requires the same kind of relationship between the sending churches' pastors.

It also requires humility about failure. Some collaborative plants do not survive. The governance collapses. The planter leaves after two years. One of the sending churches withdraws its support when a key family moves away and the giving drops. The shared vision that animated the early partnership turns out to have had different practical implications for different churches, and the differences were not negotiated honestly at the beginning. That failure is painful. It does not prove that collaboration was a mistake. It proves that collaboration is hard enough to require more than good intentions and enthusiasm to sustain it.

The three churches that plant together and survive have learned something about the body of Christ that cannot be taught in a church planting conference. They have discovered that something they believed abstractly — that the church is one body with many members, that no single congregation constitutes the whole — is actually true in a way that costs something and requires real discipline. They have practiced the theology of the body rather than merely reading it.

Ephesians 4:3 commands the church to "make every effort to keep the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace." The language is not passive. Every effort. Not hope for unity, or appreciate unity, or acknowledge unity as a good in principle. Make every effort to keep it. The collaborative church plant is an attempt to do exactly that — to act, at real cost and with real difficulty, on the claim that the church is one body.

That is not a small act. In a church culture that has learned to compete and to brand and to measure success by the growth of individual institutions, a collaborative plant is a counter-testimony. It says that the mission matters more than the credit. It says that the gospel advancing in this city is worth more than the name on the sign.

The three churches that plant together are practicing something the American church desperately needs to recover.

They are practicing it in public, with their money and their people.

That witness is worth more than the church they started.

There is a final word for the pastor who reads this and thinks his church is too small, too limited, too financially marginal to participate in planting anything. The churches in this account did not have excess capacity. They had commitment to something larger than themselves and enough trust between their pastors to try. The capacity was created by the commitment, not the other way around. The resources came because the decision came first.

This is the pattern of most of what God asks the church to do. Not: accumulate enough first, then act. Act first, and discover what becomes possible. The widow's jar of flour that does not run out (1 Kings 17:14-16) does not fill up before Elijah arrives. It fills as they eat from it, one meal at a time. The collaborative plant that waits for the conditions to be right before it begins will wait forever. The collaborative plant that begins with commitment and genuine relationship and the willingness to figure it out together will discover that what it needed was present all along — distributed across three congregations, none of whom had enough alone.
