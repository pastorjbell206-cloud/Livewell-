Not everyone who has had an abortion is at peace with it. Some women grieve the loss for years — in silence, largely alone, because the culture around them is divided into two camps that together exhaust the available options while between them leaving out the most important one.

One camp says there is nothing to grieve. The pregnancy was a collection of cells. The decision was healthcare. Grief is an import from a moral framework the person has presumably rejected. To grieve is to concede the opposition's premise. So the grief must be managed, minimized, or explained away.

The other camp says the grief is the appropriate punishment for a moral failure. The grief is what you get. The grief proves what the camp already believed. To offer comfort in the midst of it would be to soften the lesson it was meant to teach.

Neither camp offers what the grief actually needs. And the church — which should be neither camp — has too often chosen one of them.

## What Grief Requires and Does Not Require

Grief does not require a clean moral category. That is the most important thing to understand about this particular grief, and the most consistently misunderstood.

You can grieve a decision you believe was necessary. You can grieve a loss you believe was the right call. You can hold the complexity of having done the only thing you thought you could do and still mourn what that thing cost. Human beings are not required to be simple about their own pain.

The woman who grieved her abortion does not need to be assigned to a moral category before her grief is received. She is not asking whether her grief proves the pro-life position or the pro-choice position. She is not asking for a theological verdict. She is experiencing a loss — the specific, irreversible loss of a specific life that was part of her body and her future — and she needs a place to bring that loss that does not immediately convert it into evidence for someone else's argument.

Both camps want her grief for their purposes. The pro-life camp wants it as a confirmation of moral law. The pro-choice camp wants it managed away as a product of false consciousness. Both responses are a form of violence against the grief itself — a refusal to let it be what it is.

## The Psalms of Lament as Pastoral Theology

The Psalms of lament are not poems written by people who had everything sorted out. Psalm 22 opens with "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" — a question directed at God that does not have a tidy answer and is not followed by one. The psalmist does not resolve the abandonment by the end of the poem; he moves through it, arrives somewhere different, but does not pretend the opening cry was not real.

Psalm 88 ends in darkness: "Darkness is my closest friend." There is no resolution. The psalm ends exactly where the psalmist is. The tradition of Scripture preserved this psalm not as a failure of faith but as its own legitimate form of it — the faith that brings what is real into the presence of God without demanding that God immediately make it presentable.

The lament tradition in Scripture is premised on the conviction that God can receive the full weight of what a person is carrying, including the parts that are tangled and contradictory and ugly. Lament is not the absence of faith. It is the form that faith takes when the situation does not admit of praise.

The grief of abortion is a form of lament. It carries the complexity of loss and choice and aftermath and unanswered questions. It does not resolve cleanly into gratitude or contrition. It is a real wound that deserves a real tradition for receiving it — and the church has one. It has just been reluctant to apply it here.

## What the Church Has Often Done Instead

The church has sometimes made the grief worse by making the silence more necessary. The pastoral pattern that has produced the most damage is not hostile rejection — it is the combination of public certainty about the moral category and private unavailability for the actual experience. The pastor who preaches clearly about the sanctity of life in the pulpit and has no infrastructure for the person in the pew who is carrying the grief of having violated it has not been pastorally consistent. He has been theologically consistent and pastorally absent.

I have been that pastor. I have preached the conviction without creating the conditions under which the person most implicated by the conviction could bring her actual life into the room. The sermon was right. The pastoral architecture around it was incomplete. That is a distinction worth naming, because it is available to be corrected in ways that the conviction itself is not.

The church has also sometimes weaponized the grief — used the documented psychological difficulty that some women experience after abortion as evidence for the anti-abortion argument, in a way that converts the person's suffering into a rhetorical instrument. This is a specific pastoral harm. The woman whose grief is appropriated to make a point about policy has not been served by the church. She has been used by it. The difference is not subtle.

## What She Actually Needs

What the woman grieving an abortion needs from the church is not a verdict. She has usually delivered one to herself already, often far harsher than any pastoral pronouncement could reach. The self-indictment that lives in the silence is not made gentler by an external one added to it.

She needs what the Psalms model: a place to be honest about the loss. A community that will not require her to tidy it up before she brings it in — that will not send her away to process it privately and return when she is ready to be coherent. She needs a God who is described, again and again, as "near to the brokenhearted" (Psalm 34:18) and a community that embodies rather than merely announces that nearness.

She needs time. Grief does not follow a schedule. The church's pastoral attention tends to peak at the moment of crisis and drop as the urgency fades, but grief has its own timeline, and the woman still carrying this five years later deserves the same presence as the woman who arrived yesterday.

She needs, eventually, the possibility of something like peace — not the peace that denies the loss, but the peace that the New Testament describes as "surpassing understanding" (Philippians 4:7), the peace that co-exists with the memory of what happened rather than requiring the erasure of it. That peace is not produced by a program or a counseling model. It is produced by years of honest relationship with God and with a community that knows the real story.

## The Counter-Position and Its Pastoral Cost

The counter-position is that extending pastoral care to the grief of abortion risks communicating that the church regards the abortion as a normal loss, like a miscarriage, rather than a moral act with consequences. That creating a space for grief without a theological frame around it produces a kind of grief that never arrives at repentance and therefore never arrives at genuine healing. That the pastoral instinct to comfort can short-circuit the prophetic instinct to name, and that the woman genuinely needs both.

This argument is not without merit. The grief of abortion is not identical to the grief of miscarriage, and treating them as equivalent collapses a distinction that matters. Repentance and healing are not enemies of each other; in the Christian tradition, they are the path to each other. A pastoral response that offers comfort while leaving the deeper question permanently unaddressed has not served the whole person.

But the argument fails in its sequencing. The person in active grief cannot receive a theological frame before she has been received. The attempt to lead with repentance when the person has not yet been welcomed produces defensiveness, not contrition. It confirms the silence she already knew was the safer choice. The pastoral wisdom of the New Testament — the father running toward the returning son before the son has delivered his prepared speech (Luke 15:20), Jesus asking the woman at the well about her life before naming its difficulty (John 4) — is about sequence. Welcome before instruction. Presence before direction. The theological frame has a place. It is not first.

## The Door That Is Still Closed

The church has not always been the place this grief can be brought. It has sometimes made the grief worse by making the silence more necessary. That is a failure worth naming — not to assign blame for the past, but because the people still carrying this in silence deserve to know that the door is open.

Not the door to a verdict. Not the door to a theology lesson. The door to the thing the Gospel actually offers: the presence of God with people in the middle of their worst moments, before they have cleaned themselves up, before they have composed themselves, before they have anything to offer except the weight they are carrying and the hope that someone in this building can actually hold it.

That door can be opened. It requires a pastor who has created the conditions for it. It requires a congregation that has learned, over time, what it means to receive hard things without immediately reaching for a category to put them in.

The grief is real. It is still being carried, in buildings all over this country, by women who have learned that silence is the price of belonging. They deserve better than that price.

That weight remains, unresolved, asking what you will do next.
