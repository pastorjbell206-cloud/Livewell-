The Greek word is *metanoia*. It does not mean feeling sorry. It means turning around — a reorientation of mind and will so fundamental that the direction of your life changes. John the Baptist used it as the entire content of his preaching before Jesus arrived: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is near" (Matthew 3:2). Not "improve." Not "reflect." Turn around.

Most pastors preach this with conviction. Fewer of us practice it with regularity.

---

There is a particular pressure inside pastoral ministry that makes repentance difficult. You are supposed to be the one who has things together. You are the guide, the shepherd, the one with theological clarity and spiritual stability. The congregation does not say this explicitly, but the role carries the expectation, and the expectation becomes a wall between you and genuine repentance. To repent means to acknowledge that you were wrong. And the pastor who has built his authority on competence — on being right, on knowing — finds this deeply threatening.

The threat is real. There are congregations where a pastor's public acknowledgment of failure would be used as a weapon against him. There are church cultures where weakness is not safe to display, where the pastoral office is so thoroughly identified with strength and certainty that genuine vulnerability becomes an institutional liability. I am not dismissing that reality. It exists.

But the pressure to appear unrepentant is not primarily external. It is internal. It is the deep human instinct toward self-preservation that the gospel has been confronting since the garden — the instinct that reaches for fig leaves rather than standing naked before God, that blames the woman and then blames the serpent rather than acknowledging plainly what was done. The pastor who cannot repent is not primarily prevented from repentance by a harsh congregation. He is prevented by the same thing that prevented Adam: the fear of what it costs to be seen accurately.

---

The history of Christianity's greatest figures is, strikingly, a history of people who were willing to name what they had done.

Augustine's *Confessions* — written at the end of the fourth century and still being read sixteen hundred years later — opens with an adult man accounting for the sins of his childhood and adolescence with a specificity that no image-management strategy would permit. The theft of the pears from the neighbor's orchard. The sexual wandering. The years of proud intellectual searching that kept him from the God he was made for. He does not soften these. He does not contextualize them into understandability. He names them because the naming is the beginning of the healing.

What the *Confessions* is, fundamentally, is one long sustained act of *metanoia* committed to writing — a turning, public and permanent, that models for every reader what it looks like to stop managing the story of your life and begin telling the truth of it.

Martin Luther's reforming insight — *simul justus et peccator*, simultaneously righteous and sinner — was not only a theological proposition. It was a pastoral description of the ongoing condition of the Christian life. You are already declared righteous in Christ. You are still a sinner who needs daily repentance. The two are not resolved until the resurrection. The pastor who has internalized this will preach very differently than the pastor who believes the goal is to project the justified side while concealing the sinner. Luther's own repentance had urgency because he believed it was genuinely needed — not as a ritual repetition but as the actual daily orientation of a person who is, in fact, still falling.

---

I have led in ways that needed repentance — with pride, with a defensiveness that I dressed up as conviction, with a vision for the church that was partly about what I needed rather than what the people needed. I did not always name it in those terms quickly. I named it slowly, with considerable resistance, usually when something broke open — a conflict I could not manage around, a conversation that held up a mirror I could not look away from, a season where God went quiet in the way he goes quiet when you have been evading something.

The breaking open is not failure. It is invitation.

It is worth being specific about what the evasion looks like, because it is subtle enough to fool a person who is genuinely trying to follow Christ. Pastoral defensiveness often presents as theological conviction — as the pastor who "has to stand firm on this" when what he is actually doing is refusing to hear a correction. The protection of pastoral ego presents as the protection of pastoral authority, which is a real thing, and the confusion between the two can last for years without being named. I have confused them. I have defended things that deserved to fall, and called my refusal to let them fall a matter of principle.

The discipline of regular examination — the *examen* that the Jesuit tradition formalized but that runs through the whole of Christian spiritual direction — is one response to this. You review the day or the week with the specific question: where did I move away from God, away from the people I serve, away from what I know to be true? Not in order to generate guilt, but in order to generate honesty. The examination is not the punishment. It is the precondition for the turning.

---

David's prayer in Psalm 51 remains the most honest model of pastoral repentance in the canon. The backstory — the abuse of power in the affair with Bathsheba, the deliberate arrangement of Uriah's death, the cover-up that lasted until the prophet Nathan forced the confrontation — is one of the most serious failures of a leader in Scripture. And David's prayer does not manage it. He does not contextualize it. He does not offer mitigating factors. He prays: "Have mercy on me, O God, according to your unfailing love; according to your great compassion blot out my transgressions. Wash away all my iniquity and cleanse me from my sin" (Psalm 51:1-2).

And then the central request: "Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me" (Psalm 51:10). He does not ask God to help him do better. He does not ask God to manage the consequences. He asks for an interior transformation that he cannot produce himself — a new heart, the right spirit, the renewal that only God can accomplish after the acknowledgment that what was there before had become corrupt.

What David understood, and what the pastoral tradition has sometimes lost, is that repentance is not a crisis interruption. It is a continual posture. The daily need for a clean heart is not the exceptional need of the man who has committed adultery and murder. It is the need of every person who is honest about what lives in them — the small treacheries of pride, the minor dishonesties, the self-serving calculations that dress themselves as pastoral wisdom.

---

The pastor who repents regularly will preach repentance differently. Not from theory. Not from a position of moral altitude. From experience — from the actual knowledge of what it feels like to turn around, to name the thing accurately, to receive grace that was not earned and does not depend on getting it right. That experiential knowledge changes how the sermon lands. The congregation can tell the difference between a man who has been somewhere and a man who has read about it.

This is not a call to pastoral oversharing — to the confession of private sin as a preaching technique that generates emotional connection. The discipline of what to share publicly and what to carry in private with a confessor or a trusted colleague is real and must be maintained. The congregation does not need to know everything. But they do need to know that their pastor is a sinner who knows grace from the inside — not a polished professional who dispenses grace from a safe clinical distance.

There is also what repentance does structurally to a congregation's culture. The pastor who publicly acknowledges when he has been wrong — not performatively, but genuinely, specifically — gives the congregation permission to do the same. A church whose pastor repents is a church where repentance is possible. A church whose pastor cannot acknowledge failure is a church where failure must be hidden, and hidden failure is the seedbed of everything the church should fear. The great pastoral scandals are almost never sudden. They are the long accumulation of small hidden failures that were never named, never brought into the light, never turned around.

The light is not punishing. It is the condition for health.

---

Renewal follows repentance. Not immediately. Not on a schedule. But the pattern throughout Scripture is consistent: the people who turn receive the restoration that the people who manage never get. The father in Luke 15 does not rehabilitate the returning son. He throws a party. The restoration is out of proportion to the repentance, which is the entire theological point. Repentance is not a payment. It is a door.

The Psalms of ascent — the pilgrimage psalms, 120 through 134 — move from lament toward arrival, from the long road toward the house of God. The movement is never triumphant in the way that American therapeutic culture defines triumph. It is weary, honest, marked by the distance of the road already traveled. "Out of the depths I cry to you, LORD" (Psalm 130:1). But it arrives. The arrival is real, and it is received rather than achieved.

What it requires from a pastor is the willingness to stop managing his image before God and before the people he leads. To say what is actually true. To turn around when he has been facing the wrong direction.

This is not weakness. It is the most courageous thing a leader can do.

The congregation is watching, more closely than you know. They are watching not for whether you are perfect — they already know you are not — but for whether you know what to do when you are not. Whether the gospel you preach on Sunday has any purchase on the life you are actually living the rest of the week.

The pastor who repents will not lose the congregation's respect. He will gain something better: their trust.

And trust is the only foundation pastoral leadership has ever actually stood on.
