The safest sermon to preach is the one everyone agrees with in advance. The most dangerous is the one that names what the congregation would prefer not to name, confronts what they would prefer to leave unconfronted, and calls them to something that genuinely costs.

Most preachers have a sense of which topics they systematically avoid — the things that would generate pushback, that would make certain powerful members uncomfortable, that would require the preacher to have the harder conversation on Monday. Those avoidances are costing the congregation more than the feedback they spare the preacher. And they are costing the preacher something more fundamental than the feedback — they are costing the integrity of the call.

This is not a theoretical problem. It is the most common form of pastoral failure, and it is almost never recognized as failure because the sermon that avoids the costly thing still sounds good, still receives positive responses, still fulfills the formal requirements of a Sunday sermon. The congregation leaves satisfied. No one files a complaint. The pastoral evaluation comes back strong. And the deep work that the congregation needed — the word that would have unsettled something that needed to be unsettled — never arrived.

---

The biblical office of the watchman is the relevant theological framework.

Ezekiel was told: "Son of man, I have made you a watchman for the house of Israel. Whenever you hear a word from my mouth, you shall give them warning from me. If I say to the wicked, 'You shall surely die,' and you give him no warning, nor speak to warn the wicked from his wicked way, in order to save his life, that wicked person shall die for his iniquity, but his blood I will require at your hand" (Ezekiel 3:17-18).

The watchman's job is not to deliver the word the city wants to hear. It is to deliver the word the city needs to hear, at the cost of the watchman's own comfort and at risk to the watchman's own standing. The accountability is explicit: if the watchman fails to give the warning, the blood of the one who was not warned is on the watchman's hands. The silence is not neutral. It is a choice with consequences — consequences that are not primarily experienced by the preacher in the short term but by the congregation over time.

That framework puts the calculation in its proper terms. The preacher who avoids the costly sermon is not being cautious. They are being negligent — trading the congregation's formation for the preacher's comfort, offering the appearance of the office without the substance of its obligation.

---

The avoidance is systematic, which is what makes it so damaging.

One avoided sermon on one Sunday is a pastoral judgment call. The preacher who looks at the text and the room on a specific Sunday and decides that a different angle serves better — that is not cowardice; it is discernment. The problem is the pattern: the topics that are never addressed, the applications that are always softened, the congregational assumptions that are consistently affirmed rather than examined, the powerful members whose positions are never challenged.

The congregation learns this pattern faster than the preacher realizes. They learn it not by analyzing the sermons but by sensing the avoidances — the slightly different energy when the text is going somewhere uncomfortable, the pivot away from the implication, the application that could have gone further but did not. They notice. They may not be able to articulate what they are noticing, but it registers as a gap between the pulpit and the real, between what the preacher is willing to say and what the situation actually requires.

Over time, this gap produces a specific kind of pastoral authority deficit. The congregation begins, often unconsciously, to discount the preacher's voice — not the content, which may be excellent, but the weight. A preacher who has never said anything that cost them something is a preacher the congregation has not fully trusted, because they have not been shown what the preacher is willing to lose for the sake of the truth. The prophetic credibility is the credibility of the person who told the hard thing when there was a price for telling it.

---

Consider the specific categories where the avoidance most commonly lives.

Wealth and generosity. The congregation's economic behavior — the gap between average income and average giving, the financial decisions that reflect values other than the ones professed on Sunday, the way money is discussed in homes that attend services on the importance of generosity — is among the most sensitive territories in pastoral ministry. The preacher who addresses it specifically, who moves from the general principle to the actual implications for the people in the room, will receive the most direct pushback of any sermon topic. Jesus addressed it more than almost any other subject. Most pastors address it least.

Power and accountability. Congregations have power dynamics, and those dynamics often include people whose giving, tenure, or social influence has allowed them to establish a kind of informal veto over pastoral direction. Preaching that names the misuse of power, that addresses the dynamics of control and manipulation, that calls the congregation's most influential members to the same accountability as its most vulnerable — this preaching will create the most direct and personally targeted conflict the preacher experiences. Most avoid it entirely.

Sexuality. Not the easy version — the sermons that address sexual ethics in ways the congregation already agrees with — but the hard version. The sermon that addresses the congregation's actual sexual culture, including the behaviors that are widespread and quietly normalized, including the ways the church's public positions and its private realities diverge, including the genuine pastoral complexity that rigid positions often cannot hold. That sermon requires the preacher to risk accusation from multiple directions simultaneously, which is why it is almost never preached.

Racial and social justice. In the current moment, this territory has become a proxy war, and many preachers have responded to the proxy war by going silent. The silence is not neutrality. It is a position — a position that tells the congregation that this question, which the prophets addressed with relentless clarity, is not sufficiently important for the preacher to risk the conflict it would produce.

---

The steel-manned objection.

Some will argue that the pastoral relationship requires a specific kind of political intelligence — that the preacher who consistently chooses confrontation over diplomacy will eventually lose the platform that makes preaching possible, and a preacher without a platform can preach nothing to no one. The preacher who drives out the wealthy donors with sermons about wealth, who inflames the politically engaged members with sermons about power, who fractures the congregation with sermons on contested social issues — that preacher has not served the congregation. They have indulged themselves at the congregation's expense.

The argument has genuine weight. Courage without wisdom is not prophetic; it is self-destructive, and the self-destruction does not benefit the congregation. The pastor who consistently chooses the most inflammatory possible angle on every difficult topic, who mistakes unnecessary offense for prophetic boldness, who has not learned to speak the hard thing with the pastoral care that makes it hearable — that pastor is not a model.

But the argument proves too much if it becomes the permanent justification for systematic avoidance. The distinction that matters is between unnecessary confrontation and necessary confrontation — between the sermon that inflames for its own sake and the sermon that says the thing the text requires, with whatever pastoral care the relationship allows, and then accepts the consequence.

Bonhoeffer was not needlessly inflammatory. He was theologically precise, pastorally engaged, relational in his leadership. And he still said the things that cost him the approval of the majority of the German church, and ultimately cost him his life. [cite — confirm Bonhoeffer's final years and execution, 1945.] The question is not whether to be diplomatic. The question is whether the diplomacy is in service of the message or whether it has replaced the message.

---

I have felt this pull in my own preaching.

I have known what the text was going toward on a Thursday afternoon and spent the rest of my sermon preparation time finding the angle that said less of it. I have arrived at Sunday having softened the application in ways that were about my own comfort and called the softening pastoral sensitivity. I have let the powerful person's potential reaction be in the room with me while I was in the room with the text, and I have let that presence shape what I said.

It costs the congregation more than it costs me. That is the thing I have to keep telling myself.

---

A pastor in my acquaintance preaches to a congregation where the majority of the adult members are in financial positions that the New Testament's teaching on wealth directly addresses. He knows the text. He believes the text. He preaches around it with enough frequency that it has become a pattern, and the congregation has come to expect the pattern, and the giving has remained steady and the powerful members have remained satisfied and the pastoral relationship has remained comfortable.

And the congregation has been formed, year after year, in the confidence that their financial lives are substantially untouched by the gospel — that Christianity is a matter of inner transformation and Sunday practice and private morality, but that the economic life belongs to a different jurisdiction.

The text does not support that formation. Jesus was explicit about where the heart follows the treasure (Matthew 6:21). The early church sold possessions and distributed to any who had need (Acts 2:45). The epistle of James is the most economically direct document in the New Testament, and it is in the canon. The pastor who has not preached James in its full weight to a congregation of relative wealth has not done the job.

---

The congregation does not need a pastor who makes them comfortable. They need a pastor who tells them the truth — with love, with pastoral care, with the relational investment that makes the hard word hearable. But the truth, finally and fully, even when it costs.

The watchman who sees the danger and says nothing because the city might not want to hear it has not been kind to the city. They have been complicit in whatever follows.

That is the accountability. Name it honestly. Then preach accordingly.
