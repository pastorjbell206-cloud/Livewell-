Great preaching creates tension before it resolves it. The text creates a problem — about God, about human nature, about the gap between what is and what should be — and the sermon holds that tension long enough for it to do real work in the congregation before offering resolution.

Many preachers rush to resolution because sitting in homiletical tension is uncomfortable and generates less positive feedback than comfort does. The sermon that leaves the congregation unsettled — that names the problem without immediately offering the way through it, that holds the hard question open long enough for it to become genuinely difficult — will receive more ambivalent responses than the sermon that moves efficiently from problem to solution. Preachers who manage their feedback develop, over time, a systematic bias toward early resolution.

But the sermons that stay with people — that genuinely change them — are often the ones that held them in discomfort long enough for something to shift. Not discomfort for its own sake. Discomfort in service of the text's actual work.

---

The texts that form people most deeply do not resolve quickly. That is not accidental.

Psalm 88 is the only lament psalm in the Psalter that does not end in praise. It moves through fourteen verses of sustained darkness — "my soul is full of troubles," "I am shut in so that I cannot escape," "your wrath has swept over me" — and it ends in darkness: "darkness is my closest friend." [cite — confirm Psalm 88:18, exact translation.] No resolution. No turn. The psalm is in the canon because it is a true account of a real human experience that the people of God need to be able to pray — the experience of being genuinely lost inside suffering, without the immediate comfort of a turn toward light.

The preacher who cannot preach Psalm 88 without inserting a resolution that is not in the text has told the congregation, by the insertion, that that experience — darkness without immediate resolution — is not a legitimate resting place for faith. The person in the pew who is living in exactly that experience learns two things: first, that the Bible does not speak directly to their condition; second, that the church cannot hold what they are carrying. Both are false, but the premature resolution made them feel true.

Job holds the tension for forty-one chapters. The speeches of Job's friends are exactly the kind of efficient resolution the pastor who rushes to comfort would provide — they name the problem (suffering), offer the explanation (sin), and propose the solution (repent). They are theologically tidy and pastorally catastrophic, and God tells them at the end that they have not spoken rightly (Job 42:7). The resolution, when it comes, is not an explanation. It is an encounter — God speaking from the whirlwind, describing the creation in terms that make human questions about suffering seem differently sized, not answered but reframed by being placed inside a reality larger than Job had apprehended (Job 38-41).

That is the resolution Scripture offers for the deepest suffering: not explanation but encounter, not answers but a God large enough that the questions become differently weighted. The preacher who rushes to the efficient explanation has offered Job's friends' sermon when the text is asking them to take the congregation to the whirlwind.

---

The formation that happens in the tension is not available without the tension.

When a congregation is held inside a real problem — when the preacher has named something honestly and has not immediately rescued them from the naming — a specific kind of interior work becomes possible. The problem lands. It connects with the actual experience people carry into the room. It stops being an abstract theological issue and becomes a live thing, something with weight, something that generates a genuine need for what the gospel is going to say.

The resolution that follows is received differently. It is received as rescue, as word that arrives after real need has been established, as the thing the congregation actually requires rather than the thing they expected. The emotional and spiritual register is different from the resolution that arrives before the tension has had time to do its work — different the way food tastes different when you are genuinely hungry rather than eating by schedule.

This is not a technique. It is a natural consequence of taking the text seriously. The texts that form people were not designed with efficient resolution in mind. They were written by people who had been in the tension themselves — who had cried from the depths (Psalm 130:1), who had wrestled through the night (Genesis 32:24), who had sat by the rivers of Babylon without a song to sing (Psalm 137:1) — and who knew that the word from God that mattered was the word received after the full weight of the problem had been carried.

---

A concrete pastoral scene.

A woman in the congregation is in the second year of an illness that has not improved. She has prayed. She has been prayed for. She is not losing her faith but she is losing her certainty that God operates the way the sermons have implied — that faithfulness is followed, in timeframes that make practical sense, by the visible love of God in changed circumstances.

She comes to church on a Sunday when the text is Romans 8:28: "And we know that for those who love God all things work together for good." The preacher resolves the tension in the verse within four minutes. God is in control. The trials will have purpose. The outcome will be positive. Here are three things to remember when you are in the valley.

She leaves knowing the verse better. She leaves unchanged, and more alone, because the resolution arrived before the tension was allowed to be what it actually is — before the sermon was willing to hold the question that the verse raises: what do you do in the period between the affliction and the good that is supposed to come from it, which may be very long, which may outlast the life, which may be visible only from a vantage point you will not occupy on this side of the resurrection?

That question is in the passage, in the context of Romans 8. The chapter includes "the sufferings of this present time" (Romans 8:18), and the groan of creation and the groan of the Spirit (Romans 8:22-26), and the intercession of the Spirit for those who do not know how to pray because the pain has taken the words (Romans 8:26). The passage holds the tension for the full chapter before the declaration of Romans 8:38-39. The preacher who resolves it in four minutes has not preached Romans 8. They have extracted a verse from it and offered it as a comfort that the chapter was building to, not starting with.

---

The counter-position has pastoral weight.

Some congregants come to church in genuine crisis, and the last thing they need is a sermon that adds to their burden. The person who has been in the darkness all week does not need the church to increase the darkness on Sunday morning. They need the light — and the preacher who withholds it out of a commitment to holding tension longer is prioritizing craft over compassion.

That argument has real force in specific situations. There are pastoral moments — the Sunday after a community tragedy, the sermon during a specific congregational crisis — where the primary need is the word of comfort, and the preacher who insists on full homiletical tension before the comfort arrives has misread the room.

But the argument breaks down as a general principle. The person in crisis needs the comfort that is adequate to the crisis — which means the comfort that has been through the same territory, not around it. The most powerful pastoral word is not the word that never entered the darkness. It is the word that went all the way in and came out the other side carrying something. "For our light and momentary troubles are achieving for us an eternal glory that far outweighs them all" (2 Corinthians 4:17) — Paul writes this from inside the afflictions he described three verses earlier: hard pressed, perplexed, persecuted, struck down (2 Corinthians 4:8-9). The comfort has authority because it was earned in the darkness, not spoken from outside it.

The preacher who has been in the tension personally, and who preaches the comfort from inside the full weight of what it is answering, offers something entirely different from the preacher who moves efficiently to the resolution before the tension has been felt. The congregation can tell the difference. They may not be able to articulate it, but they feel it.

---

Learning to sit in the tension — as a preacher, not as a technique but as a practice — requires the preacher to develop tolerance for the discomfort of not resolving things. Many preachers are people who are constitutionally motivated to fix things, to find the answer, to move through difficulty toward clarity. That is a good impulse in many pastoral contexts. In the pulpit, it sometimes works against the text.

The disciplines that build this tolerance are mostly outside the pulpit. Sitting with Lamentations and not moving on too quickly. Sitting with the patients who are not getting better and not offering the platitude that insults the severity of what they are carrying. Sitting with the family after the funeral when there is nothing to say and not filling the silence with things designed to make the silence more bearable.

Those disciplines form a preacher who knows, in their body and not just in their theology, that there is something on the other side of the darkness. And that knowledge — not as information but as formation — is what produces preaching that can hold the tension long enough for it to do its work without drowning in it.

---

The sermon that genuinely changes people holds them in the gap long enough for the gap to feel real. The gap between who they are and who they are called to be. The gap between what they experience and what they have been promised. The gap between the God they were taught and the God they have encountered in the hours when the teaching did not hold.

That gap is not the enemy of faith. It is the place where faith develops — where the easy confidence that does not know what it costs gives way to the harder trust that has been tested and held.

The preacher who rushes past it is not being kind. They are denying the congregation the sermon they came for — the one that names the thing they have been carrying alone and holds it long enough that they know: this is the territory the gospel is built for.

End there sometimes. Not as a structural move, but because that is where the text ends, and the text knows what it is doing.
