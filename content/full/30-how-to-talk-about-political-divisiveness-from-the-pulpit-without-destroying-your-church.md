There is almost no pastoral challenge more fraught with relational risk in the current moment than the question of how the church should engage with political division. The polarization that has fractured American public life has not stopped at the church door. It has moved in, taken a seat in the pew, and is waiting to see what the pastor is going to do about it. Whatever the pastor does, some portion of the congregation will feel that it was too much in one direction or not enough in the other.

This is genuinely hard. There is no approach that satisfies everyone, and a pastoral strategy built primarily on satisfying everyone will end up satisfying no one and abdicating the prophetic responsibility of the pulpit in the process. But the goal is not to satisfy everyone. The goal is to lead people faithfully through one of the most difficult cultural moments the church has navigated in generations.

## The Historical Weight of the Moment

The church has been here before, though not in exactly this form. The antebellum church in America was riven by the slavery question in ways that eventually split every major denomination in the country — the Southern Baptist Convention was formed in 1845 precisely over the question of whether slaveholders could be missionaries [cite — confirm]. The German church in the 1930s faced a version of this question that was also, at its core, a question about whether the church would be captured by a political identity or remain free to speak from outside it. Karl Barth's Theological Declaration of Barmen in 1934 was written against German Christians who had accommodated the Nazi state, and its central claim — that Jesus Christ alone is the Lord of the church — was a directly political claim made against the political theology of the moment [cite — confirm].

These precedents are not exact analogies to the present situation, and the pastoral instinct to draw too-clean historical parallels should be resisted. But they establish something important: the question of how the church relates to political power is not a new one, and the temptations that pastors face in politically charged environments are not new either. The temptation to alignment and the temptation to silence have both appeared before. Both have led churches away from their calling. Both are available again today.

## The Temptations on Each Side

The temptation on one side is to align the church with a political identity — to become, in effect, a religious expression of a particular political tribe. This is the path of least resistance for pastors in politically homogeneous congregations, and it produces short-term unity at the cost of the church's prophetic independence and its capacity to speak to anyone outside the tribe. When the church becomes politically captured, it loses the very thing that makes it valuable to the people inside and outside its walls: the capacity to speak from a perspective that is genuinely other than the culture's prevailing options.

The sociologist Robert Bellah noted in his later work that the great danger for American religion was its tendency to become the religious ratification of American culture rather than its prophetic critic [cite — confirm]. That tendency — which Bellah traced across the full sweep of American religious history — reaches an extreme form when the church becomes the religious expression of a political party. The god of that church is no longer the God of the Scriptures. It is the political tribe's idealized self-image dressed in theological language.

The temptation on the other side is to retreat into a false neutrality — to refuse any engagement with political realities in the name of keeping the peace. This is sometimes called "not being political," but it is itself a political act. Silence in the face of injustice is a posture. Refusal to speak to the moral dimensions of political questions is a choice, and it is a choice that has consequences for the church's integrity and its relationship to the communities it serves.

Lesslie Newbigin argued that there is no such thing as a gospel that has no public implications [cite — confirm]. The claim that Jesus is Lord is a claim about the ordering of all things — about power, about the treatment of the poor, about justice and mercy and the dignity of human beings made in the image of God. A church that preaches this gospel without ever applying it to the publicly contested questions of its day has not achieved neutrality. It has achieved irrelevance, which is a different failure but a failure nonetheless.

## A Steel-Manned Counter-Position

The strongest version of the case for pastoral restraint in political matters is not cowardice but a genuine concern for pastoral authority. The argument goes: a pastor who is perceived as politically partisan loses credibility with the members of the congregation who belong to the other political tribe, and in losing that credibility, loses the capacity to pastor them. The preacher who has become a political commentator has destroyed the one thing that makes the pastoral office distinctive — the authority that comes from being seen as a person who serves God rather than a party.

There is wisdom in this. The pastor who cannot be trusted to hold the congregation's political diversity with genuine care — who regularly signals their own political loyalties from the pulpit in ways that make members of the other party feel judged rather than pastored — has damaged their effectiveness in ways that are not easily repaired. Pastoral authority is relational. It is built over years and can be eroded in a single sermon.

But the answer to this concern is not to say nothing. It is to say the right things in the right way. The pastor who engages the moral dimensions of political questions while refusing the partisan framing of those questions has not surrendered pastoral authority. They have exercised it in its truest form: by calling the congregation to a standard that transcends the options the political culture provides.

## What the Pulpit Actually Owes the Congregation

What the congregation deserves from the pulpit is not political analysis, not partisan commentary, and not the pastor's personal political preferences dressed up in theological language. What they deserve is the consistent, faithful application of the Scriptures' witness to the questions of justice, power, the common good, and the dignity of every human being — applied with enough specificity to be genuinely relevant and enough care for the full range of the congregation to be genuinely pastoral.

Amos spoke against the economic exploitation of the poor with a specificity that made the comfortable deeply uncomfortable: "They sell the righteous for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals — they trample the heads of the poor into the dust of the earth and turn aside the way of the afflicted" (Amos 2:6-7). That is not a partisan statement. It is a prophetic one. It does not endorse a candidate or a party. It applies the justice of God to the conditions of a specific society with enough precision to be genuinely challenging.

This means naming moral realities without endorsing candidates. It means addressing systemic injustice without adopting any particular political party's framing of the problem or the solution. It means holding the congregation's political diversity with enough pastoral care that people across the spectrum feel genuinely cared for, while being clear that the gospel makes demands that no political identity can fully satisfy.

## A Concrete Scene

I know a pastor who leads a congregation in a politically divided suburban church — roughly split between families whose political identities are deeply held on both sides. In the summer of 2020, he preached through Romans 13 and Romans 12 together: the passage about submitting to governing authorities alongside the passage about being transformed by the renewing of the mind, not conformed to the pattern of this world. He did not tell his congregation how to vote. He did tell them that Christians were called to love neighbors who did not share their political assumptions, that the church's primary loyalty was not to either of the options the culture was offering, and that the peace of the congregation was not more important than the truth of the gospel.

He lost three families that month. He has not recovered them. He told me he does not expect to. He also told me that the remaining congregation is more deeply bonded than it was before — that something about having been willing to name the hard thing together, without taking political sides, had produced a trust that sermons designed to avoid offense had never produced.

## Practical Approaches That Help

Several practical approaches have proven helpful for pastors navigating this terrain. First: establish the authority of Scripture clearly and consistently, so that when you engage difficult topics, the congregation understands that you are trying to be faithful to a text, not to a party. Second: engage specific passages that speak to justice, power, and the common good rather than offering general political commentary — let the Scripture lead. Third: model genuine humility about the policy applications of biblical principles, acknowledging that faithful people can and do disagree about specific policy questions while sharing the same underlying moral commitments.

And fourth: pastor the congregation across the political divide with genuine care for the actual people in the room. The person who feels that their political community is the one the pastor is critiquing needs to feel, nonetheless, that the pastor loves them and respects their genuine attempts at faithfulness. That pastoral care is what creates the safety for the prophetic word to be received rather than rejected.

## What the Church Owes the Culture

The congregation that emerges from this kind of preaching — genuinely committed to the justice of the Scriptures, genuinely free from partisan capture, genuinely holding its political diversity with care — is a witness in itself. It is a community that the culture around it cannot easily categorize, and the difficulty of categorization is itself a form of testimony.

The church is not useful to the world as a religious expression of either party. It is useful to the world as a community organized around a different set of loyalties — as a foretaste of the peaceable kingdom, where the old divisions do not have the last word.

That is a hard thing to sustain from the pulpit. The forces pushing toward capture — from both directions — are relentless. But the pastor who keeps the church free is doing some of the most important work in the American church right now.

The political parties will survive without the church's endorsement. The gospel will not survive being confused with either of them.
