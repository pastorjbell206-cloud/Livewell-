In 1934, Karl Barth gathered with other German pastors at Barmen and produced the Barmen Declaration — a theological document that crossed Lutheran and Reformed lines to say a single clear thing: Jesus Christ alone is Lord, and the German church would not confess the Nazi state as a second source of revelation alongside Scripture.

The Confessing Church that emerged from Barmen was not uniform. The men who signed that declaration disagreed with each other on baptism, on church governance, on eucharistic theology. They had been separated by four centuries of institutional division rooted in the bitterest conflicts the Reformation produced. What they discovered at Barmen was that when the essential thing is under attack, the things that divide become secondary not by being dismissed but by being properly ordered. The confession that Jesus is Lord is prior to the disagreement about how baptism should be administered. Crisis clarified the hierarchy.

I want that clarity without requiring crisis to produce it.

## The History We Are Inside

Denominationalism as we know it in America has a specific history, and that history is worth knowing because it explains what we are actually navigating. The great denominational divisions of Western Christianity — Catholic and Protestant at the Reformation, Lutheran and Reformed within Protestantism, Anglican and Nonconformist in England, the Baptist and Presbyterian splits in America, the Methodist movement under Wesley — were not primarily about personal preference or institutional competition. They were about genuine theological disagreements that people were willing to die over.

The question of how a sinner is justified before God — by faith alone, through the imputed righteousness of Christ, or by grace working through the sacramental system of the church — was not a minor adjustment. People were burned at Smithfield over it. The question of whether infants could receive the covenant sign of baptism, or whether baptism belonged only to those who confessed faith, divided communities that shared Scripture, shared prayer, and shared the Lord's table. John Calvin and Ulrich Zwingli disagreed so sharply about the nature of the Eucharist at the Marburg Colloquy of 1529 [cite — confirm] that they could not reach agreement, and their traditions have not shared the table since.

I do not say this to minimize the differences. I say it because the differences are real and the history is real and the communities that formed around those differences built rich theological traditions that have formed generations of Christians into genuine faith. The Baptist insistence on believer's baptism is not stubbornness. It is a theology of the gathered community, a conviction about conversion and covenant, that has produced some of the most vigorous missionary movements in Christian history. The Reformed tradition's insistence on God's sovereignty in salvation is not fatalism. It is a posture of radical humility before a God who is not domesticated by human decision. These are not trivial.

## What the Moment Requires

We are in a post-Christendom moment in the West. The social conditions that made denominational distinctiveness feel like the primary issue have shifted significantly. When Christianity was the default cultural religion — what Robert Bellah called the civil religion of American public life [cite — confirm] — the differences between denominations functioned like the differences between competing restaurants on the same block: you chose based on preference, and the competition was the relevant category. When Christianity becomes a minority position in a secular culture, the picture changes.

The relevant category is no longer the differences between Christian communities. It is the shared claim that the risen Christ is Lord of creation — a claim that the culture around us finds increasingly implausible. The New Atheism of the early twenty-first century, the decline of institutional religion documented by every major sociological survey since 2008 [cite — confirm], the formation of the largest religious demographic in American history — the Nones, who are not atheists but are not Christians either — these are not peripheral data points. They describe the cultural air the church now breathes.

Against that backdrop, the energy Christians spend fighting each other over secondary questions looks, from the outside, like a family arguing about the paint color while the house burns. I understand why the paint color matters. I am not dismissing the real theological differences between traditions. I am questioning whether the posture of competition and suspicion between Christian communities is the appropriate posture for this moment.

## What Paul Actually Said

Paul's letter to the Ephesians insists that the church's unity is not merely organizational aspiration. It is theological reality: "one body and one Spirit — just as you were called to one hope when you were called — one Lord, one faith, one baptism; one God and Father of all, who is over all and through all and in all" (Ephesians 4:4–6). The unity exists. The question is whether we are willing to practice it visibly rather than confining it to the level of doctrine while the actual communities remain in separate corners.

The high priestly prayer of John 17 makes the stakes explicit: "that they may all be one, just as you, Father, are in me, and I in you, that they also may be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me" (v. 21). The visible unity of the church is, on this account, not primarily an intra-church concern. It is a missional one. It is the ground of the world's belief. When the church is visibly fractured, the message that the risen Christ has reconciled all things to God is undermined at the level of its primary evidence. We are claiming a reconciliation we have not demonstrated.

That is a serious problem.

## What Collaboration Actually Looks Like

The practical form of collaboration does not require doctrinal capitulation. I do not need to pretend the differences between baptistic and paedobaptist traditions do not exist. I do not need to behave as if ecclesiology is irrelevant. What I can do is show up in the city where I serve alongside other pastors who hold Christ as Lord, work together on the needs of the community neither of us can address alone, and model to the watching city that the confession we share is more determinative than the practices that divide us.

I have done this, in fits and starts, in the communities where I have served. What I have found is that the collaboration does not flatten distinctiveness. It clarifies it. When I am working alongside a pastor whose tradition handles things differently than mine, I understand my own tradition more precisely — what I actually believe about it, what I hold firmly and what I hold loosely, and where the genuine disagreements lie. The encounter with difference is instructive rather than threatening, provided I am secure enough in my own convictions not to feel them challenged by contact with someone else's.

The Pastors Connection Network I lead is built on precisely this premise: that pastors from genuinely different theological traditions can share the burdens of ministry, support each other in the loneliness of leadership, and become more effective in their specific contexts by learning from people whose contexts are different. The network is not a doctrinal homogenization project. It is a community of practice built on the confession that precedes the differences — that Jesus is Lord, that the church exists for mission, that pastoral ministry is both a calling and a weight that no one carries well alone.

## The Steel-Man Objection

The objection worth taking seriously is this: genuine theological disagreements are not trivial, and collaboration across genuine disagreement can blur the distinctions in ways that eventually harm the communities involved. If I am collaborating with a pastor whose soteriology I believe is defective, am I not implicitly validating a distortion of the gospel? Is the unity I am performing misleading people about what actually divides these traditions?

This is a serious concern. It is not, however, an argument against collaboration. It is an argument for honesty within collaboration — for being clear about what you share, what you do not share, and why the distinction matters in each context. The Barmen Declaration did not pretend that Lutherans and Reformed had resolved their differences. It stated, clearly and publicly, the ground on which they could stand together, and distinguished that ground from the ground that remained contested. That is the model. Not pretended agreement. Honest identification of what is prior.

The church that is too afraid of other traditions to work alongside them has confused the tradition for the Lord of the tradition. It has made the container the content. And that confusion is its own kind of theological error — arguably a more dangerous one than the doctrinal differences it is trying to protect against, because it has placed institutional self-preservation above the mission for which the institution exists.

## What the City Is Watching

There is a city outside our buildings full of people who find the claim of the gospel implausible. They find it implausible for many reasons — intellectual, moral, experiential. But one of the reasons, less often acknowledged, is the visible contradiction between the message of reconciliation and the reality of a church that cannot appear in the same room as its nearest theological neighbors.

A unified witness — different expressions of the same fundamental confession, working together on the actual needs of the actual city — is at least a more interesting argument. It demonstrates something. It says: whatever we disagree about, we agree that the kingdom of God matters more than institutional protection, and we are willing to spend ourselves together in its service.

Barth and his colleagues understood this. Crisis taught them. The crisis concentrated their attention on what was actually primary and forced the secondary into its proper place.

We do not have to wait for crisis to learn what they learned. We can do the harder work of making that judgment in ordinary time, out of theological conviction rather than emergency. We can decide, before the house is burning, that the Lord of the house is worth more than the paint color.

That decision produces a different kind of church. A different kind of city. A witness that at least has the possibility of being heard.

I am still learning to make it consistently. The denominational instincts are deep. But I have seen enough of what the alternative produces — a church too busy protecting its distinctives to serve its neighbors — to know that the learning is worth it.
