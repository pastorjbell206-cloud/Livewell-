In 2020, the pastoral question that should have been theological became political almost immediately. Masks or no masks. Vaccines or conscience. Gathering in person or following government guidelines. The churches that handled the pandemic well — meaning the churches that protected their most vulnerable members while maintaining community, that held the tension of institutional responsibility and congregational care — were mostly the churches that had already thought theologically about the health of their neighbors before the crisis arrived. The churches that handled it poorly were often the churches for whom public health had never been a theological category at all.

That failure did not begin in 2020. It was the accumulation of decades of theological thinning — the gradual privatization of the gospel into personal salvation and individual morality, the slow evacuation of the social dimensions of Christian responsibility, the training of congregations to interpret biblical language about the body and health and community in exclusively spiritual terms, while the material conditions of their neighbors' lives remained outside the frame.

**What the Body Means in Scripture**

The Hebrew tradition does not permit the body-soul separation that Greek philosophy introduced into Christian theology and that American evangelicalism has often pressed further than either the Greek philosophers or the early church intended. The Hebrew understanding of the human person is integrated and indivisible — not in the therapeutic sense that word has acquired in recent culture, but in the sense that the body is not a vehicle for the soul, not a temporary housing to be escaped, not less important than the spiritual self. The word *nephesh* in the Hebrew Bible, often translated "soul," refers to the whole living person — the self as embodied, hungry, thirsty, breathing, vulnerable. When the psalmist says "my soul thirsts for God" (Psalm 63:1), the thirst is not metaphor. It is the whole person, including the body, reaching for the living God.

This matters for public health because it means that the church cannot care for the "souls" of its neighbors while remaining indifferent to the conditions that shape their bodies. The gospel is addressed to whole persons. Whole persons live in bodies that can be healthy or sick, in neighborhoods that can be clean or toxic, in systems that can support or undermine their flourishing. The God who created the body cares about its conditions.

The New Testament is not less material than the Hebrew Bible. Jesus healed bodies. He fed crowds. He touched lepers when touching them was a public health risk to himself. The early church, in Acts 2 and 4, organized itself around shared material resources precisely because the flourishing of the community required it. The deacons appointed in Acts 6 were appointed to manage food distribution — a public health function in a community where food insecurity was a real and present threat to the most vulnerable members. This is not a minor subplot in the New Testament story. It is the structure of the early church's common life.

**What Public Health Actually Is**

Public health is not primarily the government telling people what to do. It is the organized effort of communities to protect and improve the health of their populations — to identify the conditions that produce disease, injury, and premature death and to change them. John Snow mapping cholera cases in London in 1854 to identify the Broad Street pump as the source was public health. [cite — confirm] The sanitation reforms that reduced infant mortality in industrial cities through the late nineteenth century were public health. The elimination of smallpox through international vaccination campaigns was public health. The reduction of tobacco-related deaths through decades of public education and policy change was public health.

None of these interventions were primarily about individual choices. They were about the conditions in which people live — the water they drink, the air they breathe, the information available to them about health risks, the systems that distribute health-protective resources to some communities and not to others. Public health is the recognition that health is not simply the outcome of individual decisions but the product of environments and systems that individuals do not fully control.

This recognition is theologically legible. The prophets understood that communities are environments that produce outcomes for the people who live in them. Isaiah's vision of the restored city in Isaiah 65:20-22 includes people who live out their full years, who build houses and inhabit them, who plant vineyards and eat their fruit — the vision of a community whose conditions support human flourishing rather than cutting it short. The Jeremiah 29:7 command to "seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile" uses the word *shalom* — the comprehensive well-being that includes health, safety, material sufficiency, right relationship. Seeking the city's welfare is a public health mandate. It is not optional.

**What the Church Has Gotten Wrong**

The evangelical church in the United States has, in large segments, treated public health as a government intrusion on personal freedom rather than as a theological responsibility rooted in the love of neighbor. This position has required a selective reading of Scripture that magnifies the texts about individual liberty while minimizing the texts about communal obligation.

The most obvious example: the book of Leviticus, which evangelical churches tend to approach selectively, contains extensive public health legislation. The regulations about infectious disease — covering isolation of infected persons, inspection protocols, requirements for cleaning or destroying contaminated materials — were not merely ritual purity requirements. They were community health practices designed to prevent the spread of disease in a densely settled community (Leviticus 13-14). The logic was that the health of one person was a matter of communal concern because community members shared physical space, water, and food sources. Individual behavior had community consequences. The community had legitimate interest in regulating behavior that affected its health.

The early church understood this logic when it organized care for the sick at personal risk to caregivers. During the Antonine Plague of the second century and the Plague of Cyprian in the third, Christians became known in the Roman world for caring for the sick — including non-Christians — when others fled. Rodney Stark, in his sociological study of early Christian growth, argues that this practice was both theologically motivated and practically significant: Christian communities had higher survival rates partly because they organized care for the sick rather than abandoning them. [cite — confirm] The theology and the public health outcome were not competing interests. The theology produced the public health outcome.

**The Justice Dimension**

Public health and justice are not separable. The burden of preventable disease, injury, and premature death in the United States is not distributed randomly. It follows the lines of race and income with a consistency that is not incidental. Black Americans die of preventable conditions at higher rates than white Americans in almost every disease category. [cite — confirm] Poor communities have higher rates of uncontrolled asthma, diabetes, and hypertension than wealthy communities. Communities adjacent to industrial facilities with high pollution outputs — which are disproportionately communities of color — have elevated rates of childhood asthma and cancer. [cite — confirm]

This distribution is not the result of different individual choices across racial and economic groups. It is the result of different exposures — to environmental toxins, to food environments, to healthcare access, to the chronic stress of economic precarity and racial threat. It is the result of systems that distribute health risks and health protections unequally.

The church that preaches the equal dignity of all persons before God while remaining silent about the systems that produce unequal health outcomes has not finished its sentence. The imago Dei — the divine image carried by every human being — is not an abstraction that floats above material conditions. It is a claim about the person in their full embodied existence. The person whose childhood asthma is untreated because her family cannot afford healthcare carries the image of God in a body the church has declared sacred in principle while declining to protect in practice.

**What the Pandemic Revealed**

COVID-19 did not create the church's failures around public health. It exposed them. It revealed how thoroughly the theology of much of American Christianity had been shaped by the individualism of American culture rather than the communal obligations of the biblical tradition. The churches that insisted on gathering in person when the medical consensus was clear that gathering would spread disease and kill the most vulnerable among them were not being faithful to Scripture. They were being faithful to an American ideology of individual rights that had been dressed in theological language.

I am not interested in scoring partisan points on this. I am interested in the theological error, which belongs to all of us in different ways. The privatization of faith — the reduction of Christianity to personal salvation, individual morality, and private spiritual practice — has left the church without categories for thinking about the communal and structural dimensions of human flourishing. When a question arises that requires those categories, the church defaults to whatever the surrounding culture offers. In much of American evangelical culture, what the surrounding culture offers is a framework of individual liberty in which the community's claim on the individual is always presumptively suspect.

That framework is not the framework of Leviticus, or the prophets, or the early church, or the New Testament at any point. It is a framework the church has absorbed from a specific cultural context and has failed to critically examine.

**What the Church Should Be Doing**

The church that takes public health seriously as a theological matter will do several things. It will ensure that its own community practices support the health of its most vulnerable members — the elderly, the immunocompromised, the people for whom a communicable disease is not an inconvenience but a genuine threat. It will speak prophetically about the conditions that produce unequal health outcomes in its city — the food desert two miles from the church building, the industrial facility polluting the air in the neighborhood that cannot afford to move, the lack of healthcare access that means the family across town is managing its diabetes with emergency room visits rather than primary care.

And it will resist the cultural framework that treats the health of its neighbors as a private matter — outside the church's concern, outside the scope of the gospel, best left to individual choice and market mechanisms. That framework has a name. It is not Christianity. It has been confused with Christianity for long enough.

The God who made bodies cares about their conditions. The church that does not care about the conditions that shape the bodies of its neighbors has narrowed its theology to a size the Bible does not recognize.
