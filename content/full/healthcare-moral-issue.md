A woman in my congregation — I will call her Maria — worked two jobs, both without benefits. She knew something was wrong with her body for eight months before she could figure out how to pay to find out what it was. By the time she had the examination and the imaging, the cancer had moved from Stage II to Stage III. She asked me later, in one of the most precise sentences I have heard in fifteen years of pastoral ministry, "If I had been rich, would I have found this sooner?"

Yes. She would have. That is not a guess. It is what the data consistently shows.

The church has largely treated this as a political question, which means it has declined to treat it as a moral one. That is a category error with life-and-death consequences.

---

The distribution of healthcare access in the United States tracks with extraordinary consistency along the same lines as every other form of inequality: income, race, geography. Americans with employer-provided insurance or sufficient income receive healthcare at rates and quality comparable to the best in the world. Americans without consistent access — the uninsured, the underinsured, those in rural areas without nearby providers, those in communities of color with documented disparities in the quality of care they receive — receive healthcare that is intermittent, crisis-driven, and often catastrophically delayed.

As of the most recent data available, tens of millions of Americans remain uninsured or significantly underinsured [cite — confirm current figures]. Medical debt is the leading cause of personal bankruptcy in the United States [cite — confirm]. The infant mortality rate in the United States is significantly higher than in peer nations and shows marked disparities by race — Black infants in the United States die at rates more than twice those of white infants [cite — confirm]. Maternal mortality follows similar patterns, with Black women dying in childbirth at disproportionately high rates regardless of income level [cite — confirm].

These are not statistics about a distant population. These are facts about the people in your congregation's neighborhood, about the people whose children attend the same schools as your congregation's children, about people made in the image of God who are experiencing preventable suffering and preventable death because of how the healthcare system is structured.

---

The theological frame begins with the body. The Bible is not a Gnostic text. It does not treat the body as a temporary inconvenience to be escaped by the soul. Genesis 2:7 describes God forming the human being from the dust of the ground and breathing life into him — embodied life, physical existence, is God's creative act. The Incarnation (John 1:14) is the declaration that God valued embodied existence enough to take it on in the person of Jesus. The resurrection of Jesus Christ (1 Corinthians 15) is bodily resurrection — not the liberation of the soul from the body but the redemption of the whole person.

A theology that takes embodiment seriously cannot treat the health of bodies as a secondary concern. If the body is good — if God made it, took it on, and redeemed it — then the suffering of bodies from preventable, treatable disease is a matter of theological seriousness.

Ezekiel 34:4 indicts the shepherds of Israel with this: "You have not strengthened the weak or healed the sick or bound up the injured. You have not brought back the strays or searched for the lost." The failure to heal the sick is placed in the center of the prophetic indictment of failed leadership. Jesus's healing ministry — which occupies a significant proportion of the Gospel narratives — is not incidental to his proclamation of the kingdom. It is one of its primary demonstrations. When John's disciples ask Jesus whether he is "the one who is to come," Jesus responds: "Go back and report to John what you hear and see: The blind receive sight, the lame walk, those who have leprosy are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the good news is proclaimed to the poor" (Matthew 11:4–5). Healing and good news are not separate categories. They are the same announcement.

---

The specific form of the healthcare access problem in the United States is not the only imaginable way to structure medical provision. Every other wealthy nation in the world has organized its healthcare system differently and achieves better outcomes on most population-level health metrics at lower per-capita cost [cite — confirm general comparative finding]. The United States spends more per person on healthcare than any nation in the world and has worse outcomes on many markers — life expectancy, infant mortality, maternal mortality — than countries that spend significantly less [cite — confirm].

This comparison is not a political point. It is a factual observation about the consequences of structural choices. The way a society organizes its healthcare system is a moral choice. It reflects what that society believes about the value of human life and the obligations members of a community have to one another. The choice the United States has made — to organize healthcare primarily as a market commodity rather than as a common social provision — is a choice with moral consequences that are visible in the data.

The church should be equipped to say this. Not to prescribe a specific policy mechanism — there are genuine disagreements about which mechanisms best achieve universal access, and those disagreements deserve serious engagement. But to name the theological conviction that sits underneath the policy question: every person, as a bearer of God's image, has a claim on the care of their body. Their ability to exercise that claim should not depend on their income.

---

The steel-man case against framing healthcare as a right or a justice issue goes like this: rights language applied to healthcare creates an entitlement to a service that has to be provided by other people's labor, which creates serious questions about compulsion and freedom. Market mechanisms, for all their flaws, have produced remarkable medical innovation; government systems tend toward rationing and inefficiency. The church's job is not to prescribe economic policy but to inspire individual generosity and to support charitable care for the sick. The church has been doing this through hospitals and clinics for centuries.

This argument deserves engagement. Medical innovation is a genuine good. Market mechanisms have produced it. Government systems have real weaknesses. The church's historic medical mission — from the hospitals founded by early Christian communities to the modern hospital systems bearing the names of saints and church orders — is a genuine contribution to healthcare access.

But notice what the argument leaves out. The question is not whether market mechanisms produce innovation. They do. The question is whether market mechanisms, as the primary organizing principle of healthcare delivery, produce equitable access. The evidence is that they do not. The innovation is real and the access is unequal, and the people who pay the cost of the inequality are not the people making the argument.

The church's charitable healthcare tradition is also genuine. And genuinely insufficient. The scale of the access problem in the United States cannot be solved by church clinics and charitable hospitals. The structural problem requires structural response. The church that points to its charitable contributions as a sufficient answer to a structural problem has confused category again.

---

There is a passage in James 5 that does not get enough attention in healthcare discussions. James writes: "Now listen, you rich people, weep and wail because of the misery that is coming on you... Look! The wages you failed to pay the workers who mowed your fields are crying out against you. The cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord Almighty" (James 5:1, 4). The withholding of wages — the failure to pay people what their labor is worth — is presented as a justice crime of sufficient seriousness that the cries reach God's ears directly.

The failure to ensure healthcare access to workers who cannot afford it is structurally similar. The labor of the working poor generates the wealth of the economy they work in. The system that extracts their labor and withholds from them the basic provision their bodies require — adequate healthcare — is doing something structurally related to what James 5 indicts. The cries are reaching God's ears. The question is whether they are reaching the church's ears.

---

Maria finished her treatment. The Stage III diagnosis required aggressive intervention — chemotherapy, radiation, surgery. She lost her hair. She lost months of work, which nearly lost her housing. A group from the congregation came together to help cover her expenses during treatment, which was beautiful. But what I remember is that she asked me, eight months in, whether her illness was God's will.

I told her no. And then I told her something harder: that the delay that turned a potentially manageable Stage II into a Stage III requiring everything her body had was not God's will either. That it was the consequence of a system organized in a way that left her without access to early detection. That God grieves what preventable suffering does to his image-bearers.

She sat with that for a while. Then she said: "Then why doesn't the church say so?"

The answer to her question is what I am trying to work out in this essay and in my own ministry. Why doesn't the church say so? Not because the theological case is unclear — the case for the dignity of every body, the obligation to heal, the prophetic indictment of systems that deny healing to the poor, is about as clear as biblical material gets. But because the political cost of saying so is high, and the institutional habit of the American church is to avoid political costs.

That habit has its own cost. The cost is measured in the people who did not get the early detection. In the cancers that were Stage III when they should have been Stage II. In the women who died in childbirth whose deaths were preventable. In the children who will carry the health consequences of untreated childhood illness into adulthood.

Healthcare is a moral issue. The church that treats it as a political question it is not qualified to address has chosen institutional comfort over prophetic faithfulness.

Maria is in remission. I am grateful for that. I am not at peace with how we got here.
