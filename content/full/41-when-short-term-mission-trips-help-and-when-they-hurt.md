The short-term mission trip is one of the most common and most debated practices in contemporary evangelical church life. Millions of Americans participate in short-term missions annually, spending billions of dollars to travel to developing nations for periods ranging from one week to several months. The intentions behind these trips are almost universally good. The outcomes are considerably more mixed.

This is not an argument that short-term missions are inherently problematic or that churches should stop doing them. It is an argument that the question of whether a specific trip will genuinely help or inadvertently harm the people it intends to serve is worth asking honestly, before the commitment is made, with reference to research and the perspectives of people in the receiving communities.

## The Historical Problem Behind the Practice

Short-term missions as a mass practice emerged largely in the second half of the twentieth century, shaped by a confluence of factors: the rise of commercial air travel that made international movement affordable for ordinary Americans, the growth of evangelical para-church organizations that could coordinate large groups, and a genuine missionary energy that the church was right to cultivate.

But it also emerged from a theological assumption that was rarely examined: that the presence of American Christians in a foreign context was itself a form of blessing, that the desire to serve was sufficient qualification for service, and that the good news of the gospel could be adequately translated across cultural, linguistic, and relational distances in seven to ten days.

The missiological tradition should have complicated this assumption earlier than it did. Roland Allen's 1912 work *Missionary Methods: St. Paul's or Ours?* [cite — confirm] was already arguing that the Western missionary enterprise had systematically failed to plant indigenous, self-sustaining churches because it had never genuinely trusted indigenous leaders. Andrew Walls' scholarship on the historical transmission of Christianity from the West to the Global South documented how the gospel took root not when it was delivered by outsiders but when it was received and reinterpreted by indigenous believers [cite — confirm]. The short-term mission movement inherited the structural assumptions that Allen and Walls had already identified as problematic, and scaled them up with jet engines.

## When They Help

Short-term mission trips tend to generate genuine benefit in specific conditions. When they are part of a long-term partnership — when the short-term team is building on an ongoing relationship between sending and receiving churches, contributing to work that the receiving community has identified as a genuine priority, and operating under the leadership of national believers rather than assuming their own leadership — the experience tends to produce genuine value for both participants and community.

Short-term trips also tend to be genuinely beneficial when the skills being brought are genuinely scarce in the receiving context. A team of medical professionals providing care that is genuinely unavailable locally, a construction team building what the community cannot build with available local skills and resources, an English-language training team meeting a specific educational need — these are contributions that the community actually benefits from, rather than contributions that displace local workers or communicate that outsiders can do what locals cannot.

The third condition under which short-term trips help is when they are primarily designed to form the participants rather than to benefit the host community — and when that design is honest about it. The college student who spends two weeks serving alongside national believers in Guatemala, not because her skills are uniquely needed there but because the exposure to the global church and the poverty it works within will permanently reshape her understanding of the gospel — that trip can be genuinely valuable, if the host community is compensated for its time and not burdened by the participants' inexperience.

## When They Hurt

The research on the harm caused by short-term mission trips is substantial and sobering. Steve Corbett and Brian Fikkert in *When Helping Hurts* [cite — confirm] and Robert Lupton in *Toxic Charity* [cite — confirm] have documented the specific ways in which well-intentioned short-term trips can produce dependency, damage local economies, undermine national leadership, and communicate — despite the best intentions of the participants — that the Western team knows better than the local community what the local community needs.

The mission trip that sends unskilled volunteers to build structures that local workers could build more efficiently and economically is not providing help. It is providing a cross-cultural experience at the expense of the local economy. The wages that would have gone to local construction workers — men with families, men with needs — go instead toward the airfare and logistics of Americans whose primary qualification is their willingness to serve.

The team that arrives with a predetermined program and implements it without reference to what the community has asked for is not serving the community. It is serving its own vision of what service looks like.

And the trip that produces a flood of social media posts and speaking engagements and emotional testimonies when the participants return home — while the community that hosted them returns to the structural poverty that a week of American enthusiasm has not altered — has produced a spiritual commodity for the Western church at the cost of the dignity of the people who were treated as its raw material.

I have been on some version of these trips. I have returned with the photographs and the stories and the enlarged sense of my own generosity. I did not ask, while I was there, whether my presence was the best use of the money it cost to send me. I should have asked.

## The Steel-Manned Defense

The strongest defense of the short-term mission trip is not about the material outcomes. It is about formation.

The argument goes like this: American Christians are, on the whole, profoundly insulated from the global suffering of the majority of the world's population, from the vitality of the church in the Global South, and from the cultural parochialism of their own faith. The short-term trip ruptures that insulation. It produces an embodied encounter with global Christianity and global poverty that reading books about it simply does not produce. The student who returns from two weeks in Rwanda is not the same person who left. That formation is worth taking seriously.

This argument has genuine merit. Paul's vision in Philippians 4:11 of contentment that is learned rather than assumed — "I have learned, in whatever state I am, to be content" — was itself formed through the embodied experience of deprivation and abundance. Formation through encounter is a real thing. The church's investment in the global encounter of its members, particularly its young people, is not nothing.

But the defense still does not answer the question of whether the communities receiving these short-term teams are bearing the cost of American formation. That is the question the defense must answer, and it usually changes the subject instead.

## Questions Worth Asking Before the Next Trip

Before organizing or committing to a short-term mission trip, honest reflection on several questions is warranted.

What has the receiving community asked for? Not what does the sending team feel called to provide — what has the receiving community, in its own voice, identified as a genuine need and a genuine welcome?

What would it cost to hire local people to do what the team plans to do, and is that a better investment of the funds that will be spent on airfare and logistics? If the answer is yes, have the honest conversation about who the trip is actually for.

What is the long-term relationship between the sending and receiving communities, and does this trip strengthen or complicate it? A trip that is a single transaction — with no ongoing relationship before or after — is almost certainly benefiting the participants more than the hosts.

How will the trip be evaluated — by the experience of the participants, or by the genuine and measurable benefit to the receiving community? If the evaluation criteria are primarily about transformation in the participants, name that honestly, and make sure the host community is being served rather than used in the process.

## The Pastoral Scene

I sat with a pastor a few years ago who led a mid-sized suburban church with an active short-term missions program. He had taken groups to the same community in Central America for twelve consecutive years. He knew the national pastor by name, knew his wife's name, knew their children's names. He had stayed in their home. He had watched the local congregation grow from forty members to over two hundred.

He told me that the first four or five years, his teams had come with plans and left with photographs and had produced, in his honest assessment, very little that the community could not have done better and cheaper without them. What changed it was a conversation in year six when the national pastor told him, with the particular combination of grace and directness that genuine friendship permits: "What we need from you is not your volunteers. We need your pastors' network. We need you to help us get our young leaders into theological training that we cannot access from here."

The program changed. The trips became smaller, focused on connecting rather than building. The money that had gone to logistics began funding scholarships for local seminary students. The church in Michigan became a genuine partner rather than a periodic benefactor. That is what mission looks like when the receiving community is finally allowed to define it.

The communities on the receiving end of American mission trips deserve that level of care. The gospel deserves to be pursued with enough wisdom to genuinely serve the people it claims to love. The question of whether the next trip will help or hurt is worth sitting with long enough to answer honestly — even if the honest answer is inconvenient.

Start with what they asked for. End with whether they were better served than you were formed. Let the gap between those two questions be the work of this year's trip planning.
