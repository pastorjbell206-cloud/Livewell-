Paul said it plainly and we quote it without feeling its full weight: "If Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile; you are still in your sins. Then those also who have fallen asleep in Christ are lost. If only for this life we have hope in Christ, we are of all people most to be pitied" (1 Corinthians 15:17-19).

Most pastors. Of all people most to be pitied.

He was not speaking hypothetically. He was drawing the line directly: either the resurrection is a historical event with cosmic consequences or the pastoral vocation is, at its core, a massive mistake. There is no middle position available. No version of ministry that affirms the resurrection as meaningful symbol while quietly treating it as something other than bodily, historical, and actual. The middle position is the one Paul calls pitiable. And I think more pastors than would admit it have quietly drifted toward it.

Not through theological apostasy. Through something more common: the slow erosion of resurrectional confidence under the weight of pastoral experience. You sit with enough grief to know it is not easily resolved. You watch enough people walk away from faith to stop being confident that God reliably shows up. You bury enough church members — including ones whose faith was enormous and whose suffering was severe — to find the resurrection feeling more like comfort language than world-altering claim. You keep preaching it. You stop being shocked by it. The shock, it turns out, was doing more work than you knew.

The historical stakes of the resurrection are worth recovering. The first proclamation of the risen Christ took place in the city where the crucifixion had happened, within weeks of the event, in the presence of people who could have verified or falsified the claim. Peter's sermon at Pentecost (Acts 2:14-36) is not a call to spiritual experience. It is a piece of historical argument: this man was executed before your eyes; God raised him, and we are witnesses. The proclamation is testable in principle. The fact that the movement exploded rather than collapsed in that environment is not nothing.

The early Christians did not treat the resurrection as a spiritual category. They treated it as the explosive announcement that changed what was possible inside history. The fact that the Roman authorities moved to suppress the story — bribing the soldiers to say the disciples stole the body (Matthew 28:12-13) — indicates they understood that the claim, if believed, would be socially destabilizing [cite — confirm]. They were correct. The early church expanded across the Roman Empire not because it offered a better moral philosophy but because it proclaimed that the specific man Rome had executed was alive, that death had been defeated, that the future was permanently different from what it had been before that Sunday morning.

N.T. Wright's exhaustive work on the resurrection demonstrates that the early Christian proclamation was distinctive precisely in its insistence on bodily resurrection — not spiritual survival, not the immortality of the soul, but the transformation of the body into the kind of body that belongs to the renewed creation [cite — confirm]. The resurrection in the Christian claim is not the departure of the soul from the world. It is the irruption of the new creation into the old. Jesus's resurrection is the first installment of a renovation that will encompass everything.

The pastor who genuinely believes this leads differently.

He sits at the deathbed and holds something more than professional composure. He presides at the graveside and means what he says — not as rhetorical consolation but as the statement of a truth he actually inhabits. He endures the seasons of apparent failure in ministry without losing his footing entirely, because his confidence is not in the trajectory of the congregation's attendance but in the God who raises dead things.

I have led from that confidence in seasons, and I have led without it in others. The difference is not primarily in what I said from the pulpit. The difference was in what happened to me when the week went badly, when someone I loved suffered, when the church felt like it was going backwards. Without the resurrection, those moments produce despair. With it — genuinely with it, not merely confessionally attached to the proposition — they produce something harder to manufacture: a grief that still hopes, a realism that does not collapse into nihilism, a willingness to keep going that does not depend on visible signs of progress.

The counter-position deserves a hearing. There are serious theologians who have argued that the resurrection's significance is primarily soteriological and existential — that what matters is not the brute historical fact but the meaning it carries, the encounter it enables, the community it founds. Rudolf Bultmann's demythologization program, which was enormously influential in the mid-twentieth century, moved in this direction: strip away the mythological container, preserve the kerygmatic content [cite — confirm]. The pastoral appeal of this position is real. It seems to protect the gospel from the precariousness of historical claims.

The problem is that Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 15 does not allow this move. Paul does not say: if Christ has not been raised as a powerful symbol of new life, your faith is futile. He says: if Christ has not been raised as a historical event, you are still in your sins and those who have died are lost. The claim is contingent on historical reality. Remove the history and you have not preserved the gospel. You have replaced it with something smaller.

This is not the prosperity gospel's confident abundance. The prosperity gospel has no room for the cross. Resurrectional hope has plenty of room for the cross, because it knows what came after. The cross is real and devastating; the empty tomb is also real and changes everything about what the cross means. Both must be held, together, without letting either cancel the other.

The cross says: God is not absent from suffering. The resurrection says: suffering is not the last word. Together they produce the peculiar posture that Paul describes as "sorrowful, yet always rejoicing" (2 Corinthians 6:10). Not the forced joy that performs happiness in the face of pain. Not the despair that treats the suffering as final. The grief that carries hope. The hope that does not require the suffering to have been unreal.

That posture is what pastoral ministry runs on over the long arc. The congregation will not always be growing. The finances will not always be stable. The community will not always be healthy. There will be betrayals and departures and seasons when the effort seems to be producing nothing visible. What keeps a pastor in the room — what keeps him from the slow drift toward cynicism that eventually kills more ministries than any public collapse — is not optimism. Optimism is defeated by evidence. What keeps him is the operative conviction that God raises dead things, that the trajectory of history is not determined by current appearances, that the God who brought Israel out of Egypt and raised Jesus from the tomb is still active in the particular, unglamorous, sometimes discouraging circumstances of this congregation, this city, this year.

The pastoral vocation is sustained in the long run by this hope or it is not sustained at all. You can manage a congregation for a while on competence and charisma and genuine care. Ministry across decades requires something the competent manager does not have: the actual, operative belief that God raises the dead.

That belief is not an achievement. It is a gift. It comes through the Scripture, through prayer, through the repeated encounter with the living God in the ordinary practices of ministry — the Lord's Supper where Christ is genuinely present, the baptism where the water means what it says, the sermon where the ancient text addresses the present moment with uncanny precision. The belief must be regularly renewed, because pastoral experience — with its suffering and failure and complexity — will erode it if you are not attending.

Wendell Berry writes, in the agrarian tradition, about the importance of practicing resurrection — not as a one-time event but as a daily commitment to acts of renewal and return and repair that embody the conviction that the world is not finished [cite — confirm]. The phrase has become common enough to be abstract. But for the pastor, it names something specific: the practice of leading from the conviction of the empty tomb rather than the management of present appearances.

The tomb is still empty. Lead like you believe it.

Every deathbed you sit at, every marriage you counsel through the hardest season, every person you receive back after a departure — you are either bringing the resurrection into that room or you are not. You cannot fake it for long. Either you actually believe that God raises dead things, or you believe something smaller.

Go back to the text. Stay there until it shocks you again.
