The therapeutic version of sin works like this: sin is the things you do that hurt yourself or others. The solution is awareness, accountability, and better habits. The gospel, in this reading, is primarily information about what constitutes sin and motivation to do better. Jesus shows us what right behavior looks like and provides inspiration and community to pursue it.

This is not wrong exactly. It is so inadequate that it produces a Christianity that cannot explain why people who are aware of their failures, who are accountable to communities, who are genuinely trying — still cannot change the things that matter most. Why the man who preaches against anger loses his temper with his children on the way home from church. Why the woman who has confessed envy a hundred times still finds it rising in her chest when someone she resents succeeds. Why, after years of spiritual formation, the pride that drives so much of what I do is still there — disguised, perhaps, wearing the clothing of pastoral dedication or theological seriousness, but recognizable to anyone paying careful attention, including me.

Paul knew the problem from the inside. Romans 7 is his autobiographical account: "I do not understand my own actions. For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate" (Romans 7:15). He is not describing a person who lacks information about the right behavior. He is describing a person who knows precisely what the right behavior is and finds himself unable consistently to do it — not from laziness or lack of effort, but because there is something wrong at a level that effort cannot reach.

---

The Hebrew word for sin — chatta't — can mean a missing of the mark, but the broader canonical picture of sin is not reducible to that archery image. Genesis 3 does not present the first sin as a simple behavioral failure. It presents it as an act of disordering — of placing the self in the position of God, of reaching for the knowledge of good and evil, of deciding that the creature's judgment should override the Creator's word. The disorder is ontological before it is behavioral. The wrong action is the expression of a disordered self, not merely a bad choice made by an otherwise healthy self.

This is why Jeremiah can say that the human heart is deceitful above all things and desperately sick (Jeremiah 17:9) — not that certain hearts are this way, but that this is the universal condition, the description of the interior from which human beings generate their lives in the absence of intervention. This is why the psalmist, in Psalm 51:5, traces his sin not to a particular bad decision but to the condition from which he emerged: "Behold, I was brought forth in iniquity, and in sin did my mother conceive me." Not blame toward the mother. An acknowledgment that the problem runs deeper than any particular act.

Jesus, in Mark 7:21-22, locates the source of all defilement inside the person — in the heart — rather than in external contact with the unclean. "For from within, out of the heart of man, come evil thoughts, sexual immorality, theft, murder, adultery, coveting, wickedness, deceit, sensuality, envy, slander, pride, foolishness. All these evil things come from within, and they defile a person." He is not describing a list of behaviors that certain people with particular weaknesses practice. He is describing the interior from which all human beings, without intervention, generate their lives.

Not actions. Interior. The source is the heart.

---

Augustine articulated this with a precision that shaped Western theology for more than a thousand years, and whose insight remains the most honest account I have found of the actual human condition. His concept of the will's bondage — the phrase non posse non peccare, the inability not to sin — described not a person who lacks freedom in the ordinary sense but a person whose will has been so disordered that even its best efforts at righteousness are directed by something other than the pure love of God [cite — confirm]. The will functions. It chooses. But the chooser is bent, and bent-ness produces bent choices even when the bent person is trying to be straight.

The steel-man case against this position is worth sitting with honestly. Pelagius argued in the fifth century that if sin is a condition of nature rather than a behavior, then it cannot properly be our fault, and if it is not our fault, then God's judgment of it is unjust [cite — confirm]. If we cannot do otherwise, we cannot be held accountable for what we do. This is not a frivolous argument. It has an internal logic that the church has never entirely dismissed. The councils that condemned Pelagius did not dismiss his concern for human accountability. They answered it.

The Augustinian answer was this: the bondage of the will is itself the consequence of a choice freely made. The condition is not imposed from outside by a God who then judges us for what he imposed. It is the result of what the human being, using genuine freedom, did with the freedom given. The fire burned because humans lit it. The chains are real, but the chain-making was an act of will. We are accountable for the condition because we produced it — not any individual descendant of Adam who is personally responsible for the original act, but a species that has consistently, comprehensively, without exception in any historical generation, chosen what Genesis 3 describes: the self in the position of God.

But more importantly than the philosophical response: the Pelagian account does not fit the experience of people who have actually tried to change. I have tried to change. I have watched people try to change who are more disciplined than I am, more aware, more accountable. The things that need changing most deeply — the pride that shapes even my most apparently generous acts, the fear that masquerades as caution, the desire to be seen that drives even my most apparently humble service — do not yield to effort and awareness. They yield to something that comes from outside the self. That is not a theory. That is what I have observed in thirty years of following Christ and pastoring people who are trying to do the same.

---

Why does this matter for salvation? Because the shape of the problem determines the shape of the solution.

If sin is primarily behavioral, the solution is primarily behavioral — education, motivation, spiritual disciplines, community accountability, sustained effort over time. These are not worthless. They address real things. But they address symptoms while the condition remains. You can change the behaviors and still be the same person. You can stop the particular acts that are most visible and still be operating from the same disordered interior that produced them. Anyone who has watched a religious community that emphasizes behavioral compliance without dealing with the interior knows the result: people who have gotten better at hiding what they are while remaining what they are.

If sin is ontological — a condition of the will and the self, not merely a catalog of misbehaviors — then the solution must address the self at its root. Paul calls this new creation (2 Corinthians 5:17). John calls it being born again, born from above — the generation of a new life that the flesh cannot produce from within itself (John 3:3-8). The language is deliberately biological. It is not language about improved performance. It is language about a different kind of being.

This is not a minor theological distinction. The difference between a Christianity that addresses behavior and a Christianity that addresses being is the difference between a new coat of paint on a condemned building and actual reconstruction. The condemned building may look better from the street. It is still condemned.

The gospel actually announces something different from behavior modification. In the death of Jesus, something was accomplished — not just demonstrated. The cross is not primarily a moral example. It is, in Paul's language, the place where "him who knew no sin" became sin for us, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God (2 Corinthians 5:21). Not that we would now try harder to be righteous. That we would become what we were made to be, from the inside, by the agency of the Spirit who applies what the cross accomplished.

---

The practical consequence of getting this wrong is visible in the pastoral experience of most churches.

The congregation that understands sin primarily as behavior will try to address it primarily through accountability — accountability groups, confession, public commitments to do better. These structures are not useless. But they are regularly experienced as exhausting and ultimately ineffective in precisely the areas that matter most, because what they address is the expression of the condition while the condition itself remains. The person commits again and again to stop the behavior, and the behavior returns, and each failure produces shame, and shame produces hiding, and hiding prevents the honest reckoning with the interior from which the behavior grows.

The congregation that understands sin as a condition will understand why the disciplines of formation — prayer, Scripture, communal worship, service — are not primarily techniques for improving behavior but practices that create the conditions in which the Spirit can work at the level of the will itself. They are not causes of the new birth. They are, in Eugene Peterson's language [cite — confirm], the means by which we practice the life we have been given — the way the new creation, given from outside, is exercised into an actual new manner of living.

I have tried both, in my own life and in the churches I have served. The congregation that focuses primarily on behavior produces people who are exhausted by the effort and secretly convinced that they are failing what the gospel requires. The congregation that grasps the depth of the condition — and therefore grasps the depth of what the gospel addresses — tends to produce people who are honest about their failures, who understand that honesty as part of the work rather than evidence of its absence, and who are, over long years, genuinely different rather than merely more disciplined.

---

Sin is what we are. Not the worst of us — all of us. Not only in our most dramatic failures, but in the orientations that drive even our best moments.

Salvation is what God does about what we are. And what God does is not primarily announce the rules more clearly or increase the motivation to follow them. What God does, in the incarnation and the cross and the resurrection and the ongoing work of the Spirit, is make something new where something old had been. Slowly, not without suffering, not without the long work of formation, not without honest reckoning with what is still being changed — but genuinely new.

That is harder to believe than the behavioral account. It removes the sense of control that the behavioral account preserves. It requires admitting that the problem is deeper than any strategy you can devise can reach. It requires trusting that what God has promised, God will do — which means living in the tension between the condition being addressed and the completion not yet arrived.

I have not arrived. I am not where I was. That difference is not the result of my sustained effort. It is the result of a grace that met me in the condition I was in, which was worse than I admitted for years, and that is still meeting me there.

That is the only account I have found that is honest about the problem. It is also the only one that offers a solution commensurate with the problem's actual depth.
