Psalm 88 ends without resolution.

That is unusual. Most lament psalms move through darkness toward some form of trust — they end with a declaration of confidence, a memory of God's past faithfulness, a choice to keep worshipping in spite of the darkness. Psalm 88 does none of that. It begins with a cry to God and ends with the words "darkness is my closest friend." There is no turn. There is no uplift. The darkness is the last word.

The church has generally not known what to do with Psalm 88. We quote Psalm 23 at funerals. We sing Psalm 100. We return to Psalm 51 for confession. Psalm 88 sits in the collection, rarely preached, rarely sung, rarely prayed — because it does not go where we expect the Psalms to go, and its refusal to go there makes us uncomfortable.

But the Psalter is not a collection of devotional verses arranged for our comfort. It is the prayer book of the people of God, assembled over centuries, including the full range of what happens when human beings try to talk to God honestly. The fact that Psalm 88 is in the canon is a theological statement: this is also what prayer looks like. This is also what faith sounds like when it has nowhere left to go.

## The History Behind the Collection

The Psalter took its final shape over roughly a thousand years of Israel's life with God. The earliest psalms may reach back to the wilderness period. The latest reflect the experience of Babylonian exile — Psalm 137's anguished cry "by the rivers of Babylon, there we sat and wept" (Psalm 137:1) is the cry of people who have watched Jerusalem burn, who have been marched hundreds of miles from home, who are singing the Lord's songs in a foreign land because their captors have demanded entertainment.

The Psalter was assembled as the prayer book for the Second Temple community returning from exile — people who came back to a land that was not quite the land they had expected, to ruins they had to rebuild, to a form of worship that had to be reconstructed from memory and imagination. They needed prayers for every condition of the human heart before God: celebration, thanksgiving, orientation, disorientation, rage, grief, accusation, trust, exhaustion, and praise. The editors included all of it. They did not sanitize.

That editorial decision is itself a form of pastoral theology. The community that will say all of this to God is a community that believes God can receive all of it. The God of the Psalter is not fragile. He is not offended by honest complaint. He does not require the worshiper to approach in a particular emotional register before the prayer counts. Every form of the interior life — including the forms the church has been uncomfortable with — is admitted to the conversation.

## What the Psalms Actually Are

Walter Brueggemann's distinction between psalms of orientation, disorientation, and new orientation [cite — confirm] is the most useful framework I know for understanding why the Psalter is structured the way it is. Psalms of orientation celebrate the goodness of God in a world where things are working. Psalms of disorientation — the laments, the complaints, the expressions of abandonment — name what it is like when the world has fallen apart and God seems absent or hostile. Psalms of new orientation describe what it is like to have come through darkness to a different kind of trust: not the naive trust of orientation but the hard-won trust of someone who has been in the darkness and survived it.

The church has been very comfortable with orientation psalms and new orientation psalms. It has been systematically uncomfortable with disorientation psalms — the ones where God is accused of sleeping, of forgetting, of turning his face away. We have treated those psalms as embarrassing ancestors of a more mature faith, the raw expressions of people who had not yet learned to trust properly.

This is exactly backwards. The laments are not evidence of immature faith. They are evidence of honest faith — of people who believed in God enough to bring their grief and their accusation to him rather than collapsing into silence or turning away. The person who cannot bring anger to God has not matured beyond the lament psalms. She has lost access to part of her own interior life that God already knows is there.

Psalm 44 accuses God of sleeping: "Awake, Lord! Why do you sleep? Rouse yourself! Do not reject us forever" (verse 23). This is not the prayer of someone who has given up on God. It is the prayer of someone so convinced that God matters, so committed to the relationship, that she will not pretend the unanswered prayer is fine. The accusation is a form of faith. The silence would be the abandonment.

## What We Have Lost by Aestheticizing Them

The King James Bible's translation of the Psalms is genuinely beautiful. That beauty has been, in certain respects, a problem. When Psalm 22 becomes a text we admire for its poetry — "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" — we have placed it at aesthetic distance from the person in the hospital room who is praying the same prayer without the archaic diction and without any sense that this psalm belongs to her.

She should know it belongs to her. That is precisely what it is for.

Eugene Peterson spent years rendering the Psalms into contemporary English for what became The Message [cite — confirm], not because the original translations were inaccurate but because through familiarity and liturgical distance they had become texts people admired rather than prayers people prayed. He wanted to give people the Psalms as working documents — as tools for the interior life rather than exhibits in the museum of religious literature.

The imprecatory psalms are where this becomes most acute. Psalm 137, which ends with a brutal image of infant deaths as an expression of the Babylonian exiles' grief and rage, is genuinely difficult. We do not sing it. We do not pray it. We explain it in footnotes. But it is in the canon, and its presence there is the canon's acknowledgment that the grief of people who have lost everything — who have watched their city burned and their families destroyed and their children taken — can produce a darkness that Scripture does not sanitize. The psalm does not endorse the image it contains. It names what grief at that depth sounds like. The naming is itself an act of pastoral care.

I have sat with people whose grief was of that depth. Whose loss was not metaphorical. Whose anger was not performing but actual, directed at God with the full force of someone who had believed and been devastated. The impulse of the American evangelical church is to move quickly in those rooms toward comfort and resolution, to offer the psalm of orientation before the person is ready for it, to skip the lament and go straight to the new orientation. I have done this. I have been the pastor who rushed the prayer toward resolution because the unresolved darkness made me uncomfortable. What I was communicating in those moments, without intending to, was that this person's honest condition was not acceptable in the presence of God — that they needed to adjust before they could pray properly.

The Psalter says otherwise.

## What Is Actually at Stake

Thomas Merton observed that the Psalms are in some sense the prayers of Christ himself — that when we pray the Psalms, we are praying in the voice of the one who learned them and prayed them before us [cite — confirm]. The cry of dereliction from the cross is Psalm 22. That is not incidental. Jesus, at the moment of maximum suffering, prayed the lament psalms. He had learned them in the synagogue at Nazareth. He prayed them when nothing else was left. The church that has excised the lament psalms from its prayer practice has, in a specific sense, excised from its prayer practice the prayers that Jesus prayed on the cross.

The Psalms also contain declarations of trust that only make sense coming from the other side of lament. Psalm 46's "God is our refuge and strength, an ever-present help in trouble" (verse 1) lands differently if you have prayed Psalm 44's accusation first. The trust has weight because it has been tested. The orientation of Psalm 23 — "Even though I walk through the darkest valley" — is not the orientation of someone who has never been in the valley. It is the orientation of someone who has been there and has found God there.

The church that only uses the trust psalms and skips the accusation psalms is producing a thin form of faith that is not equipped for the actual conditions of human life. People who pray only psalms of orientation are not prepared for the conditions that call for psalms of disorientation. When those conditions arrive — and they will — they discover that their prayer vocabulary does not include what they are experiencing, and they conclude that either their experience is wrong or their faith is insufficient. Neither is true. Their prayer education was simply incomplete.

## How to Pray Them Again

The most important thing is to read the whole collection, including the parts you have skipped. Particularly the parts you have skipped.

The next most important thing is to understand that the Psalms are permission. Permission to be angry at God. Permission to accuse. Permission to say that you cannot find him, that the prayers are not being answered, that the darkness is lasting longer than you were told it would. The permission is not the end of the prayer. It is the beginning of the kind of prayer that goes somewhere real, because it starts somewhere real rather than starting where we think prayer is supposed to start.

Pray Psalm 88 in a season when you have no resolution to offer. Pray Psalm 22 when you are sitting with someone who has none. Pray Psalm 137 with honesty about what grief at the level of total loss actually feels like, even if the specific circumstances differ. Pray Psalm 44 when the prayers have not been answered and the silence is piling up.

The Psalms are not poetry, though they are also poetry. They are not history, though they are also history. They are the voice of people in extremity, talking to God, and they are the record that God accepted that conversation — that the conversation counted, that the cry was heard even when the answer was slow or strange or not what was asked for.

Pray them. All of them.

Especially the ones that end in darkness.
