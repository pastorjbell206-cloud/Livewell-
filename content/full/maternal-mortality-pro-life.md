The United States has the highest maternal mortality rate among wealthy nations. By a significant margin. A woman in America is more than twice as likely to die from a pregnancy-related cause as a woman in Canada, more than three times as likely as a woman in Sweden [cite — confirm]. Black women in America die from pregnancy-related causes at roughly three times the rate of white women — a disparity that persists even when controlling for income and education [cite — confirm]. These deaths are not medical mysteries. They are, in substantial measure, preventable. They are the consequence of a healthcare system that has systematically underinvested in maternal care, that has closed labor and delivery units in rural hospitals, that provides inadequate prenatal care to low-income women, and that produces outcomes that track along racial and economic lines with striking consistency.

The American church that calls itself pro-life has been largely silent about this.

Not totally silent. Not every congregation. Not every pastor. But as a matter of broad ecclesial culture — the institutions, the publications, the advocacy organizations, the political energy that the American evangelical pro-life movement has generated and sustained for fifty years — the silence about maternal mortality is glaring.

The question is why. And the answer to that question is a diagnosis.

---

The pro-life movement as it has functioned in American evangelical culture has been organized, almost entirely, around the moment of abortion — the legal right to end a pregnancy. This focus has generated enormous political energy, produced legal changes of historic magnitude, and constituted the primary political identity of a large portion of American evangelical Christianity for two generations. Whatever one believes about the underlying questions, the movement's capacity for sustained political engagement has been remarkable.

But the human life at the center of that engagement has a mother. And that mother's life, in the framework that has dominated the American pro-life movement, has consistently received less attention than the life she carries. This is not a minor theological inconsistency. It is a fundamental asymmetry that reveals the actual operating logic of the movement — an operating logic that is more coherent as a political strategy than as a consistent theology of human life.

A theology of human life that treats the unborn with absolute moral seriousness while treating maternal mortality as a policy question beyond the church's concern has not achieved theological consistency. It has achieved political coherence at the cost of theological coherence. The life of the mother is as sacred as the life of the child. Both bear the image of God. Both matter in the same way, for the same reason. A pro-life commitment that is not also a maternal care commitment has made a selection among lives that the theology of the imago Dei does not permit.

---

The biblical tradition does not make this selection.

The law of Moses is distinctive in the ancient Near Eastern context for the specific protections it extends to pregnant women and to mothers. Exodus 21:22-25, whatever its specific application in debates about fetal life, establishes that harm to a pregnant woman carries consequences — the law is attentive to her vulnerability in ways that other ancient legal codes are not. The Psalms include multiple images of God as the one who formed the person in the mother's womb, who was present to the created life from before birth — but those texts do not exist in isolation from the broader tradition that also insists on care for the widow, the orphan, and the vulnerable person who has no one to advocate for her.

The widow in the New Testament is the paradigmatic figure of vulnerability — the person without protection in the social order, without income, without status, dependent on the decency of the community for her survival. The church's care for widows is described in Acts 6, organized and structured and taken seriously enough that the appointment of deacons was organized specifically to ensure it was being done adequately. Paul's instruction to Timothy about widows (1 Timothy 5) is detailed and specific, addressing the different circumstances of widows in ways that demonstrate the tradition's serious engagement with the reality of vulnerable women.

A church that is attentive to that tradition and not attentive to the death of women in childbirth has failed to read its own material carefully.

---

The racial dimension is not separable from the theological one.

That Black women in America die from pregnancy-related causes at three times the rate of white women is not a coincidence. It is the outcome of specific systems — the legacy of a medical culture that, until very recently, treated the pain reports of Black women as less credible than those of white women [cite — confirm]; the disproportionate closure of labor and delivery units in communities of color where hospital economics made the units unprofitable; the inadequacy of prenatal care access in communities where poverty and insurance gaps intersect; the chronic stress of living in a society where your racial identity marks you for differential treatment in every institutional context you move through.

These are not mysteries. They are outcomes that have been studied, documented, and named. They are the application of the same logic that the prophets condemned — the logic of the social order that assigns value to human beings according to their proximity to power, their economic productivity, their social standing. The prophet Amos did not catalog abstract injustices. He named specific economic and legal practices that produced specific suffering for specific people (Amos 2:6-7, Amos 8:4-6). The contemporary prophetic tradition owes the same specificity.

The church that professes concern for human life from conception and says nothing about the racial disparity in maternal mortality has made a choice about whose life matters enough to generate its energy. That choice requires theological justification that I have not seen provided.

---

The counter-argument deserves a direct hearing.

The argument is this: the unborn child has no political voice, no capacity to advocate, no access to any system of protection except the legal one. The pregnant woman is a person with full legal personhood, with the capacity to vote, to organize, to advocate for her own care. The urgency of protecting the unborn is therefore distinctive in a way that makes the comparative energy allocation defensible: the most vulnerable, those with no voice at all, deserve the most concentrated advocacy.

There is something to this argument. The relative vulnerability of the person who cannot speak at all is a real moral consideration. Advocacy for those with no voice is a genuine calling, and the tradition that insists on it is reading its Scripture.

But the argument does not survive examination of the actual maternal mortality data, because the women who are dying in childbirth are not, in most cases, dying because they lack political voice in the abstract. They are dying because the specific systems that serve them — the rural hospital that closed its labor and delivery unit, the insurance plan that does not cover adequate prenatal care, the medical provider whose implicit bias shaped his clinical judgment — are failing them. And the political voice they theoretically possess has not produced adequate correction of these systems, because the political coalition most energetically organized around the sanctity of human life has not organized around these specific failures.

The vulnerability is real. The absence of advocacy is real. The theological problem is real.

---

What would a genuinely comprehensive pro-life engagement with maternal mortality look like?

It would involve the same kind of sustained public advocacy that the movement has generated around abortion law. It would name specific policy failures — the Medicaid coverage gaps that leave low-income pregnant women without prenatal care, the rural hospital closures that have eliminated labor and delivery capacity in underserved communities, the inadequacy of postpartum care that allows preventable deaths in the weeks after delivery.

It would involve direct engagement with the racial disparity — not as an incidental detail but as a central moral scandal. The fact that where you live and what you look like determines whether you survive childbirth is not a secondary concern. It is a justice indictment that the biblical tradition is equipped to name and that the church, if it is serious about the imago Dei, must name.

It would involve the local congregation asking: what do the pregnant women in our community need, and are we providing it? Not the middle-class pregnant women whose insurance is adequate and whose prenatal care is excellent. The women without insurance, the undocumented women who fear that seeking care will generate consequences, the women in the county where the hospital closed its birthing unit four years ago.

---

I have preached the sanctity of human life. I believe it. The unborn child matters. That conviction is not the problem.

The problem is what the conviction does not include — the lives it does not name, the deaths it does not mourn, the systems it does not challenge because challenging them would require a political energy directed at targets other than the ones the movement has chosen.

Pro-life is a claim about which lives matter. The church that makes that claim is accountable for its full application. Not its convenient application. Its full one.

The woman who dies in childbirth matters. Her death is not a policy footnote. It is a theological indictment of every framework that named her child's life as sacred and fell silent about her own.

Both of them bear God's image. Both of them matter.

The church that knows this and does not say it has not yet decided to be pro-life in the fullest sense.

That decision is still available. Make it.
