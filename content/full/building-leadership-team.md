Look at how Paul planted a church and you will notice what he did before he left.

He appointed elders. In every city, in every congregation, he and Barnabas appointed elders and committed those congregations to the Lord with prayer and fasting (Acts 14:23). Not because Paul was finished with those churches — the letters prove he was not. But because a church that exists only as long as the planter stays is not yet a church. It is a gathering dependent on one man's presence, and Paul understood that dependency as a failure of formation rather than a sign of success.

This is the theology of the leadership team that most of us have not fully received.

---

The pastoral tradition that produced the American church has, in many of its forms, centered itself on the gifted individual: the preacher whose personality draws the congregation, the visionary whose energy moves the organization, the leader whose presence fills the room in ways that make the room feel like the point. There is something genuinely good in this tradition. The calling is real. The gift is real. The capacity to preach well and lead faithfully and love people consistently over decades is a grace worth receiving.

But the gifted individual is a starting point, not a destination. The end toward which the New Testament consistently moves is not a single leader with a following. It is a distributed body with shared responsibility, where each member contributes according to the Spirit's gifting and no one person bears the whole weight alone. Paul's metaphor in 1 Corinthians 12 is precisely this: a body in which every part is necessary, in which the parts that seem less significant are given special honor, in which the eye cannot say to the hand "I have no need of you" without injuring the whole.

The pastor who leads alone — who is the primary decision-maker, the sole vision-holder, the only one with sufficient insight to direct the congregation's future — has built a structure that is by design fragile. When he travels, it stalls. When he is sick, it drifts. When he eventually leaves or cannot continue, it collapses into a search committee trying to find another single person to carry what should have been distributed from the beginning.

I have built ministries that way. I know what they feel like from the inside — there is a particular satisfaction in being needed, in being the person through whom everything flows. I would not have called it pride at the time. I would have called it dedication. But the satisfaction of being indispensable is not the same thing as healthy leadership, and the difference becomes visible at precisely the moments when healthy leadership is most required.

---

The New Testament images of leadership consistently picture not a single leader with followers but a plurality of leaders with shared responsibility. The elders in Acts 20 are addressed collectively. Paul's farewell address at Miletus is given to the elders of Ephesus — plural — who together are charged with overseeing a congregation that is Paul's legacy. The leadership structures he describes in Titus and 1 Timothy are designed for multiplicity: multiple elders, multiple deacons, a distributed rather than centralized set of functions. This is not accident. It is theological conviction about how the Spirit distributes gifts and how the church is designed to function.

The elder lists in 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1 are not lists of credentials for a position. They are character profiles for people who will share in the pastoral work. "Above reproach, the husband of one wife, sober-minded, self-controlled, respectable, hospitable, able to teach, not a drunkard, not violent but gentle, not quarrelsome, not a lover of money. He must manage his own household well, with all dignity keeping his children submissive, for if someone does not know how to manage his own household, how will he care for God's church?" (1 Timothy 3:2-5). The household management criterion is especially revealing — not because the elder must have a perfect family, but because the way a person exercises authority in the most intimate and unobserved context reveals the character that will shape how he exercises authority in the most public one.

Building a leadership team is not primarily a management problem. It is a discernment problem — the work of asking who in this congregation has the gifts, the character, and the capacity to bear genuine responsibility for the mission. The answer is almost never the person who asks for the responsibility most loudly. It is almost always the person who has been quietly faithful over a long stretch of unglamorous work, who has earned trust in small things before being offered large ones, whose household is in reasonable order, who can be disagreed with without becoming defensive or punitive.

Those people are in your congregation. Finding them requires looking rather than waiting for them to appear fully formed and self-nominated.

---

Once found, they must be developed rather than just deployed. The pastor who adds people to a leadership team without investing in their formation is creating committee members, not leaders. The formation is the work — the long, patient work of shared discernment, honest conversation, accountability across hierarchies, and the kind of trust that accumulates only through time and repeated decisions made together.

What does formation look like concretely? It looks like bringing developing leaders into the room where real decisions are made rather than presenting them with outcomes after the fact. It looks like explaining the reasoning behind decisions, not just the decisions themselves, so that the reasoning becomes part of the team's shared framework over time. It looks like asking for their honest assessment even when the honest assessment will complicate your plans — and then actually receiving it without punishing the honesty that makes it possible.

This is harder than it sounds. The pastor who has been leading alone has usually been leading alone because alone is easier, faster, and less prone to the friction that genuine shared leadership produces. A room full of people with different perspectives and genuine authority will slow the process. It will complicate decisions you would have made cleanly on your own. It will produce disagreement you will have to work through rather than simply overriding.

The price is real. What you purchase with it is also real: people who own the mission, who have been inside the reasoning, who understand the congregation's actual condition, who can speak honestly to you when you are wrong, and who can carry the work when you cannot. That room is not a threat to your leadership. It is what makes your leadership worth having beyond your own tenure.

---

Nehemiah understood this. The most detailed account of leadership team formation in the Old Testament is not a description of one man single-handedly rebuilding a wall. It is an account of a leader who organized the community into sections, assigned each section to specific people with specific accountability, and then addressed the systemic problem — the exploitation of the poor by the wealthy that was undermining the rebuilding effort — as a leader who understood that the mission required the whole people, not just his personal energy (Nehemiah 3-5).

When Nehemiah's enemies tried to stop the work, his response was to post guards alongside workers — to maintain both the building and the defense by distributing responsibility rather than centralizing it. "I stationed the people by their clans, with their swords, their spears, and their bows... and I said to the nobles and to the officials and to the rest of the people, 'The work is great and widely spread, and we are separated on the wall, far from one another. In the place where you hear the sound of the trumpet, rally to us there. Our God will fight for us'" (Nehemiah 4:13, 19-20). Rally to us. Not rally to me.

---

The pastoral tradition has produced a specific form of leadership team dysfunction that is worth naming directly: the advisory board that exists to provide the pastor with cover while the pastor makes every substantive decision alone. This structure has all the formal appearance of shared leadership while preserving the actual reality of solo leadership. It produces people who are called leaders but are, in practice, ratifiers — who learn over time that their role is to affirm what the pastor has already decided rather than to genuinely deliberate toward decisions together.

The people in this structure usually know what it is. The brightest and most committed among them will eventually leave, because genuinely gifted leaders will not remain indefinitely in a structure that does not actually use their gifts. The ones who stay are those who prefer the comfort of membership in the inner circle to the accountability of genuine shared leadership. That is not the team you need. That is the team that protects the pastor's blind spots rather than challenging them.

The pastor who fears the leadership team fears being known too well. That fear is worth examining carefully, because it is usually the fear that is most worth addressing. The leaders who outlast their churches are almost always people who gathered around them men and women who knew their limitations and believed in their calling anyway — who were empowered to fill what the pastor could not.

---

The church was never yours to carry alone. The most honest reading of the New Testament account of leadership is that the pastoral calling is a calling into a community of leaders, not a calling to stand above one. The gifts you have are real. They are also partial, which the body metaphor insists upon. Your eye cannot see what the hand feels. Your vision for the congregation, however genuinely Spirit-given, requires the wisdom of people who are seeing what you are not — the people in the pews, in the counseling rooms, in the neighborhoods where the congregation lives its week.

Build the team. Not because you cannot lead alone — some days you can, and on those days the satisfaction is real. Build the team because the mission is too important and too long-ranging to survive the limits of any one person's capacity, vision, or tenure.

Paul didn't stay. He appointed elders and moved on to the next city. He did not interpret that movement as abandonment. He interpreted it as confidence — confidence that the Spirit who had begun a work in that community would continue it through the people he had identified and formed to lead it.

That confidence is available to you too. Build the team. Invest in the formation. Let go of what should not be held by one person alone.

The church will outlast you. The leadership team you build is the primary way that happens well.
