Of all the things that are slowly changing in pastoral culture, the stigma around professional counseling may be the most stubbornly persistent. Despite growing awareness of mental health issues in ministry, despite Barna data showing that 18 percent of pastors have thought about self-harm or suicide in the past year [cite — confirm], and despite the visible collapse of pastor after pastor who carried burdens they could not carry alone, many pastors still resist the idea of seeing a therapist with a tenacity they would never apply to, say, seeing a cardiologist for chest pain.

The resistance is not random. It is rooted in specific beliefs and cultural norms that are worth examining directly, because until those roots are addressed, the argument for counseling will continue to feel like an argument against something the pastor holds dear.

## The Reasons Pastors Give

The most common reason pastors cite for not seeking counseling is theological: they believe that the Scriptures, prayer, and the Holy Spirit should be sufficient for any struggle they face, and that turning to a secular professional implies a deficiency of faith. This is a real conviction sincerely held, and it deserves a real response rather than dismissal.

The response is this: God works through means. He heals bodies through doctors, teaches through educators, and adjudicates through lawyers. The consistent pattern of Scripture is not that God works exclusively through direct, unmediated action of the Spirit, but that He works through created agents and institutions as instruments of His grace. The Hebrew prophets understood this — Isaiah's own emotional and spiritual depletion under the juniper tree in 1 Kings 19 was addressed by God through the mundane means of food and sleep before the word of the Lord came again. Elijah did not need more prayer in that moment. He needed bread, and God sent an angel with it. A skilled counselor who understands the human mind and has been trained in evidence-based approaches to emotional and psychological health is not an alternative to God's provision. They are one of its forms.

The Reformation itself reasserted this logic against a form of spirituality that sought to bypass the ordinary means of grace. Luther's insistence on the Word, Calvin's doctrine of common grace — both represent a theological commitment to the idea that God's goodness reaches human beings through creaturely channels, not only through direct spiritual intervention. To refuse professional counseling on the grounds that the Spirit is sufficient is to apply a standard to psychological health that we would apply to no other area of human need.

## The Historical Moment We Are In

The pastoral mental health crisis is not a recent phenomenon. It has structural roots that run deep into the nature of the pastoral vocation. The nineteenth-century pastor worked in a community where ministry was one of the most socially respected professions, where the pastor's authority was largely unquestioned, and where the expectations placed on the pastor — though significant — were bounded by the social and geographic limits of a defined community. That world is gone.

The twenty-first-century pastor works in a context of institutional distrust, constant availability through technology, the expectation of professional-level competence across an impossible range of disciplines, and the performative demands of a culture in which pastoral identity is constructed and broadcast in public. The pastor must be an executive leader, a skilled therapist, a compelling public speaker, a social media presence, a fundraiser, a crisis counselor, a theologian, and a visible model of spiritual health — all simultaneously, often without the formation or support that any of these roles would require on its own. The structural weight of this is not a character failure. It is an institutional reality that produces suffering in largely predictable ways, and the refusal to seek help for that suffering is not courage. It is something closer to pride.

## A Steel-Manned Counter-Position

I want to take the theological resistance seriously on its strongest terms, not its weakest. The most sophisticated version of the argument goes like this: the categories that secular therapeutic culture uses to describe human suffering — disorders, trauma, attachment styles, dysregulation — are not neutral. They carry embedded anthropologies that often conflict with a Christian understanding of sin, grace, and human nature. The pastor who enters therapeutic culture uncritically may absorb a framework that explains all suffering as psychological and all solutions as psychological, and this may actually impoverish their pastoral ministry by replacing theological categories with therapeutic ones.

This is not a frivolous concern. James K.A. Smith has argued [cite — confirm] that our formative practices shape our imagination more deeply than our explicit beliefs, and a pastor who spends years in an unreflective therapeutic framework may find that they have slowly replaced the language of sin and repentance with the language of trauma and healing in ways they never consciously chose. The concern is real.

The answer to this concern is not to avoid counseling. It is to bring the same theological discernment to counseling that the pastor brings to any other practice. A pastor who engages therapy thoughtfully — who is willing to examine the anthropological assumptions behind a therapeutic framework and hold them against a biblical one, who brings their own theological convictions into the room rather than leaving them at the door — will likely find that the two disciplines, while genuinely different, are not as antagonistic as the caricature suggests. The best therapeutic practitioners are not ideologues. They are skilled students of human suffering who have developed genuine expertise in helping people through it. That expertise is worth engaging, critically and carefully.

## The Practical Concerns

Beyond theology, pastors also resist counseling for practical reasons: cost, time, confidentiality concerns, and the fear that being seen entering a counselor's office might generate questions they do not want to answer. These concerns are real, and they deserve practical responses.

On cost: many counselors offer sliding scale fees, and some denominations and pastoral care organizations provide counseling benefits specifically for pastors and ministry families. On confidentiality: licensed professional counselors are bound by strict confidentiality rules that protect your information with very limited exceptions. On being seen: telehealth has made it possible to receive high-quality counseling from the privacy of your own home or office without anyone needing to know you are doing it.

There is also a deeper practical concern that pastors rarely name but often feel: the fear that a counselor will find something that invalidates the calling. The fear that if someone actually looked closely at the interior life, they would discover that the pastor is not as spiritually healthy as the role requires — and that this discovery would unravel something they have spent their life building. I recognize this fear because I have felt it. It is the fear that honesty is too expensive. And it is precisely backward. The unexamined interior does not stay stable. It leaks. It shapes the preaching and the pastoral care and the marriage and the parenting in ways that careful, honest self-knowledge would at least allow to be chosen rather than merely suffered.

## A Concrete Scene

I think of a pastor I know — fifteen years in ministry, three churches, a reputation for pastoral wisdom among his peers. He came to counseling in his early forties not because he was in crisis but because a trusted friend looked at him across a table and said: "You are not as okay as you present." He was not. The first six months of counseling surfaced grief he had not allowed himself to name: the death of a vision he had carried for a decade, the slow erosion of a friendship with a former associate who had left under painful circumstances, the unresolved anger at his own father that was surfacing — he could see it clearly only in retrospect — in how he related to criticism from the church's elders. None of this was visible from the outside. All of it was shaping everything.

He is still in ministry. His preaching has more weight than it did before, which is not a coincidence. The weight came from the willingness to carry what was actually there rather than managing it out of awareness.

## What Counseling Actually Offers

What a good counselor offers is not what the pastor's accountability partner offers, and not what their mentor offers, and not what their spouse offers — though all of those relationships have genuine and irreplaceable value. A counselor offers a trained, structured, confidential space for examining the patterns, histories, and internal dynamics that most directly affect the pastor's wellbeing and leadership.

Many pastors who begin counseling discover that the presenting issue — burnout, anxiety, a difficult relationship — is connected to much older patterns they have been managing unconsciously for decades. Families of origin, early formative experiences, deeply embedded beliefs about their own worth and performance — these are not things that prayer and accountability naturally surface, not because those tools are insufficient, but because they are not designed for this particular kind of excavation. Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 4:7 that we carry this treasure in jars of clay, and the jars are real. They crack under pressure in ways that have identifiable patterns, and those patterns can be known, addressed, and changed.

Bonhoeffer wrote in *Life Together* that the person who cannot bear to be alone will damage community [cite — confirm]. The inverse is equally true: the person who refuses to be genuinely known — whose interiority is managed rather than inhabited — will eventually find that their external ministry is sustained by a kind of hollow performance. The counselor's room is not where holiness is produced. But it is often where the obstacles to it become visible.

## A Word to Church Boards and Congregations

If you are a church leader reading this and thinking about your pastor: one of the most significant investments you can make in the long-term health of your church is to provide your pastor with a counseling benefit and the explicit permission — from the pulpit, in the elder board, in the culture of the church — to use it without shame. The pastor who has access to ongoing professional support is a safer pastor, a more stable leader, and a more sustainable presence in the church than one who is carrying everything alone.

The congregation that expects its pastor to model perfect composure and spiritual steadiness in all circumstances — that treats pastoral vulnerability as a liability rather than an asset — is asking for a performance. Performances are not the same as pastoral care. The pastor who has done the interior work is the pastor who can sit with someone in a hospital room at midnight and actually be present rather than managing the moment. That presence is not accidental. It is formed. And counseling is one of the primary means of its formation.

The stigma will not disappear overnight. But it will begin to dissolve when the leaders of the church are willing to be honest about their own needs, and when the structures around them make that honesty possible without cost.

We tell the people we serve that they do not have to carry it alone. We should stop exempting ourselves.
