Psalm 24 opens with a statement so simple it can be missed: "The earth is the LORD's, and everything in it, the world, and all who live in it." This is not poetry about sunsets. It is a claim about property rights.

The earth belongs to God. We are tenants.

This is where the conversation about creation care should begin — not with climate data, not with policy debates, not with the question of what evangelicals think about environmentalism, but with who owns what. If the earth belongs to God, then the way we treat it is an act of stewardship, and stewardship is accountable. You do not get to trash what belongs to someone else and call it freedom. You are responsible for what you were given to tend.

---

The account in Genesis 2 uses a specific word for the human relationship to the garden: *abad* — to work, to serve. The same root is used for priestly service at the temple. The connection is not accidental. Adam's vocation in the garden is priestly in structure: he tends the place where God is present, exercises a representative function between Creator and creation, maintains the ordered beauty of what has been made. When the church reads the dominion mandate of Genesis 1:28 as permission for extraction and consumption, it has badly misread what dominion means in its own cultural context.

Ancient Near Eastern kingship was characterized by the king's responsibility to protect and sustain the land and its people. The king who dominated through exploitation was the bad king — the tyrant, the one who devoured what was entrusted to him. The king who fulfilled dominion was the one who cultivated, protected, extended the flourishing of what was under his care. Dominion in Genesis 1 carries this same expectation. It was stewardship. It was never license.

The fall in Genesis 3 disrupts this priestly vocation with consequences that are ecological as well as relational. "Cursed is the ground because of you; through painful toil you will eat food from it" (Genesis 3:17). The broken relationship between humanity and God produces a broken relationship between humanity and the soil. The corruption of the image affects the whole creation it was commissioned to tend. Paul makes this explicit in Romans 8:19–22 — the creation "has been groaning as in the pains of childbirth right up to the present time," subjected to frustration, waiting for the liberation that comes with the restoration of the children of God. Creation's suffering is not incidental to the human story. It is part of the same story.

---

I grew up in a tradition that treated creation care as a liberal distraction. Environmental concern was soft. It was political. The real issues were souls, not soil. I preached from that tradition without examining it, which means I preached a truncated gospel — one that cared for human beings while dismissing the world God made, the world God called good, the world God inhabits.

The tradition I was formed in was not stupid. It had reasons for its suspicion of environmentalism. Some of those reasons had to do with the theological error of treating creation as an end in itself rather than a gift from and to God. Some had to do with legitimate concerns about certain strands of environmental politics that displaced human dignity in favor of nature. These are real concerns.

But they do not constitute a biblical position. They constitute a cultural anxiety that was used to suppress a biblical concern. The church's abandonment of creation care was not the result of careful exegetical work. It was the result of culture war politics that sorted environmental concern into the wrong coalition and then declared the concern itself suspect.

The psalmist, the prophets, and the apostle Paul did not make that error.

---

The vision of the renewed creation at the end of the biblical story is not escape from the earth. It is the earth restored.

John's Revelation does not describe the saints escaping to heaven and leaving the world behind. It describes the New Jerusalem coming down to a renewed earth (Revelation 21:1–2). The trajectory of redemption is not away from matter. It is toward matter made whole. The New Jerusalem is a city — a social, material, architectural reality — not a cloud. The river of the water of life, the tree of life whose leaves are "for the healing of the nations" (Revelation 22:2) — these are not metaphors for spiritual states. They are the restored creation, finally fulfilling the vocation it was given in Genesis 2.

If that is where the story ends, it has implications for how we live in the middle of it. The church that treats the physical world as a waiting room to be abandoned at the end has not read the last two chapters of the Bible carefully. The eschatology of Scripture is creational, not escapist. The end is not the annihilation of matter but its redemption.

This has immediate consequences for how we think about the creation we are currently tenanting. We are not occupying a world about to be discarded. We are occupying the world God is in the process of renewing. What we do to it matters in that framework — not only as a moral question, but as a theological one.

---

Climate change is a justice issue as well as a creation issue, and these are not separable.

The populations most affected by rising seas, extreme weather events, crop failures, and water scarcity are disproportionately the world's poor — those with the fewest resources to adapt and the least political power to demand protection. [cite — confirm] The small island nations that will be uninhabitable within decades are not large emitters. They are the recipients of consequences produced overwhelmingly by wealthy industrialized economies. The subsistence farmers in sub-Saharan Africa whose crops are failing because of rainfall pattern disruptions did not produce the carbon that disrupted the patterns. They are absorbing the cost of someone else's consumption.

The church that cares about the widow, the orphan, and the stranger — the church that takes seriously the biblical insistence that God stands with the vulnerable — cannot be indifferent to a crisis that falls hardest on those same populations. To ignore creation justice is to ignore justice. The categories do not divide cleanly. They are the same category, seen from different angles.

Wendell Berry has written for decades about the connection between how we treat the land and how we treat each other — that the logic of extraction and exploitation applied to the earth is the same logic applied to the poor. [cite — confirm] The community that treats the soil as a resource to be used up rather than a gift to be tended has already made a decision about its relationship to the creation's other inhabitants. The farm that depletes its soil for short-term yield and the economic system that depletes its workers for short-term profit are operating from the same theological error: the assumption that the world and its contents exist to be consumed, not stewarded.

---

The early monastic communities understood something about creation that the contemporary church has mostly lost. The Benedictine rule of *ora et labora* — pray and work — included working the land with care, treating the material world as a site of encounter with God rather than a resource to be extracted. [cite — confirm] The monks who cleared forests and drained swamps in medieval Europe to create productive land were doing theology as well as agriculture. They were enacting the priestly vocation of Genesis 2 — tending the place where God is present, serving the creation as an expression of service to its owner.

That instinct was not environmentalism in the contemporary sense. It was older than that, and more basic. It was the recognition that the ground under your feet is holy because its owner is holy — that the care of the material world is a form of worship rather than a distraction from it.

---

What does faithfulness look like here? Not primarily a policy program, though policy matters. It looks like the church refusing to treat creation care as a partisan position — which means refusing the cultural alignment that made environmental concern a left-wing issue and indifference to it a right-wing virtue. The earth does not belong to any political coalition. It belongs to God. The question of how we treat it is a theological question before it is a political one.

It looks like congregations examining their own energy consumption, their own land use, their own patterns of waste and production and consumption, with the same seriousness they bring to examining their budget or their membership rolls. It looks like preachers willing to say from the pulpit what Psalm 24 says: this is not our world to consume. It belongs to God. We are accountable for what we have done to it.

It looks like Christian communities taking seriously the theological claim that creation itself has been included in the scope of God's redemptive work — that our bodies and the earth we inhabit are not incidental to the gospel but part of what the gospel is about.

---

The earth belongs to God. We have treated it as if it belonged to us, and more specifically as if it belonged to the present generation, free to consume in proportion to our capacity and to hand the consequences to people who had no voice in the decisions.

That is not stewardship. That is the old sin, dressed in modern machinery: the refusal to live within limits, the insistence that we are accountable to no one, the ancient hunger to be as God rather than to serve the God who made all of this and called it good.

The Psalmist does not ask whose it is. He states it. The claiming is already settled. The earth is the Lord's.

What remains is whether we will live accordingly — whether the people who claim to worship the earth's owner will treat it with the accountability that ownership implies, or whether we will continue to perform worship of God while treating God's world as our own to use up.

Those two things cannot both be true.

One of them has to give.
