Every pastor makes referrals — to counselors, to other ministries, to specialists in areas outside their competence. The reality of pastoral ministry is that the needs people bring to their pastor span an enormous range of domains, and no single pastor can adequately address all of them personally: addiction recovery, financial crisis, legal trouble, mental health conditions, domestic violence, grief, medical crises, and a hundred other specific situations that require specific expertise.

The quality of those referrals matters enormously. A pastor who refers people to resources that are genuinely helpful — that meet the person where they are and provide competent, caring support — becomes a meaningful point of connection to the broader ecosystem of care in their community. A pastor who refers people to resources without knowing whether those resources are actually effective, or who gives generic suggestions without genuine relationships with the people or organizations being recommended, is not serving the person well.

## Why Most Pastors' Referral Networks Are Inadequate

Most pastors' referral networks are built accidentally rather than intentionally. They know the counselor who goes to their church, the ministry that spoke at a conference they attended, the organization whose brochure is in the church lobby. These resources may or may not be well-suited to the people being referred to them. They may or may not have capacity. They may or may not share enough theological and relational framework with the pastor to provide continuity of care.

Building a genuinely effective referral network requires intentional investment — getting to know the specific resources in your community, understanding their approaches and their limitations, developing the relationship between pastor and referral partner that allows for genuine coordination of care, and maintaining awareness of what is available and what is not so that referrals are genuinely useful rather than perfunctory.

The inadequacy of most referral networks is not a reflection of pastoral negligence. It reflects the structural isolation in which most pastors operate. The pastor who works in a large church with a pastoral care team and established community partnerships has far better referral infrastructure than the solo pastor of a congregation of eighty. And the congregation of eighty is, statistically, the normative American church context — which means the problem of inadequate referral networks is widespread and mostly invisible.

## The Theology Beneath the Practice

There is a theological claim beneath the referral network question that is worth naming explicitly. The good Samaritan in Luke 10 does not simply leave the wounded man on the road with a prayer and a word of encouragement. He binds the wounds, transports the man, installs him in a place of care, and provides for his ongoing needs with his own resources (Luke 10:34-35). The parable's claim about what love of neighbor looks like is not abstract. It is specific, resourced, and followed through.

The pastor who gives a referral and considers the pastoral care completed has not quite reached the standard of the parable. The pastoral relationship does not end at the referral. It includes the ongoing relationship with the person being referred, the follow-up to know whether the referral landed well, and the willingness to remain present with the person through the process of getting help that the pastor cannot personally provide.

That is more demanding than most pastoral care training describes. It is also what the people who come to the pastor's office in crisis most need — not just a name and a phone number, but the confidence that their pastor is still with them, that the referral does not mean abandonment.

## The Core Relationships Worth Building

Every pastor should have developed genuine relationships with at least one competent Christian counselor or therapist — ideally several, with different specializations, since the needs that come to the pastor's office are varied. These relationships should be built on actual knowledge: the pastor should know the counselor's approach to their work, their faith, their specific areas of competence, their availability, their fee structure, and whether they have capacity to receive referrals.

Beyond counseling, the effective referral network includes: legal aid resources for people facing legal crises without financial resources, financial counselors who can provide competent guidance on debt and financial recovery, domestic violence and family crisis resources, addiction recovery communities (both faith-based and secular, since different people will be best served by different approaches), and medical social workers who can help people move through healthcare systems when illness or disability creates crisis.

Each of these relationships is built the same way: through deliberate, repeated personal contact. The pastor who has met the executive director of the local domestic violence shelter, who has toured the addiction recovery program, who has had coffee with three different counselors and knows their names and their specific gifts — that pastor is in a position to make referrals that actually help people. The pastor who has a brochure in a drawer is not.

## The Steel-Man Against Building This Network

A serious counterargument: the pastor who spends significant time building external referral relationships is investing time that could be spent in prayer, Scripture study, sermon preparation, and direct pastoral care. The pastor's primary gifts and calling are theological and spiritual. Becoming a social services navigator is not what the office requires. Let the deacons and the congregational care team manage these relationships; the pastor's time is better spent in the work that only the pastor can do.

This is a real argument. There is genuine wisdom in it — particularly for pastors who have a tendency toward activity over depth, who find the networking and relational building of community partnerships more energizing than the harder interior work of spiritual formation. The referral network should not displace prayer. It should be built by someone in the congregation, with the pastor providing oversight and theological direction rather than doing all of it personally.

But the counterargument proves too much if it concludes that the pastor can be completely ignorant of the referral options available and delegate it entirely without losing something pastoral. The pastor who has never met the counselor to whom they refer people, who does not know whether that counselor is actually competent or genuinely caring, who has no basis for confidence in the referral beyond institutional reputation — that pastor is providing a kind of pastoral care that stops at the door of the real need. The people who come in crisis deserve better than a referral to an unknown resource. They deserve a pastor who knows what they are sending them toward.

## How Other Pastors Fit In

One of the most underutilized referral resources for any pastor is other pastors. The pastor whose congregation does not have the particular community of faith that a specific person needs — the person of a different cultural background, the person working through a very specific identity question, the person who has been hurt by a congregation similar to yours — deserves to be connected to a pastor and community that can genuinely serve them.

This kind of referral requires enough genuine relationship with other pastors to know what their congregations are actually like — whether they are genuinely welcoming, what their culture is, whether the person being referred will be well-received. The relational investment in other pastors is not just for the pastor's own benefit. It is for the benefit of every person they will ever need to refer.

A pastor referral network is, in this sense, a form of care for people the pastor has not yet met — the person in crisis who will arrive two years from now in a situation that requires a congregation the pastor does not lead. The relationships built now make that future care possible.

## Building the Network Practically

The practical steps are less complicated than the conceptual framework. Begin with the five or six referral categories where you most regularly feel inadequate: counseling, addiction, financial crisis, legal, domestic crisis, and cross-cultural or cross-congregational referrals. In each category, identify who is genuinely excellent in your community. Schedule meetings. Get to know them as people. Understand how they work and who they serve best. Tell them what your congregation looks like and what kinds of referrals you make. Build the relationship before you need it.

This takes time. Set aside one meeting per month for referral network development, and within a year you will have a functioning network that most pastors have spent decades without. The investment is not large. The return — in the quality of care your congregation receives when they are in crisis — is significant.

And then tell your congregation that it exists. The people who need it most are often the ones who will never ask for it directly — who will suffer in silence because they do not know that the pastor has actual relationships with actual people who can help. Making the network visible is part of making the care available.

## What the Network Is For

A concrete pastoral scene: a couple came to me in financial crisis — not a management problem but a genuine collapse, the kind that involved multiple creditors, a house they were about to lose, and a shame so thick it had taken them eight months to ask for help. I could pray with them. I could listen. I could provide a theology of grace that was genuinely needed. What I could not do was help them make a plan.

Because I had spent time building relationships with a financial counselor at a local nonprofit who shared enough theological framework to hold the spiritual dimension of the conversation, I was able to make a referral that was specific, warm, and followed through. I called the counselor myself that afternoon. I told him the couple was coming. I stayed in touch with the couple through the process.

That is what a healthy referral network looks like in practice. Not a list of resources. A set of relationships built in advance of the need, activated on behalf of a specific person in a specific crisis, with the pastor still present throughout.

The couple kept their house. More importantly: they came back. They are still in our congregation. The referral was not a hand-off. It was a form of pastoral presence extended beyond what the pastor alone could provide.

That is the whole point.
