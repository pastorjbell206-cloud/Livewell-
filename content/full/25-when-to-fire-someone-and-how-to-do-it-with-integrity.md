No pastor goes into ministry hoping to fire people. The whole orientation of the calling is toward welcome, restoration, and the generous extension of grace. Termination sits at the opposite end of the relational spectrum from everything that pastoral training emphasizes, which is precisely why so many pastors handle it badly — either by waiting far too long and allowing a damaging situation to continue, or by doing it abruptly and without the care that the person and the moment deserve.

Letting someone go is one of the hardest leadership tasks in any context. In ministry, it carries the additional weight of the relational and spiritual dimensions — this is not just an employment decision but a pastoral one, affecting a real person's livelihood, sense of call, and relationship with the church community. Getting it right, or at least getting it less wrong than the default, requires both clarity and genuine compassion.

## The Waiting Problem

The most common mistake in church staff terminations is waiting too long. The pastoral instinct toward patience and mercy, the genuine discomfort of the conversation, the hope that things will improve on their own, the fear of being wrong — all of these conspire to delay the inevitable past the point where it could be handled with the most grace and the least damage.

By the time most church leaders finally have the termination conversation, the situation has been allowed to deteriorate to the point where the departure is painful for everyone involved, the relationship has been strained beyond easy repair, and other staff members who have been watching the situation have drawn their own conclusions about whether leadership will protect the culture. Earlier is almost always kinder — kinder to the person, kinder to the team, and kinder to the ministry.

I have made this mistake. I have watched a situation that needed a clear decision and turned it into an eighteen-month pastoral project, hoping that enough encouragement and support would eventually produce the change that was not coming. The person I was trying to preserve ended up more wounded by the extended ambiguity than they would have been by an earlier, cleaner decision. The team I was trying to protect endured eighteen months of watching me fail to act. Mercy that delays the necessary is not mercy. It is avoidance wearing mercy's clothes.

## The Theology of Termination

There is a theological framework for this that most pastoral training never provides, and its absence is part of why pastors handle it so badly.

The image of God in every human being (Genesis 1:26-27) is the non-negotiable foundation. The person being terminated is not a problem to be solved — they are a person for whom Christ died, whose dignity does not diminish because their role in this organization has ended. Every decision about how to handle a termination flows from that conviction, or it should.

But the imago dei also applies to the other staff members whose work environment is affected by a poorly performing colleague, to the congregation members whose care is inadequate when a pastoral staff member is not functioning well, and to the organization as a whole whose ability to pursue its mission is constrained by leadership failures allowed to continue. The theological argument for acting clearly and promptly is not a rejection of grace. It is the recognition that grace for one person that imposes ongoing cost on others is not the same thing as grace.

Paul's instruction to the Corinthian church about the man who had sinned seriously — "You must not associate with anyone who claims to be a brother or sister but is sexually immoral" (1 Corinthians 5:11) — is not a model for every staff termination. But it reflects the principle that the community's health sometimes requires a separation that is difficult and specific, done for reasons that can be clearly stated and that are oriented ultimately toward restoration rather than merely removal. Paul's goal in 1 Corinthians 5 is that the person "may be saved on the day of the Lord" (5:5). The termination, properly handled, can serve that same goal.

## The Counter-Position

The serious counterargument is that the power differential between a pastor and a church staff member makes the decision to terminate inherently coercive — that the theology of grace and the pastoral relationship create obligations that organizational efficiency cannot override. The person hired into church ministry has often understood themselves to be called, not merely employed, and terminating that position involves not just ending a job but making a judgment about their calling. Who is the pastor to make that judgment?

This is not a cynical argument. It is a serious one. The pastor who is too quick to terminate — who resolves personnel problems by removing people rather than developing them, who lacks the patience for the difficult work of genuine growth — is making a category error about what pastoral leadership is for.

The response is not to remove the power differential — it is real and cannot be wished away — but to be accountable within it. Accountability means involving the elder board or governance structure in significant termination decisions. It means ensuring that feedback has been clear, consistent, and genuinely developmental rather than a paper trail. It means being honest about your own failures in the relationship before acting on the other person's. And it means maintaining the pastoral relationship after the employment relationship ends — not pretending the conversation was easy, not abandoning the person to figure out the aftermath alone.

The person whose calling should be honored in a termination is not just the person being let go. It is the congregation, the other staff members, and the broader community the church is called to serve.

## Before the Conversation: Documentation and Process

A termination that arrives without prior documentation and process is both pastorally unkind and legally risky. Before a termination conversation, there should be a clear record of the performance issues or behavioral concerns, the specific feedback and expectations that were communicated, the support and development opportunities that were offered, and the clear communication that continued employment was contingent on change. This documentation protects the organization and ensures that the terminated employee cannot credibly claim they were surprised.

It also provides the opportunity for genuine course correction. The performance management process that precedes a termination should be a genuine attempt to help the person succeed — not a paper trail building toward a predetermined conclusion. Some people, given clear feedback and genuine support, do change. The process that makes termination possible also makes redemption possible.

## The Conversation Itself

The termination conversation should be brief, clear, and kind. It is not the time for extensive review of all the problems — that should have happened in the prior feedback conversations. It is the time to communicate the decision clearly, to explain what the next steps look like practically, and to treat the person with the dignity they deserve as a human being made in the image of God.

Be honest about the reason. Vague language — "this isn't the right fit" — leaves people without the honest feedback they need to grow, and it tends to fuel speculation and rumor. A clear, honest, specific explanation — delivered with genuine compassion and without extended justification — is the kindest version of a hard conversation.

Have another leader present. The witness matters — both for accountability to the organization and as pastoral support for the person being let go, who should not have to hold the weight of the conversation alone.

Follow up with practical care: clear information about severance and benefits, an offer of reference where appropriate, genuine prayer for their next chapter. The prayer is not performative. If it cannot be genuine, do not offer it. But if you can pray honestly with the person you are letting go — if you can mean it when you ask God to open doors they cannot yet see — that prayer may be the most important pastoral act of the entire conversation.

## After the Conversation

The person you let go is still someone for whom Christ died. The ending of the employment relationship does not end the pastoral one.

This does not mean maintaining the same level of involvement in their life — the relationship has changed, and pretending it has not is dishonest. It means remaining genuinely open to them, following up with genuine care, being willing to be honest about the conversation and your own failures within it if they ask, and treating their departure from the staff as the beginning of a different chapter rather than the end of the relationship.

The rest of the staff needs a conversation too — one that is honest about the fact of the departure, appropriately discreet about the reasons, and clear about the vision going forward. Silence creates rumors. Vagueness creates anxiety. The leader who can say "this was a hard decision and we made it because we care about both this person and this team" — without disclosing confidential personnel information — is giving the remaining staff what they need to trust that the culture is healthy enough to handle hard things honestly.

## What It Costs

Every significant termination leaves a mark. The pastor who handles it well still carries the weight of it — the specific face, the specific conversation, the specific grief of an ending that was necessary but not desired. I carry several of those conversations still. I would not undo the decisions. But the decisions cost something, and the pastor who acts as though they do not is not being strong — they are being defended.

The weight is appropriate. It means the person mattered. The work is to carry the weight honestly while still acting with the clarity the situation requires.

Not every situation requires termination. The process of clear feedback and genuine support should be real, not procedural. But when the situation requires the hard decision, the pastor who can make it with honesty, speed, compassion, and genuine follow-through is not betraying the pastoral calling. They are fulfilling it — in one of its most demanding forms.

That is what integrity looks like when the conversation is unavoidable.
