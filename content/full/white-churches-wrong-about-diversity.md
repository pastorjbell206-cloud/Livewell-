Revelation 7:9 describes a vision John received of the throne of God: a great multitude from every nation, tribe, people, and language, standing before the Lamb. John describes it as a vision of what is coming. The white evangelical church in America has often treated it as a distant promise rather than a present obligation — something to aspire to eventually, while continuing to operate in ways that make the aspiration functionally empty.

I am a white pastor. I lead a predominantly white congregation. What I am about to say implicates me more than anyone else in the room.

The conversation about racial diversity in predominantly white churches is real, and it is growing, and in many cases it is earnest. The problem is not the desire. The problem is that the desire is being pursued through frameworks that ensure it will fail, and the people on the receiving end of those frameworks have been explaining why for decades, and predominantly white churches have been slow to listen.

---

The error that predominantly white churches make when they engage the question of racial diversity is not usually malicious. It is structural.

The church decides it wants to be more diverse — a desire that is real and, in many cases, genuinely motivated by the text. Then it proceeds to pursue diversity in ways that ensure it will fail. The worship style does not change. The leadership structure does not change. The cultural assumptions embedded in every program, every process, every piece of communication do not change. What changes is a marketing effort: the website gets updated, some stock photos are diversified, a visiting preacher from another ethnicity is invited for a designated Sunday.

And then the pastor is confused when people of color do not stay.

They do not stay because they were not invited. They were recruited. There is a difference, and it is not subtle. Invitation says: come as you are, and this community will be changed by your presence. Recruitment says: come as you are, and you will be expected to become like us. People of color in America have developed a finely tuned capacity for detecting the difference between those two messages, because the cost of misreading it has historically been very high. The church that presents itself as inviting while operating as recruiting will be perceived accurately, not graciously.

---

The second error is that the conversation about diversity gets framed as a conversation about aesthetics rather than about power.

The predominantly white church that adds a diverse worship team, or adjusts the music mix, or makes the welcome language more inclusive — and then calls that diversity work — has mistaken the surface for the structure. It has changed the decoration without touching the building. The structural question is not what the worship set looks like. The structural question is: who makes decisions? Whose theological instincts shape the sermon series? Whose cultural assumptions are written into the governance documents? Whose discomfort, when expressed, gets taken seriously?

If the answers to those questions are all the same — if power rests entirely with the same people it rested with before the diversity conversation began — then the worship set is decoration on an unchanged building.

This is where the language of "cultural competency" and "inclusion" often fails, because it trains the eye on behavior and communication while leaving the power arrangement undisturbed. A congregation can be trained to use more inclusive language, to avoid certain phrases, to demonstrate cultural sensitivity in its communications — and maintain, entirely intact, a leadership structure where every significant decision is made by people who share the same ethnic background, the same class position, and the same cultural formation.

Emmett Price, who has written extensively on the relationship between the Black church tradition and American Christianity [cite — confirm], has argued that the predominantly white church's approach to diversity typically imports people of color into white institutions rather than allowing the presence of people of color to change the institution itself. This is a precise description of the asymmetry. The institution is the fixed point. The incoming person is expected to adapt. That is not diversity. That is assimilation with a diverse welcome.

---

The third error is the most subtle and the most damaging: treating diversity as a gift the majority culture offers to the minority, rather than a gift the minority culture offers to the whole body.

The white church that approaches diversity with the posture of benefactor — we are welcoming you in, we are opening our doors, we are making room — has already positioned the conversation wrong. It has placed itself at the center and the incoming community at the periphery, as recipient of the white church's generosity. This is not the New Testament vision. The New Testament vision is not of majority culture opening its doors to minority culture. It is of a new humanity being created in Christ, in which the categories that once defined who was in and who was out have been fundamentally reorganized.

Galatians 3:28 is not a statement about tolerance. It is a statement about ontology. "There is neither Jew nor Greek, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus." What we are in Christ reorganizes what we were. The boundary markers that previously determined community membership — ethnic identity, social status, gender — are not erased, but they are no longer determinative. The community is organized around a different center.

This means the predominantly white church pursuing genuine diversity must be willing to be changed by it. Not changed at the level of marketing, but changed at the level of power, culture, and leadership. Changed enough that a Black pastor or a Latino elder or an Asian worship leader is not experiencing the church's diversity program but co-authoring the church's identity. That requires the white church to hold its cultural particularity more loosely — to recognize that its worship practices, its theological emphases, its governance assumptions are not universal Christian norms but specific cultural expressions of Christianity, and that other expressions are not less than them, only different.

---

The historical dimension cannot be skipped if this conversation is to be honest.

The racial segregation of the American church did not happen accidentally or gradually. It was enforced, in many cases, by explicit policy. The denominational structures that supported slavery, the church bodies that actively opposed civil rights legislation, the white flight from urban congregations that left the cities to the Black church while white churches built their institutions in the suburbs — these are not distant history. They are the recent past. Many people alive today remember them. The children of people who experienced them are in the workforce now.

The predominantly white church that wants to have a diversity conversation without reckoning with this history is asking people of color to accept a friendship that refuses to acknowledge the wound. That is not friendship. It is the request to begin at a point of reconciliation that has not yet been reached, to skip over the acknowledgment that makes reconciliation possible.

This is why diversity work in the white church, done honestly, begins not with a new program but with a willingness to hear the history that the community you are seeking to welcome carries. This requires listening that goes beyond comfort. It requires the willingness to hold still under critique rather than explaining or defending. It requires what James 1:19 calls being "quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger" — applied specifically to the institutional dimension rather than only the interpersonal one.

---

I have not done this well.

I have been the pastor who felt good about the intentions while being defended from the feedback. Who received the critique and processed it privately and thought I had heard it without actually changing anything. Who believed the diversity conversation was going well because it was going smoothly — not understanding that smoothness in a power-asymmetric relationship often means the less-powerful party has learned it is not safe to say the hard thing.

The feedback is the beginning. Not the goal — the beginning.

The white church that is receiving honest critique about its diversity failures is in a better position than the white church that is not, because the honest critique means there are still people willing to try. When the critique stops coming, it usually means the people who were trying have concluded it is not worth the cost. Their silence is not approval. It is departure.

---

What does genuine diversity work look like? Some specifics.

It looks like building relationships with leaders of color before you start a diversity initiative — developing genuine, non-transactional friendships with pastors from different backgrounds, not to gain their endorsement for your program but because the body of Christ is impoverished by their absence from your relational network.

It looks like inviting critique of the systems you have built and sitting with that critique long enough to act on it — not inviting it and then managing it, not offering the appearance of openness while maintaining the reality of defensive unchangingness.

It looks like asking the question: whose voice is absent from the room where decisions get made? And then figuring out why, and changing it.

It looks like being willing to lose something — some cultural comfort, some authority, some of the unconscious ease of operating in an institution that was built in your image — for the sake of the vision that John saw: every tribe, every tongue, every people, gathered before the one who makes all things new (Revelation 21:5).

That is not a program. It is a conversion.

The question is whether the predominantly white church is willing to undergo it.
