The Bible contains roughly 2,350 verses addressing money, wealth, poverty, and economic justice [cite — confirm]. One scholar I read years ago put the number at over 2,000 for economic themes alone — making it the subject addressed more frequently than any other in the canon, including prayer, faith, and eternal life. Against that canon, the church's teaching on economic ethics has been, in most of the congregations I have served and most of the conferences I have attended, remarkably thin.

On sexual ethics, the church has not been thin. On sexuality, the church has maintained a clear, consistent, publicly stated position and defended it against considerable cultural pressure. Sermons are preached. Books are written. Denominational battles are fought to their bitter conclusions. The clarity is real and the courage sometimes genuine.

The disproportion requires explanation.

---

I am not arguing that the church's sexual ethics are wrong to hold or wrong to teach. The biblical witness on sexuality is coherent and worthy of careful proclamation. But it is worth asking why the subjects on which the Scriptures are most extensive have received the least consistent pastoral attention, while the subjects on which the Scriptures are less extensive have received the most.

The answer is not primarily theological. It is sociological.

Sexual ethics make demands on the behavior of individuals. They govern what happens between consenting people in private. They address choices that do not depend on economic position or social power — choices that are equally available to the poor and to the wealthy, to the powerful and to the powerless. When the church proclaims a sexual ethic, it challenges the choices of individual members without necessarily challenging the structures from which those members benefit. The rich man and the poor man are equally subject to the sexual ethic. Their bank accounts are not touched by the sermon.

Economic ethics are different. Economic ethics make demands on systems. They challenge the structures that produce inequality, concentrate wealth, and convert human beings into instruments of production. When the church proclaims a full economic ethic — the kind the prophets proclaimed — it challenges not just the choices of individual members but the arrangements from which the wealthiest members benefit most. The sermon about money does not just address what people do in private. It addresses what they do with everything they have and how those decisions connect to the conditions of people they will never meet. And it implies a verdict about the structures that produce those conditions.

This is a much more uncomfortable sermon to give. It is also a much more uncomfortable sermon to receive, if you are receiving it in the congregation of a suburban American church while sitting next to people whose combined financial assets would address significant human need.

I have been more comfortable talking about sex. I have preached careful sermons about sexuality with more confidence and less anxiety than I have preached about wealth and poverty and economic obligation. This is a failure I can identify, and naming it is the first step toward doing differently.

---

The prophets did not make this separation. The eighth century BC prophets — Amos, Hosea, Micah, Isaiah — were not primarily concerned with the sexual behavior of Israel. They were concerned with economic exploitation, with the crushing of the poor, with the corruption of the legal system by those with money to buy verdicts, with the accumulation of land by those who had the power to take it from those who did not. Amos describes a society where "they sell the righteous for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals — those who trample the head of the poor into the dust of the earth and turn aside the way of the afflicted" (Amos 2:6-7). This is the grievance. This is what God is bringing to judgment.

Micah pronounces woe on "those who devise wickedness and work evil on their beds. When the morning dawns, they perform it, because it is in the power of their hand. They covet fields and seize them, and houses, and take them away; they oppress a man and his house, a man and his inheritance" (Micah 2:1-2). The sin Micah names is economic predation — the systematic use of legal and economic power to take from those who cannot protect what they have. It is not adultery. It is not false worship, though false worship follows from it. It is the organization of economic life around the accumulation of the powerful at the expense of the vulnerable.

Isaiah 5:8 pronounces the same woe in language that has not aged: "Woe to those who join house to house, who add field to field, until there is no more room, and you are made to dwell alone in the midst of the land." The accumulation of real estate. The elimination of neighbors through displacement. The production of a landscape where the wealthy hold everything and everyone else has been crowded out. If this does not sound recognizable in twenty-first-century America, you are not paying attention to the housing market.

---

Jesus talked about money more than he talked about almost anything else except the kingdom of God — and the connection between those two subjects was, in his teaching, very close. The parable of the rich fool in Luke 12. The parable of the rich man and Lazarus in Luke 16. The story of Zacchaeus. The encounter with the rich young ruler. The cleansing of the Temple. The widow's offering. The Sermon on the Mount, where the accumulation of treasure on earth is named as an act of divided loyalty: "No one can serve two masters. Either you will hate the one and love the other, or you will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve both God and money" (Matthew 6:24).

That last verse is used in stewardship campaigns. It is very rarely preached to its full conclusion. If money is a rival master — a claim on loyalty that competes with God's claim — then the church's silence about economic structures is not a neutral position. It is a form of service to the rival master. Every time the church proclaims a clear sexual ethic and a vague economic ethic, it is, functionally, telling its wealthiest members that their money is not subject to the same scrutiny as their bodies. That the kingdom of God makes robust claims on their sexuality and attenuated claims on their bank accounts. That God is interested in what happens in their bedrooms and largely uninterested in what happens in their portfolios.

Jesus did not teach this. The prophets did not teach this. James did not teach this — and James 5 is about as explicit an economic indictment as the canon contains: "Come now, you rich, weep and howl for the miseries that are coming upon you. Your riches have rotted and your garments are moth-eaten. Your gold and silver have corroded, and their corrosion will be evidence against you and will eat your flesh like fire. You have laid up treasure in the last days. Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts" (James 5:1-4).

The wages kept back by fraud. Crying out. The ears of the Lord of hosts. This is not a suggestion about generous giving. It is an indictment.

---

The honest account of why the American church is more comfortable with sexual ethics than economic ethics is this: the sexual ethic, however challenging to individuals, does not threaten the institutional survival of the church. The economic ethic, preached consistently and to its full implications, very well might.

Churches are funded by the generosity of their wealthiest members. The twenty percent of givers who produce eighty percent of the budget [cite — confirm] tend to be people for whom the economic ethic is most costly. A pastor who preaches the full prophetic tradition on wealth and poverty and economic justice is preaching to the people whose giving keeps his church operational. That is a conflict of interest that is structural, not personal, and it shapes what gets preached without anyone consciously deciding to compromise. The avoidance is often not cowardice. It is the accumulated effect of a thousand small decisions not to go quite that far this week.

I have made those decisions. I recognize the calculation. I have told myself that the congregation is not ready, that the political implications are too easily misread, that the prophetic word needs to be earned through relationship before it can be received. Some of that is true. None of it is entirely honest.

---

The way forward is not to stop teaching sexual ethics. It is to start teaching economic ethics with the same clarity and courage. To preach James 5 with the same willingness we bring to 1 Corinthians 6. To name wage theft as the Scriptures name it. To say clearly that the organization of an economy around the systematic extraction of wealth from those who cannot protect themselves is not a matter of political opinion — it is a matter of prophetic judgment, and the prophetic tradition is not ambiguous about it.

This will lose some people. Every prophetic word loses someone. The rich young ruler walked away. Jesus watched him go. He did not revise his statement about what the man needed to do.

The church that only challenges the sexual choices of its members while leaving their economic arrangements unexamined has not yet proclaimed the whole gospel. It has proclaimed a partial gospel — a gospel comfortable enough to be received without cost by those whose social position is determined by the very arrangements the full gospel would challenge.

The prophets spoke to the whole life. Jesus taught about the whole life. The church that speaks about only part of the life is not being prophetic. It is being selective, which is another name for cowardice dressed in theological clothing.

The Bible has a lot to say about money. It is time the church started saying it.
