The word is found early in the Exodus story, in the episode most preachers move past on their way to more dramatic material. God rains bread from heaven — manna, the substance with no prior reference, a word that means roughly "what is it?" — and the instruction accompanying it is specific and strange: each household is to gather as much as it needs for that day. No more. The household that gathers more and tries to store it will find it rotten by morning. On the sixth day, gather two portions, because the seventh belongs to rest and no manna will come. The system is designed against accumulation.

This is not an efficiency arrangement. It is a theological statement about the nature of provision — about who owns the supply, who determines the portion, and what happens to communities built around the refusal to trust that enough will come again tomorrow. Exodus 16 is not a food-distribution logistics story. It is the first extended act of formation for a people who have been enslaved in an economy built on surplus extraction — pharaoh's logic — and who must now learn a different logic for a different kind of life.

They struggle with it. They keep trying to store extra. They find it corrupted. The lesson requires repetition because the lesson is fighting against something deep in them, something the years in Egypt had written into their instincts: the person who controls tomorrow's supply is safe, and the person who trusts today's provision and has nothing left by nightfall is vulnerable. Pharaoh's formation goes down a long way.

So does ours.

The civilization that produced the contemporary American church has been running on a different account of provision than Exodus 16 describes. The Industrial Revolution, with its logic of scale and accumulation, produced in the nineteenth century a new kind of wealth — not the aristocratic landholding wealth that had defined European society for centuries, but a mobile, abstract, endlessly compoundable wealth that measured success not in the size of the harvest but in the rate of the return. The cultural formation that accompanied this new economy was correspondingly different: the virtuous person was not the one who received enough and rested but the one who reinvested, leveraged, expanded, compounded. The parable of the talents in Matthew 25:14-30 was read not as a story about faithfulness to what has been entrusted but as a warrant for the investment logic that governed the Gilded Age. That reading required ignoring the context in which the parable appears — which is a sustained discourse about the reversal of the powerful and the vindication of the vulnerable — but the reading was convenient and it stuck.

We inherited it. I inherited it. The pastor who grew up in suburban American Christianity absorbed, alongside the theology, an entire account of what flourishing looks like that has almost nothing to do with manna and almost everything to do with the quarterly return.

The theologian who has done the most rigorous work on how modern economic formations shape desire and imagination is James K.A. Smith, whose work on cultural liturgies traces the way that practices and environments form us before ideas do [cite — confirm]. Smith's argument is that the mall, the stadium, the market are not merely neutral spaces where pre-formed people make choices — they are formative environments that train desire, that habituate us toward certain ends, that produce in us a particular vision of the good life. The good life, in the formation of consumer capitalism, is an expanding life: more space, more options, more accumulated capability. Enough is a resting point on the way to more. It is never a destination.

Against this formation, the Scriptures offer something comprehensive and strange: a theology of enough.

The manna story is the earliest statement, but it is not alone. Proverbs 30:7-9 contains one of the most remarkable prayers in the biblical literature: "Two things I ask of you, Lord; do not refuse me before I die: Keep falsehood and lies far from me; give me neither poverty nor riches, but give me only my daily bread. Otherwise, I may have too much and disown you and say, 'Who is the Lord?' Or I may become poor and steal, and so dishonor the name of my God." The prayer refuses both poles. Not poverty. Not wealth. Enough. The wisdom here is that both poverty and riches create conditions hostile to covenant faithfulness — poverty through desperation, wealth through the particular blindness that comes with comfort.

Jesus returns to this territory repeatedly, in a way that the church has consistently tried to soften. The Sermon on the Mount's teaching on anxiety — "do not worry about your life, what you will eat or drink, or about your body, what you will wear" (Matthew 6:25) — is not addressed to the desperately poor. It is addressed to people with enough options to have something to worry about. And the argument is theological before it is practical: the God who clothes the grass of the field and feeds the birds of the air is not indifferent to your need. The anxiety that drives accumulation is, at its root, a failure of trust. The accumulation does not resolve the anxiety — it reseeds it, because the horizon of enough under accumulation logic is always just beyond what is currently possessed.

The parable of the rich fool in Luke 12:13-21 is perhaps the sharpest statement. The man's crops are abundant. His response is entirely reasonable by any standard the contemporary church has absorbed: expand the barns, store the surplus, rest secure. God calls him a fool. Not because he was dishonest or violent or openly unjust. Because he organized his entire existence around the logic of accumulation and called it security — and he was about to discover that the logic was wrong at the most fundamental level. The grain in his expanded barns would do him no good. He had become rich toward himself and poor toward God.

What does it mean to be poor toward God? I have lived in that territory. I recognize it from the inside. It is the state of having so organized your desires around what you control that the very concept of dependence on anything beyond yourself has atrophied. The person poor toward God is not necessarily the person who has rejected belief. He may attend church faithfully, may give generously by percentage, may have his theology entirely correct. But the underlying formation — the deep grammar of his life — is the grammar of accumulation and control. He has never genuinely sat with the manna question: what if tomorrow's provision belongs to tomorrow, and my task today is to receive what today requires and trust the rest?

There is a serious counter-argument here, and it deserves honest engagement. The counter-argument goes: "This reasoning, taken seriously, would produce irresponsibility. It would counsel against retirement savings, against insurance, against the prudent planning that protects families from catastrophe. Are you really arguing that accumulation is sinful?" No. The theology of enough is not a theology of improvidence. The ant in Proverbs 6 stores food in summer for winter's use, and is commended for it. Joseph's seven years of Egyptian surplus savings in Genesis 41 protected an entire region from famine. Prudent provision is not the same as the endless expansion of appetite that the manna prohibition targets.

The distinction is between provision for need and accumulation as an end in itself — between the barn that holds enough and the barn that must always be expanded because the question of enough can never be answered. The theology of enough does not prohibit saving. It prohibits the making of accumulated wealth into the organizing principle of a life, the thing that produces security, the ultimate hedge against the vulnerability of being a creature in a world you did not make and do not control.

The practical question for the church is not primarily about individual financial practice, though it reaches there. It is about institutional practice. American churches hold enormous collective wealth — in endowments, in buildings, in investments [cite — confirm]. That wealth is almost always justified as necessary infrastructure for mission. And some of it is. But some of it is the barn. Some of it is the accumulation that protects the institution against a vulnerability that the institution, in its formation, has learned to fear more than it fears the judgment of the God who asked the rich fool whether he had made provision for the moment his soul would be required of him.

The theology of enough is not comfortable to sit with. It asks questions that have no comfortable answers: How much of what you hold is need and how much is anxiety made solid? What would it mean, actually, to give as though tomorrow's provision were not your problem? What would it mean for the church to operate with a budget that had no cushion, that trusted the God it preaches?

I am not certain I know how to live this. I am certain that the church I grew up in never taught it — not because the teachers were dishonest, but because the formation that surrounded us had made it unthinkable. We were shaped by the same cultural liturgies as everyone else, and those liturgies produce people for whom enough is always one barn-expansion away.

The manna spoiled when they tried to store it.

That was not punitive. That was pedagogical. The God who provided it was teaching them something about the nature of the economy they were being invited into — an economy of trust rather than control, of today's provision rather than tomorrow's security, of community whose members could not individually hoard because the system itself was designed against hoarding.

The kingdom of God is not the American economy. The sooner the church remembers this, the more it will have to say to a world that is discovering, with increasing desperation, that the American economy does not answer the deepest questions of what it means to have enough.
