The diversity initiative started with a conversation in the elder meeting. Someone noted that the neighborhood had changed. The congregation had not changed with it. A Hispanic family attended for three months and stopped coming. No one was sure why. There was general agreement that the church should reflect the community around it, that the image of God was displayed more fully in the gathering of people unlike each other, that something was missing when everyone in the room looked the same. The conversation was earnest. The people in it meant what they said.

Then someone suggested hiring a worship leader of color. Someone else proposed a special service celebrating different cultural expressions of faith. There was talk of changing some music. The committee formed. The initiative launched. Two years later, the congregation looked essentially the same.

This is the story that repeats in predominantly white evangelical churches across America, with variations in the programs but consistency in the outcome. The diagnosis is usually that the programs did not work. The programs were not the problem. The diagnosis was.

## What Diversity Is Not

Diversity is not the presence of different skin colors in the same building. The mall is racially diverse. The bus is racially diverse. Proximity is not the thing.

Diversity as a program to add is not diversity as a theological reality. The program approach treats the absence of racial difference in the congregation as a marketing gap — a demographic the church is missing, a segment it should be reaching. The solution, on this logic, is to make the congregation more appealing to people from those demographics: hire someone who looks like them, play music they might prefer, give the programs names that signal welcome. This is not theology. It is market segmentation.

The theological claim is something else. Paul writes in Galatians 3:28 that in Christ "there is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is neither male nor female." He is not saying these differences have been erased — the slave is still enslaved, the Greek still speaks Greek. He is saying that the community gathered around Christ is one in which the social hierarchies that govern the surrounding world do not determine belonging, status, or voice. The Gentile is not welcomed into a Judaized community. The Jew is not welcomed into a Hellenized community. Both are welcomed into a community that is being reconstituted around a new center that is not culture or ethnicity or class. This is the thing Revelation 7:9 is describing: every nation, tribe, people, and language — not assimilated into one, but gathered as many.

## The Power Question the Program Avoids

Here is what most diversity initiatives do not address: who holds the power to decide what the community is.

A predominantly white church that adds programs of cultural inclusion while maintaining white leadership, white preaching, white theological frameworks, and white assumptions about what the worship service should accomplish has not diversified. It has annexed. The people of color who attend exist within a structure that was built by others, reflects the preferences of others, and will continue to be governed by others. Their presence is welcomed. Their authority is not extended.

This is not hospitality. It is something more like the Roman hospitality of colonized territories: you are welcome here, provided you receive what we offer, in the form in which we offer it, on the schedule we have set.

The sociologist Eduardo Bonilla-Silva has documented what he calls color-blind racism — the way that racial inequality persists and reproduces itself through ostensibly race-neutral structures and preferences, rather than through explicit prejudice [cite — confirm]. The church that says it is color-blind, that it welcomes everyone, that it does not see race — that church has not actually removed race from its operations. It has removed race from its vocabulary while leaving the power structures built by race entirely intact.

James K.A. Smith would ask: what do your practices love? What does the shape of your worship, your governance, your decision-making, your pastoral appointments actually express about what you value? [cite — confirm] The practices are the theology. The diversity that exists in the church's rhetoric but not in its structures tells us what the church actually believes about whose experience is normative, whose leadership is trusted, whose way of being in community is the real center.

## What the New Testament Church Actually Looked Like

The Jerusalem Council of Acts 15 [cite — confirm] is the first major institutional crisis in Christian history, and it is a crisis about exactly this question: will the Gentile church be required to conform to Jewish patterns in order to belong fully to the people of God? The question had practical urgency — Gentile converts were receiving the Holy Spirit, which was the unambiguous sign of God's acceptance, without being circumcised. The theological conservatives argued that the sign of the covenant was still required. Paul and Barnabas argued that God had already spoken.

The resolution — that Gentiles were not required to become Jews — was not merely an administrative decision about ritual requirements. It was a declaration that the community of Christ was not a monoculture that admitted outsiders on the condition that they assimilate to the majority's forms. It was a community whose center was Christ, not culture, and into which genuinely different human experiences were brought as they were, not after being converted to the majority's idiom.

The Antioch incident that follows in Galatians 2 shows how difficult this was to maintain in practice. Peter ate with Gentiles until the delegation from Jerusalem arrived, at which point he withdrew, "fearing the circumcision party" (Galatians 2:12). Paul opposed him "to his face" because the behavior was a contradiction of the gospel — not just an awkward social error, but a practical denial of what the cross had accomplished. The unity of the table was not optional. It was the sign of the thing the church claimed to be.

## The Specific Failure in American Church History

The American church's record on race is not ambiguous. The Southern Baptist Convention was formed in 1845 specifically to defend the right of slaveholders to serve as missionaries [cite — confirm]. The majority of white churches in the South defended segregation from their pulpits within living memory of many Americans still sitting in those pews. The evangelical world that produced the modern racial reconciliation movement also produced a record of silence, accommodation, and active theological support for a racial order that the prophets would have called by its name without hesitation.

This history is not extrinsic to the question of contemporary church diversity. It is the ground the contemporary church stands on. The structural whiteness of most American evangelical institutions — the theological seminaries, the mission agencies, the denominational bureaucracies, the bestseller lists, the conference circuits — did not emerge from nowhere. It was built by people making decisions, over generations, about whose leadership was trusted, whose voice was authoritative, whose experience was normative. The fact that those decisions are no longer explicitly made on racial grounds does not mean the structures they produced have been dismantled.

When a predominantly white church begins a diversity initiative without examining the history of how it became predominantly white, it is trying to solve the consequence without addressing the cause. The family that was explicitly excluded from the neighborhood in the 1950s by restrictive covenants, and is now welcomed through a diversity program in 2026, is receiving something different from the person who was never excluded. The welcome is real. The history is also real. Acknowledging only the welcome while erasing the history is not reconciliation. It is amnesia with good intentions.

## What Genuine Change Requires

It requires power-sharing that is uncomfortable. Not just diversity in the congregation but diversity in governance — in who chairs the elders, who preaches the sermons, who shapes the budget, who decides what the community is allowed to ask. It requires the willingness of people who hold power to give some of it away, and to make decisions that they might not have made, because people who see differently are at the table with genuine authority.

It requires listening that precedes programming. The question is not "how do we make our church more appealing to people of color?" The question is "why have we been a church that has not reflected the community around us, and what do we need to repent of before we can genuinely welcome people into something different than what has existed?" Repentance precedes reconciliation. It is not a preliminary formality. It is the theological substance of the thing.

It requires patience with a process that does not produce outcomes on the timeline of a church strategic plan. The distrust that many communities of color carry toward predominantly white institutions was not created in a strategic planning cycle. It will not be dissolved by one. The credibility of the welcome has to be demonstrated over time, in decisions that have costs, not only in programs that have budgets.

## The Theological Stakes

The failure of the American church to become what Revelation 7 describes is not primarily a sociological problem. It is a theological one. The church that gathers only within the lines of racial and cultural similarity has decided, in practice, that its primary identity is cultural rather than Christological. It has made a choice about what is more determinative — the confession that Jesus is Lord, which crosses every human boundary, or the comfort of homogeneity, which reinforces them.

The watching world notices this. The church that preaches reconciliation and practices segregation — even polite, program-mediated segregation — has undermined its most basic claim at the level of its primary evidence.

What we owe the people the programs have not reached is not another program. It is a church willing to become something different from what it has been — willing to share the authority, hold the history, and let the encounter with genuine difference change not just who is in the room but who the room belongs to.

That is a different order of change than adding a worship leader. Most churches have not been willing to make it.

The question is whether the gospel is the reason we gather or merely the banner over the gathering we would have held anyway.
