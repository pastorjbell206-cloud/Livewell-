The book of Deuteronomy commands that the law be read aloud to the entire assembly — men, women, children, the foreigners living in the town — at the Feast of Booths every seven years (Deuteronomy 31:10-13). Not summarized for the educated. Not read to the leadership who would then explain it to the rest. Read aloud to everyone. The reason given is explicit: *so they can listen and learn to fear the Lord your God and follow carefully all the words of this law.* The word of God was not to be the property of specialists. Access to the word was the condition for faithful life, and access required comprehension, and comprehension required a community committed to ensuring that every member of the community could receive it.

This is not a minor textual footnote. It is the theological architecture beneath a claim the church has not always honored: that access to the written word — to the capacity to read, understand, and participate in the traditions of meaning that constitute a community's life — is not a privilege for the capable but a condition for full human dignity. Literacy is a justice issue. The church that has not understood this has missed something foundational in its own Scripture.

The history of this question is a history of power.

In medieval Europe, the restriction of Scripture to Latin — a language the vast majority of the population could not read — was not accidental. It was structural. The church that controlled the text controlled the interpretation of the text, and the interpretation of the text controlled the community's understanding of God, authority, sin, and salvation. John Wycliffe's insistence in the late fourteenth century on translating Scripture into English was not primarily a linguistic project. It was a justice project — an argument that the word of God belonged to the plowman as much as to the priest, that the capacity to read and interpret it was not a clerical monopoly but a human right [cite — confirm]. Wycliffe was condemned for this. The institutional church understood exactly what was at stake.

William Tyndale understood it too. His English translation of the New Testament, completed in 1526, was burned by church authorities and cost him his life — he was strangled and burned at the stake in 1536. His reported last words, *Lord, open the King of England's eyes*, are the words of a man who understood that access to the written word was bound up with access to power, and that the powerful did not give up that control without cost [cite — confirm]. The Reformation's democratizing impulse — the priesthood of all believers, the accessibility of Scripture — was premised on literacy in ways that the Reformers understood and the institutions they challenged feared.

In the American South before the Civil War, teaching enslaved people to read was illegal in most slaveholding states [cite — confirm]. This is the clearest possible evidence that literacy is power. The enslaved person who could read could read Frederick Douglass's *Narrative*. Could read the Bible without a white minister's interpretation of it. Could read the forged passes that allowed movement. Could read the advertisements for escaped slaves that described what they were running from. The prohibition was not about pedagogy. It was about control, and the people who enforced it understood exactly what they were controlling.

Nat Turner could read. Frederick Douglass, who described in his autobiography the moment he understood that literacy was the path from slavery to freedom, later said: *Once you learn to read, you will be forever free* [cite — confirm]. The connection between literacy and liberation was not metaphorical. It was material.

Today, in the United States, approximately 54 percent of adults read below a sixth-grade level [cite — confirm]. This figure is not distributed evenly. It is concentrated in communities of poverty, in communities of color, among populations that have been systematically underfunded by the school systems that property-tax-based funding models favor. The child in the underfunded district does not get worse education because the community values education less. She gets worse education because the system was designed — not by accident but by a series of policy choices stretching back generations — to concentrate educational resources where wealth already exists.

This is systemic sin in the technical sense: an arrangement of institutions and policies that produces unjust outcomes regardless of the individual intentions of the people operating within it. Most of the teachers in underfunded schools are working as hard as or harder than their counterparts in well-funded schools. Most of the parents in those communities want their children to learn to read. The system produces illiteracy not because anyone inside it is malicious but because the system was built to produce this result and has not been fundamentally changed.

The church's response to this has been mostly charitable and occasionally substantive, but rarely prophetic. There are after-school tutoring programs, literacy ministries, reading circles attached to congregations across the country. They are doing real good and the people running them are often among the most faithful people I know. But they are addressing the symptom without addressing the system that produces it. The child who learns to read in a church basement program in a neighborhood with an underfunded school will return the next morning to that school. The symptom has been addressed. The cause is still operating.

Prophetic witness requires the willingness to name the cause.

The cause is a public school funding model that ties educational quality to neighborhood wealth, that produces — by design — unequal educational outcomes for children who had no choice about where they were born. The cause is a political economy that has consistently underinvested in the communities with the least political power and overinvested in the communities with the most. The cause is a historical inheritance of segregation — both residential, produced by redlining and discriminatory housing policy, and educational, produced by the school funding model that maps onto those residential patterns — that continues to shape outcomes decades after the legal instruments of segregation were dismantled.

James 2:14-17 asks the question directly: *What good is it, my brothers and sisters, if someone claims to have faith but has no deeds? Can such faith save them? Suppose a brother or a sister is without clothes and daily food. If one of you says to them, 'Go in peace; keep warm and well fed,' but does nothing about their physical needs, what good is it?* The church that runs reading programs while supporting politicians and policies that perpetuate the inequality producing illiteracy has said *go in peace* without addressing the need. It has faith without deeds, in precisely the sense James condemns.

This is not an argument that every pastor should become an education policy advocate in the specific legislative sense. It is an argument that the church that takes seriously the Deuteronomy vision — that the word should be accessible to every member of the community, that full human participation in the life of God's people requires the capacity to receive it — has a theological stake in the conditions that make literacy possible or impossible.

The imago Dei claim carries this. If every person bears the image of God, then the capacity for every person to participate fully in the written traditions of human civilization — to read contracts, to access medical information, to engage with Scripture directly rather than through filtered summary, to participate in democratic processes that require reading to engage — is not a nice-to-have. It is a condition for the full expression of the dignity that the image of God confers.

The church that has historically cared about Scripture should care, with equivalent intensity, about the conditions that make Scripture accessible. Not only in translation — though the translation project remains vital in many parts of the world. But in the literacy that makes translation useful. The word can be in the language of the people and still be inaccessible if the people cannot read it.

In the nineteenth century, the Sunday school movement in England and America — begun not primarily as a religious education program but as a literacy program for children of the industrial poor who worked six days a week and had Sunday free — was one of the church's most significant justice contributions [cite — confirm]. Robert Raikes, who launched it in Gloucester in 1780, was responding to what he observed in the streets: children without education, without access to the traditions of meaning that might give their lives structure and dignity, moving toward a predictable adulthood of poverty and institutional neglect. The Sunday school taught them to read. And it taught them to read using the Bible, which was the text those communities had. The program was both charity and justice — addressing the immediate need of the individual child while building the infrastructure for a community in which literacy would be possible.

That tradition is worth recovering — not in the form of the Sunday school, which has evolved into something else, but in the spirit of a church that understands literacy as a theological imperative rather than a social service, that sees the relationship between the word of God and the capacity to read it as something more than incidental, that is willing to address not just the immediate gap but the systems that produce it.

The word was given to be read. The community was commanded to ensure that everyone could receive it. What that costs and who pays it is not a minor question. It is a question about what the church actually believes is at stake in the person sitting across from it who cannot read.
