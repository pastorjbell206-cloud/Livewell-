The conversation started at a pastors' lunch — three pastors from three different small churches in the same mid-sized city, meeting monthly as part of a loose network of local pastors who had been trying, with mixed success, to build genuine relationships across their denominational differences. The conversation that started the whole thing was not particularly visionary. It was actually a complaint.

One of the pastors — I will call him Marcus — was describing a neighborhood about four miles from his church building: a rapidly growing area of new apartment developments, young families moving in, almost no established churches in the immediate vicinity. "Every time I drive through there, I feel the pull," he said. "We should have a church there. But we're already maxed out. I can't do it."

The second pastor said, "We've been thinking the same thing about the same neighborhood." The third said nothing for a moment, and then: "What if we did it together?"

## Why Small Churches Have Been Told They Cannot Plant

The dominant model of church planting in American evangelical culture for the last several decades has been the large-church send model: a congregation of significant size and resources identifies and resources a church planter, sends a launch team, and provides financial support for the first three to five years of the new congregation's existence. This model has produced many successful plants. It has also created, implicitly, a belief that church planting is something large churches do and small churches watch.

This belief is not supported by the New Testament. The church in Antioch, which sent out Paul and Barnabas on their first missionary journey (Acts 13:1-3), was not the largest or most resourced congregation in the early church. It was a young, multiethnic congregation in a Gentile city, formed only a few years earlier, and it acted on the Spirit's prompting without waiting to achieve a scale that would make the mission comfortable.

The model that most constrained small churches was also the model that concentrated the energy and resources of the church's planting efforts in communities that could support large-church launches — which tended to be growing suburban contexts rather than the urban neighborhoods, rural communities, and lower-income areas where the church's presence was most needed and where the large-church send model was least financially viable.

The small-church collaborative model does not replace the large-church send model. It fills the geography that the large-church send model cannot reach.

## Working Through the Obvious Obstacles

The obstacles were real and the three pastors named them honestly. Different theological traditions — one Baptist, one non-denominational, one Anglican-influenced. Different views on church governance, worship style, and several secondary theological matters. Different financial situations, with none of the three having significant reserves. And the practical challenge of how to lead a church plant that was genuinely shared rather than dominated by whoever contributed the most.

These are not trivial obstacles. The history of ecumenical cooperation in American Christianity is littered with initiatives that began with genuine enthusiasm and ended in either compromise that satisfied no one or conflict that damaged the relationships it had tried to build. The three pastors in this story were not naive about the risks.

They spent three months meeting regularly before committing to anything. During those months, they mapped their areas of genuine theological agreement — which turned out to be more extensive than any of them had initially assumed — and their areas of difference. They agreed on the essentials of the faith as the foundation. They agreed to hold the secondary matters with openness and ongoing conversation rather than requiring prior agreement. They agreed that the primary mission of the plant — reaching people who currently have no church connection — would function as the organizing principle that subordinated their differences without erasing them.

The willingness to sit with genuine theological difference rather than resolving it artificially or papering over it was the relational achievement that made everything else possible. They did not agree on everything. They agreed on enough, and they trusted each other enough to work together despite the rest.

## How the Structure Worked

Each of the three churches committed a portion of its annual budget — not a large portion, but a meaningful one — to a shared plant fund. Each church identified one or two families who felt genuine call to be part of a church planting team and were willing to relocate to the target neighborhood. The three pastors together identified and called a lead planter — a young pastor who had expressed interest in church planting and who was theologically comfortable enough with all three traditions to be genuinely supported by each.

The governance was explicitly collaborative: the lead planter was accountable to a joint oversight team that included representatives from each of the three churches. This created real accountability without a single church having controlling authority — and it required the kind of trust that only the prior months of relationship-building made possible.

The financial arrangement addressed the power dynamics directly. Each church's contribution was proportional to its size, not equal, which prevented the largest of the three from having disproportionate influence through the weight of its contribution. The joint oversight team made decisions by consensus rather than majority vote, which required the three churches to work toward genuine agreement rather than allowing any two to outvote the third.

This was slower. There were conversations that took three meetings to resolve because the consensus requirement meant that no one could be simply outvoted. The slowness was, in retrospect, the point — it built the relational trust that allowed the partnership to endure.

## The Theological Case for Collaboration

The model deserves its theological scaffolding, not just its organizational description.

John 17 records Jesus praying for the unity of his disciples — not a procedural unity, not a doctrinal uniformity, but a unity that would be visible to the watching world and would function as the primary evidence of the gospel's truth. "May they be brought to complete unity to let the world know that you sent me" (John 17:23). The visibility of unity is, in Jesus' own framing, an evangelistic act. The church that is divided — not on essential doctrine but on tradition and preference and institutional territory — is withholding from its community a form of gospel witness that it could be providing.

The three churches in this story were not merely being organizationally efficient when they planted together. They were being evangelistically obedient. The church in the new neighborhood that emerged from three different traditions working together was itself a form of witness that no single-tradition plant could have provided.

Lesslie Newbigin argued in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* [cite — confirm] that the primary apologetic for the gospel in a skeptical culture is the quality of the community that the gospel produces — specifically, its capacity to hold together people who would otherwise be separated by the forces that divide the world. The co-planted church was a proof of concept for exactly this claim.

## What Happened

The church launched eighteen months after that original lunch conversation. In its first two years, it baptized thirty-one people, the majority of whom had no prior church background. All three of the sending churches reported that the act of sending had energized their own congregations — that the experience of being part of a mission larger than their individual church had produced new generosity, new engagement, and a renewed sense of what the church was actually for.

This is a documented phenomenon in church planting research, though it runs counter to the intuition that sending will deplete a congregation. Churches that send tend to grow, not because sending guarantees numerical growth, but because the experience of genuine mission redirects the congregation's energy from self-maintenance to outward purpose, and that redirection tends to produce both spiritual vitality and, over time, numerical health [cite — confirm].

The three sending churches did not lose the families they sent to the plant. They gained a purpose that their internal programs had not been generating.

## The Pastoral Ending

The three pastors still meet for lunch — still meeting monthly, still building the relationship that started this whole story. Marcus said something to me that stayed: "The plant is great. But honestly, the best thing that came out of it is that we are genuinely friends now. I don't know what I would do without these two."

The organizational achievement — a new congregation, thirty-one baptisms, a neighborhood now served by a church community it previously lacked — is real and significant. But the friendship is not incidental to the story. It is the condition that made everything else possible, and it is the fruit of the story that the organizational metrics do not capture.

The three small churches that could not plant alone planted together, not because they had solved all their differences, but because they had built enough trust across them to act on a shared conviction.

Your church probably cannot do this alone. There is likely another pastor within driving distance who is looking at the same neighborhood you are looking at, feeling the same pull, and also concluding that he cannot do it alone.

Make the call. Go to the lunch. Let the complaint become the question: what if we did this together?

You cannot do what they did. You can start where they started.
