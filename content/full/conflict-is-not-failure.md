The New Testament church had conflict before it had a second generation.

In Acts 6, the Hellenistic widows were being overlooked in the daily distribution of food. There was a complaint, public and serious, about inequity in the earliest Christian community. The apostles did not pretend it wasn't happening. They did not reframe it as a misunderstanding or announce a prayer initiative and hope the problem resolved itself. They gathered the disciples, named the problem directly, and restructured the community's leadership to address it (Acts 6:1-6). The seven deacons were appointed not as a gesture of pastoral care but as a structural response to a genuine failure.

The conflict did not threaten the church. The willingness to address it made the church stronger.

That is the pattern, and most pastoral training has missed it.

I spent years in pastoral ministry treating conflict as a diagnostic of inadequate leadership. If the congregation was in conflict, something had gone wrong on my watch, and my job was to smooth it over as quickly and quietly as possible before it spread. I have sat in more meetings than I want to count where the entire pastoral agenda was managing the temperature rather than addressing the underlying reality. The conflict was treated as the disease, when in almost every case it was the symptom — and the disease was something we were not willing to name.

Avoidance is not peace. It is pressure building behind a wall.

## What Conflict Is Actually Telling You

What I have learned — slowly, and not without cost — is that conflict in a congregation is almost always information. It tells you what the community actually values. It tells you where the unspoken agreements have been violated. It tells you which relationships have been maintained at the cost of honesty and which have accumulated enough trust to tell the truth.

A congregation that never surfaces conflict has not achieved harmony. It has achieved a kind of managed suppression in which the real conversations happen in parking lots and living rooms rather than in the rooms where they might actually be resolved. The absence of visible conflict in a congregation is not a sign of health. It is a sign that people have decided the cost of honesty is too high — and that conclusion, once established, affects everything else. The sermon that cannot be questioned. The decision that cannot be challenged. The elder who cannot be held accountable. The culture of managed surfaces under which the real conditions of the community's life remain unaddressed.

Patrick Lencioni's work on organizational dysfunction places the absence of conflict — or more precisely, the avoidance of productive conflict — near the center of what kills teams and communities [cite — confirm]. The team that has learned not to disagree has not learned to trust each other. It has learned to protect itself from the discomfort of honest exchange. The result is not cooperation. It is a kind of paralysis in which everyone privately knows what the real problems are and no one says them.

Congregations are not immune to this. They are perhaps more susceptible to it, because the theological language of peace and unity can be deployed to suppress legitimate disagreement in ways that secular organizations cannot as easily manage. "Let's not cause division in the body of Christ" is sometimes a genuine theological concern. It is also sometimes a way of protecting the powerful from the feedback of the powerless.

## The Biblical Framework for Conflict

The pastor's job in conflict is not to be a peacemaker in the sense of eliminating disagreement. The biblical word for peacemaker in Matthew 5:9 comes from the Greek *eirene*, which carries the same weight as the Hebrew *shalom* — not the absence of tension but the presence of rightness, of whole relationships, of communities functioning in the truth about themselves. That kind of peace is not achieved by avoiding conflict. It is achieved by addressing it faithfully.

Matthew 18 is the conflict resolution protocol that Jesus gave the church, and it is more practical than most people realize. "If your brother sins against you, go and tell him his fault, between you and him alone" (Matthew 18:15). The first move is direct, private, and relational. It does not route through the board or the prayer chain or the rumor network. It goes directly to the person. Jesus gives these instructions not because he expects them to always work but because the alternative — unaddressed grievance spreading through a community — is more destructive than the discomfort of the direct conversation.

The three-stage process Jesus describes (verse 15-17) is a process of escalating accountability, not a process of escalating punishment. Each stage is designed to give the person an opportunity to respond before the circle of involvement widens. The goal throughout is restoration: "If he listens to you, you have gained your brother" (verse 15). The win is not being right. The win is the relationship restored to health.

Paul's letters contain more conflict than any other portion of the New Testament. The Corinthian correspondence is, among other things, a master class in addressing a congregation's problems directly. He names factions by name (1 Corinthians 1:12). He identifies specific sexual immorality and says the congregation should have addressed it already (1 Corinthians 5). He tells two women who are in conflict — Euodia and Syntyche — to reconcile, by name, in a letter that will be read publicly to the whole congregation (Philippians 4:2). Paul's pastoral style is not the avoidance of difficult conversations. It is the direct, named, publicly accountable address of them.

## What the Pastor Models

This is where the pastoral failure of avoidance does its deepest damage. The congregation learns how to handle conflict by watching how the pastor handles it. If the pastor smooths, they learn to smooth. If the pastor names things, they learn that naming things is something this community does. If the pastor delays, routes around, and lets problems fester until they explode, they will do the same — and they will have learned it from the person whose job was to model something better.

I have had conversations I delayed for months because I did not want them to go badly. In every case, the delay made them worse. The thing I was avoiding did not diminish while I avoided it. It grew, in the dark, into something that required twice the courage and twice the relational damage to address.

The elder who needed to hear that his leadership style was hurting the staff: the longer I waited, the more people the style hurt and the harder the conversation became. The board member whose financial decisions were creating legal exposure for the church: the longer I waited, the more entrenched the behavior and the more defensive the response. The staff person whose personal crisis was affecting the ministry: the longer I waited, the further the crisis progressed and the less I could do about it.

Direct address, done with care and clarity, is almost always less damaging than delayed address. The damage is contained. The relationship has the chance to respond before it has hardened. The community learns that this is a place where difficult things can be said and the speaker is not destroyed for saying them.

## The Counter-Position

The honest challenge to this is the pastor who has watched direct address done badly — who has been in congregations where conflict was addressed not with care but with dominance, where naming things meant the powerful naming the powerless and calling it accountability, where the Matthew 18 process was weaponized by the person with more institutional standing.

This is a real problem. Direct address without genuine humility and genuine commitment to restoration is not the Matthew 18 process. It is confrontation using Matthew 18 language. The difference is the heart of the person initiating the conversation — whether they are going to "gain a brother" or to win a conflict they have already decided is theirs to win.

The answer to badly done conflict address is not to return to avoidance. It is to develop the skill and the humility to address conflict well. That skill is learnable. It includes learning how to enter a difficult conversation without having already decided its conclusion, how to listen before responding, how to hold firm conviction and genuine openness simultaneously, how to name what is true without making the person the enemy.

## What Remains

A congregation without visible conflict is not a healthy congregation. It is a congregation where people have learned not to say what they actually think. That is a far more serious problem than a congregation with visible conflict, because the silent congregation is operating on a lie about itself — a managed pretense of agreement that is itself a form of institutional dishonesty.

The church that can address conflict directly — that can say hard things and stay in the room, that can be corrected and correct others without the relationship being destroyed, that can disagree on significant matters without fracturing into separate camps — is demonstrating something about the gospel. It is demonstrating that the reconciliation the gospel claims is actually possible, that forgiveness is not just preached but practiced, that the community formed by the cross is capable of the hard relational work that the cross made possible.

Conflict is not a sign that you have failed. It is a sign that real people with real convictions are in the same room together. The question is not whether it will appear. The question is whether you have the skill and the courage to address it in a way that makes the community more honest rather than more afraid.

That skill is learnable. The courage is a choice.

Make the choice before the wall gives way.
