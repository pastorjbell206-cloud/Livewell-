Read Acts 9 carefully. Paul has just been converted on the Damascus road. He is on fire, preaching in the synagogues, confounding the people who thought he was coming to arrest Christians. He arrives in Jerusalem and the church there wants nothing to do with him. They are afraid of him. The word on the street is that Saul of Tarsus destroys communities from the inside, and they are not persuaded that a vision on a road changes a man.

Then Barnabas does one specific thing. He takes Paul and brings him to the apostles. He vouches for him. He tells the story of Damascus in a room where no one wanted to believe it (Acts 9:27).

That is it. That is the whole act. Barnabas believed Paul when the church did not, and he said so out loud, in a room where the saying cost him something. Without that moment, there is no Galatian mission, no letter to the Romans, no Athens sermon. The theological architecture that has shaped Christian thought for two thousand years begins with one man willing to say: I know him. He is who he says he is.

Every pastor I know who has lasted in ministry has a Barnabas in the story.

## What Barnabas Actually Did

Before we can talk about finding this person, we need to be clear about what Barnabas was doing. He was not a cheerleader. He was not someone who reflexively supported everyone in the room because conflict made him uncomfortable. He was someone who had paid close enough attention to Paul to have a formed opinion about him — who had observed something real and was willing to stake his own credibility on what he saw.

His name means "son of encouragement" (Acts 4:36), and the biographical note is introduced deliberately. This is not just his given name. It is a vocational description. The text wants us to understand that there are people whose primary contribution to the body of Christ is not their preaching or their administration or their theological innovation. It is the specific gift of seeing what others cannot yet see in a person and saying so at the moment when it matters most.

The encouragement Barnabas offered Paul was not generic affirmation. It was specific, costly, and public. He did not tell Paul privately that he believed in him and then stay silent when the apostles in Jerusalem raised their concerns. He took Paul into the room. The encouragement had weight precisely because it was witnessed and because it required Barnabas to be wrong with Paul if Paul turned out to be wrong.

That combination — belief specific enough to stake your reputation on it, offered in the room where the stakes are real — is what distinguishes a Barnabas from a flatterer. The flatterer agrees with you privately and says nothing when the room turns against you. Barnabas agrees with you and says so in the room.

## The Middle of Ministry

The pastors I know who have quit, who burned out quietly or collapsed publicly, almost universally share one thing: they were not accompanied. They may have had mentors in the early years. They may have had friends from seminary. But somewhere in the long middle of ministry — the decade after the early successes, when the novelty is gone and the weight of the work is fully apparent and the original enthusiasm has been worn down by the complexity of actual people in actual conflict — they lost the person who could hold both what they were capable of and what they were actually doing.

I did not understand this for a long time. I thought that needing someone's belief in you was a kind of weakness — that a mature pastor should have sufficient theological conviction and internal stability to move without an external witness. I was wrong, and the wrongness cost me years I would like to have back. The independent man in ministry is not strong. He is brittle. He breaks in ways that accompanied men do not, because the breaks happen in the dark where no one sees them coming.

The isolation of pastoral ministry is structural, not incidental. You know things about the congregation that you cannot share. You carry confidences that create distance. You are in the position of caring for people whose care of you is complicated by the pastoral relationship. The asymmetry is real. The isolation it produces is not a character flaw. It is the predictable result of a role that requires you to give more than you receive in almost every relationship you have.

The Barnabas relationship is the corrective to this. It is the one relationship in which the asymmetry is not absolute — where you can receive as well as give, where you can be known rather than only knowing, where you can be believed in rather than only believing in others.

## The Shape of the Relationship

Barnabas and Paul's relationship was not without conflict. Acts 15:36–41 records a disagreement sharp enough to end their partnership — a dispute over whether to take John Mark on a second missionary journey, John Mark having turned back from the first. Barnabas wanted to give him another chance. Paul did not. The text says the disagreement was so sharp that they parted company.

This is essential to understand. The Barnabas you need is not a yes-man. A yes-man agrees with everything and protects the relationship by never raising the hard thing. A Barnabas believes in you while being capable of genuine disagreement — which means the belief is not conditional on agreement. He can see something in you that is worth believing in and simultaneously think you are wrong about John Mark. Those two things can coexist in the same relationship.

That kind of relationship is rarer than we admit. Most of what passes for pastoral accountability is either mutual encouragement that never rises to real challenge, or formal accountability structures that create compliance without intimacy. The Barnabas relationship requires both intimacy and honesty, and developing both simultaneously is a slow project.

## How It Gets Built

Finding this person is not a passive process. Barnabas did not appear because Paul was waiting. Barnabas was already in Jerusalem, already known to the apostles, already someone who had demonstrated the character to make his vouching mean something. The relationships that produce this kind of accompaniment are built before they are needed.

You do not find your Barnabas in a crisis. You find him in the ordinary Tuesday lunches and the phone calls that are about nothing in particular and the Sunday nights where you are exhausted and you call someone just to say that. The relationship that can hold you in the hard year is built in the easy years.

The practical implication is that this is not something to defer. Many pastors, pressed by the demands of ministry, treat personal relationships as the thing to get to when there is margin — which means they never get to it. The margin never appears. The relationship never forms. And when the crisis arrives, there is no one present with enough history to be useful.

I have had men in my life who did this for me. They believed in my calling before my calling had produced anything worth believing in. They sat across from me in the dark seasons and refused to let me conclude that the dark was permanent. They were not naive about my failures. They knew them — sometimes in more detail than I was comfortable with. They held the belief and the knowledge at the same time, and that combination — belief that survives knowledge — is precisely what makes it worth having.

Mere affirmation does not survive knowledge. The person who only knows your public self can only believe in your public self. The person who knows the full thing and believes in it anyway is carrying something different. That is costly for them to carry. It is irreplaceable for you.

## The Discipline of Being a Barnabas

The pastoral tradition tends to focus on receiving mentorship — finding the older, wiser figure who will invest in you. But Barnabas was not Paul's mentor in the conventional sense. He was a peer who believed in Paul's potential before Paul had demonstrated it. And one of the most important shifts in any pastor's formation is the shift from only looking for a Barnabas to also becoming one.

There is a young pastor in your network right now who is three years into ministry and making every mistake that three years of ministry produces. He may be gifted in ways he cannot yet see. He needs someone to see it first. The discipline of looking — of paying close enough attention to young leaders to form a real opinion about their potential, and then being willing to say it in the rooms where it matters — is not a supplement to your ministry. It is a substantial part of what your experience is for.

And there is something else: the discipline of offering it tends to make you more capable of receiving it. The pastor who has learned to see what is worth believing in young leaders has usually, in the process, learned something about what it means to be believed in. The giving and the receiving are not separate practices. They are the same posture, turned in different directions.

Find that person. Or better: be that person for someone younger than you, and trust that the discipline of offering it will make you capable of receiving it.

The ministry that lasts is always built on something that looks, from the outside, like ordinary friendship.

It is not ordinary. Nothing about it is ordinary.
