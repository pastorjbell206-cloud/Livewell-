Congregations have felt needs — the things they know they want help with. Parenting, marriage, finances, purpose, anxiety. These are real needs, and preaching that consistently ignores them is preaching that has become disconnected from the actual lives of the people in the room. A pastor who is not attentive to the burdens the congregation carries is not serving the congregation, however theologically sophisticated the sermons may be.

But preaching that consistently begins and ends with felt needs produces a congregation that knows how to apply biblical principles to the problems on the surface of their lives while having little formation at depth. The deeper need — for genuine encounter with a holy God, for the transformation of desire itself, for the capacity to suffer faithfully and love without return — is rarely felt acutely until it is urgently needed. And by then, a diet of felt-need preaching has not prepared the congregation for it.

This is the first of five sermon habits that slowly shrink a congregation's faith. It is the most subtle, because it starts with something true — people have real needs — and then goes wrong not by lying but by stopping too soon.

---

The felt need is not the deepest need. That distinction is the crux.

C.S. Lewis described the human condition as one in which we are too easily pleased — in which we settle for the proximate desire when the deeper longing is what actually drives us. [cite — confirm Lewis, Weight of Glory or Mere Christianity, relevant passage.] The person who comes to church anxious about their marriage has a real problem. But the problem beneath the problem — the formation of character that makes sustained love possible, the theological reordering of expectations, the confrontation with the idol of the marriage as the source of one's identity — is the deeper problem, and it is the one the gospel is actually equipped to address.

The sermon that addresses the anxiety about the marriage without engaging the deeper architecture is helpful as far as it goes. It offers real tools and real encouragement. What it does not do is address the thing underneath the anxiety — the question of what the person is fundamentally oriented toward, what they are trusting in, what would happen to their sense of self if the marriage did not recover. Those are the questions the gospel addresses. Felt-need preaching often cannot reach them because it has defined the problem at the level of the symptom.

---

The pastoral scene that makes this concrete.

A couple attends a church with a strong reputation for practical, applicable preaching. They have been there for four years. They have heard dozens of sermons on marriage — communication principles, conflict resolution strategies, the importance of date nights, the theology of covenant commitment. The content has been sound and practically useful.

The marriage is quietly failing anyway. Not from lack of trying the principles. From something deeper: a fundamental misalignment of desire, an idolatry of marriage as the source of identity and validation that the marriage itself cannot sustain, a patterns of relating that has theological roots neither of them has been given the vocabulary to address.

The sermons gave them tools for the surface. They were not formed in the deeper structure. They were not confronted with the specific question the gospel raises — not *what techniques will improve your marriage* but *who is the Lord of this marriage, and what does it mean for two people to be known fully and still loved, not by each other but by God, which is the only foundation on which a marriage can bear the weight you are asking it to bear*.

They leave the church when the marriage ends. Not angry at the church — grateful, in fact, for the years of community. But they leave without the resources for what comes next, because those resources were not built in a felt-need preaching diet.

---

The New Testament model does not start with felt needs and stay there.

Jesus begins with the felt need constantly — hunger, blindness, grief, desperation. He heals the man at the pool of Bethesda who had been paralyzed for thirty-eight years (John 5:1-9). He feeds the five thousand who are hungry (John 6:1-14). He is not indifferent to the presenting problem. The presenting problem is where the encounter begins.

But he does not leave the person at the presenting problem. After healing the man at Bethesda, he finds him again and says: "See, you are well! Sin no more, that nothing worse may happen to you" (John 5:14). After feeding the five thousand, he gives the bread-of-life discourse — one of the most demanding theological addresses in the Gospels, which many of the crowd cannot receive and which thins the following substantially (John 6:60-66). The felt need was the entrance. The deeper demand was the destination. Jesus used one to get to the other, and he was not deterred when many preferred the entrance to the destination.

Paul follows the same pattern. His letter to the Philippians addresses real anxiety — the congregation is worried about him, worried about circumstances, worried about internal conflict. His response acknowledges the anxiety and then moves immediately to the theological frame that addresses it: "Do not be anxious about anything, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God" (Philippians 4:6). The felt need is named. But the address moves quickly to the deeper structure — the peace that passes understanding, which is not a technique but a theological reality grounded in the character of God and accessible only through relationship with him.

Felt-need preaching that does not make that move — that names the anxiety and offers the steps without pointing through the steps to the God whose character is the ground of peace — has done a smaller work than the text was doing.

---

The formation damage accumulates slowly and invisibly.

A congregation formed primarily on felt-need preaching develops certain characteristics over time. They are consumers of spiritual content — good at receiving, evaluating, and applying what is offered, less formed in the interior capacities that generate faith from the inside rather than receiving it from the outside. They know what Christianity teaches but are not deeply shaped in who Christians are. They can describe the principles of a godly marriage or a generous financial life, but the principles do not go down into the bones in ways that hold under pressure.

When the crisis comes — the diagnosis, the death, the collapse of the thing they had built, the suffering that does not yield to any principle — they find their faith thinner than they expected. Not absent. Real. But not built for this. The sermons formed them for manageable challenges. The crisis is not manageable.

This is not the fault of the person in the pew. The formation they received was the formation the preaching provided. The preaching formed them for the surface of life. Life, at its most determinative, happens at depth — and depth formation requires preaching that goes there regularly, not only in the crisis sermon but in the ordinary course of what a congregation hears.

---

The counter-position.

Some will argue that reaching unchurched people requires meeting them where they are — that the church which does not speak to felt needs will not get the opportunity to speak to the deeper needs, because the unchurched person will not return after a sermon that does not seem to address their actual life. The felt-need sermon is the front door; the deeper formation happens as the person becomes more established in the community.

There is something to this. A sermon that ignores all contemporary life in favor of pure theological abstraction is not prophetic — it is disconnected, and disconnection is its own kind of failure. The application to life is not optional; it is where theology becomes practice, which is where it becomes formative.

But the argument breaks down when it becomes the permanent structure rather than the entrance strategy. A person who has attended for two years and is still receiving primarily felt-need preaching is not in the same position as a first-time visitor. They have been formed — by two years of consistently surface-level preaching — in a particular understanding of what Christianity is for. Reversing that formation requires deliberate pastoral investment in deeper material, and the congregation that has been trained on the accessible will resist the difficulty. Which is itself data about what the accessible preaching has produced.

---

The sermon that takes felt needs seriously and then moves through them — that uses the real problem as the entrance to the deeper question the real problem is pointing toward — is harder to preach than either the pure felt-need sermon or the pure theological lecture. It requires the preacher to know the congregation well enough to name the actual presenting concern, to have the theological depth to trace it to its roots, and to make the connection between the two in a way that does not feel like bait-and-switch.

That is pastoral preaching at its most demanding. It is also the preaching that forms people capable of holding their faith in actual life — not because it avoided difficulty but because it trained them to bring the full resources of the gospel to bear on the full texture of their experience.

The felt need is real. It is the right place to begin. It is never the right place to end.

The text almost always has something to say that is larger than the presenting problem, and the congregation is waiting — whether they know it or not — to be led to it. The preacher who stays at the surface has not been kind to them. They have been convenient.

Convenience is not the pastoral calling. Formation is. And formation begins where the felt need is, and goes somewhere the felt need alone could never reach.
