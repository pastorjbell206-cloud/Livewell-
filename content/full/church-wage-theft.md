The law is direct. Leviticus 19:13: *Do not defraud or rob your neighbor. Do not hold back the wages of a hired worker overnight.* Deuteronomy 24:14–15: *Do not take advantage of a hired worker who is poor and needy, whether that worker is a fellow Israelite or a foreigner residing in one of your towns. Pay them their wages each day before sunset, because they are poor and are counting on it. Otherwise they may cry to the LORD against you, and you will be guilty of sin.* The malakh — the hired worker — is named, protected, and the specific mechanism of exploitation is described in detail: holding wages. The law does not address an abstraction. It addresses a specific practice by which employers extract additional value from workers by delaying or withholding what is owed.

Three thousand years of canonical clarity on this point, and the American church has nearly nothing to say about wage theft.

Wage theft — the failure to pay workers what they have legally and contractually earned — is not a minor problem at the margins of the economy. By the most comprehensive analysis conducted, wage theft costs American workers more annually than all property crimes combined [cite — confirm]. The Economic Policy Institute estimates that minimum wage violations alone — employers paying workers less than the legal minimum — affect approximately 2.4 million workers and cost them roughly $8 billion per year [cite — confirm]. This is theft. The label is not rhetorical: it describes the taking of what belongs to another person. The employer who does not pay the agreed wage has taken something. The law says so. The Scriptures say so at greater length than they address most things the church treats as urgent.

Wage theft takes several forms, each worth naming concretely. Minimum wage violations are the most straightforward: paying less than the legal minimum, either by keeping wages formally below the threshold or by stealing hours through mandatory off-clock work. Overtime violations are among the most common: requiring workers to work more than forty hours without paying the mandated overtime rate, often by misclassifying employees as managers exempt from overtime requirements. Tip theft occurs when employers skim gratuities workers earned from customers. Illegal deductions reduce a paycheck below what is owed. Off-the-clock work requirements — arriving early, staying late, working through unpaid breaks — extract labor for which the worker receives nothing.

These are not primarily the practices of small fly-by-night operations. They occur systematically in major industries: food service, retail, domestic work, agriculture, construction [cite — confirm]. The workers most affected are, with remarkable consistency, the ones who least have the power to challenge the arrangement: workers without documentation, workers who fear job loss, workers who do not know their legal rights, workers in industries where retaliation is common and reporting mechanisms are inaccessible. The geography of wage theft maps onto the geography of poverty and marginalization. It is not random. It is structural — the exploitation of vulnerability by those who have arranged the power relationship to their advantage.

Deuteronomy's specific instruction is illuminating. *Because they are poor and are counting on it.* The command to pay the worker before sunset is not about the ledger. It is about the person behind the labor — a person who is counting on the wage to buy food that night, who has structured their life around the expectation that what was promised would arrive, who lacks the financial cushion that makes delayed payment merely inconvenient rather than catastrophic. The law encodes a specific attentiveness to vulnerability — a recognition that the power differential between employer and worker requires protective constraint, not because employers are necessarily malicious but because the logic of accumulation, left unrestrained, tends toward the extraction of everything it can take from the people who have the least power to resist.

James 5:1–6 is one of the most severe passages in the New Testament, and it is addressed to the wealthy who have withheld the wages of their field workers: *Look! The wages you failed to pay the workers who mowed your fields are crying out against you. The cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord Almighty.* The image is striking — the unpaid wages themselves are crying out, as if the abstracted economic harm has become a living complaint before God. James does not soften this. He is describing a specific structural sin — not personal moral failure, not sexual ethics, not any of the categories the American church has organized its moral concern around. Economic exploitation of workers. The wages held back. The luxury of the employer purchased at the cost of the worker's survival.

The church's near-total silence on this is not an oversight. It reflects the social composition of many congregations — specifically, the presence of business owners and managers in leadership roles, people whose institutional interests are not served by prophetic attention to wage theft. This is worth naming honestly. The congregation in which a significant portion of the giving comes from business owners is a congregation in which the pastor faces institutional pressure when addressing economic justice. The pressure is rarely explicit. It operates structurally, through the sense of what is preachable, what will be received as pastoral and what will be received as political. Most pastors absorb that pressure and preach accordingly, without ever consciously deciding to do so.

I have been that pastor. I have looked at texts like James 5 and found ways to preach about financial anxiety in general rather than wage theft in particular. I have treated the prophetic indictment as a metaphor for spiritual displacement from God rather than a concrete condemnation of a concrete practice. The shift was not dishonest — the spiritual dimension is real. But it evacuated the text of its most immediate and disturbing specificity. The workers whose wages are crying out are not metaphors. They are the two and a half million Americans who will work this week and receive less than what they are owed.

The steel-man for restraint is worth taking seriously. The argument runs that the church is not a labor regulatory agency, that wage and hour law is complex, that pastors lack the technical expertise to adjudicate specific employment claims, and that churches that wade into these waters risk politicization and congregational division. These are not frivolous concerns. The church that turns every Sunday into a policy seminar is not a church — it is a progressive advocacy organization with liturgy attached, and that is a failure mode as real as any other.

But there is a distance between preaching policy and preaching Scripture. James 5 does not require the pastor to propose regulatory reform. It requires him to read the text, explain the practice it condemns, and let the congregation sit with the implication that God hears the wages being withheld from workers and will not remain silent about it indefinitely. That is preaching Scripture. The congregation can do its own discernment about what it means for how they run their businesses, how they vote, and what they support. The pastor's job is to refuse to protect them from the texts that address those questions.

The Deuteronomic vision of the economy is not market neutrality. It is a system of obligations, protections, and limits designed to prevent the concentration of power at the top from destroying the conditions of life at the bottom. The Jubilee, the sabbath year debt release, the gleaning laws, the prohibition on wage withholding — these are not a comprehensive economic plan. They are a set of structural commitments to the proposition that the poor worker is not simply a production input whose compensation should be minimized by every available means. He is a person. He is counting on it.

The wages crying out have reached the ears of the Lord Almighty. That is not a small statement. It is a claim about what God hears when the church is silent. It is a claim that the silence of the pulpit on a practice the law specifically condemns is not neutrality but complicity.

Jeremiah wrote to Israel that the way to know God is to do justice — to judge the cause of the poor and needy (Jeremiah 22:16). *Is that not what it means to know me?* The church that knows God but does not judge the cause of the worker whose wages are being stolen has not yet fully known the God it claims to serve.

Wage theft is theft. The Scriptures say so. The question for the church is whether it will say so too, or whether it will continue to let the management of institutional relationships determine which thefts are worth naming.

There is a practical dimension to this that the church has largely left unexplored. Congregations contain workers who have experienced wage theft and employers who may have practiced it. They sit in the same pews, often at some distance from one another in terms of wealth and power. The sermon that names James 5 without flinching, that reads Deuteronomy 24 as applying to the employment decisions of the people in the room — that sermon creates an uncomfortable but genuinely necessary confrontation between what people confess and how they operate during the other 167 hours of the week.

The congregation that navigates that confrontation honestly — where the business owner hears the text and asks hard questions about his own practices, and where the worker who has been shorted hears the text and understands that God has heard his cry — is being formed into something resembling the community the prophets envisioned. Not comfortable. Not tidy. But alive to the same God who spoke through Amos and James and who has not changed his mind about what the wages withheld from workers represent.

The last word belongs to the text. Deuteronomy 24:15 closes with an observation that functions as a warning: *Otherwise they may cry to the LORD against you, and you will be guilty of sin.* The cry has already gone up. The question is whether the church will speak before the verdict arrives — or whether it will wait, as it has waited on so many other prophetic questions, until the cost of silence has already been paid by the people who could least afford it.

The workers are counting on it. They were counting on it in the wilderness, at the edge of Canaan, in the first-century Jewish diaspora, and in the restaurants and fields and warehouses of America in 2026. The God who hears their cry is not waiting for the church's permission to respond. The church's choice is whether to join its voice to what God has already said, or to protect the arrangement by preaching around it.
