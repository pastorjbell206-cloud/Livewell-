In 1493, Pope Alexander VI issued a papal bull called *Inter caetera* declaring that any land not inhabited by Christians was available to be "discovered," claimed, and brought under the dominion of the Catholic monarchies of Spain and Portugal. The logic was simple: the earth belongs to those who confess Christ, and those who do not confess Christ have no standing to prevent Christian nations from claiming what they have left unclaimed — which is to say, anything they happen to occupy.

This is the Doctrine of Discovery. It is not ancient history. It is the legal and theological foundation on which the Supreme Court of the United States still decides cases involving Native American land rights [cite — confirm]. It was cited as recently as 2005 in *City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation*, when the Court used the doctrine to prevent the Oneida from reasserting sovereignty over land their ancestors held before it was taken. A legal framework derived from a fifteenth-century papal document about the right of Christian nations to dispossess non-Christians remains embedded in American property law.

The American church has not reckoned with this. It is past time to say so plainly.

The Doctrine of Discovery was not secular policy dressed in religious language. It was theology. It was an argument about what the God of Scripture requires of Christian nations — namely, that they extend Christ's dominion by claiming the earth for the church. The missionaries who accompanied the conquistadors, who stood on the shore and read the *Requerimiento* to people who did not understand Spanish — a legal notice informing them that they were now subject to the Spanish Crown and the Church, and that if they resisted they would be enslaved — those missionaries believed they were doing God's work. That is not incidental to the doctrine. It is the doctrine.

American Protestantism inherited and adapted this framework. The Puritan settlers of New England understood their mission in explicitly covenantal terms that did not differ substantially from the Catholic logic of *Inter caetera*. They were God's people, entering a land promised to them, displacing a pagan population that had no standing before the God who was giving them the land. John Winthrop's famous "city on a hill" sermon drew on the language of Deuteronomy — the covenant people, the promised land, the blessing and curse — without questioning whether the inhabitants of the continent they were entering might also be people with standing before God and claims upon the earth they occupied.

The theological problem is not subtle. The Doctrine of Discovery requires that the imago Dei — the image of God borne by every human person — be selectively applied. It requires that the God who declares in Genesis 1 that every human being is made in the divine image, and who declares in Deuteronomy 27:19 that cursed is anyone who withholds justice from the foreigner, the orphan, and the widow, be understood to have authorized the dispossession of millions of people bearing that same image. The doctrine requires a God who is the God of Christian nations first and the God of humanity second. That is not the God of the Hebrew prophets. It is not the God of Jesus.

The response I most commonly hear from theologically serious Christians is that the Doctrine of Discovery represents a historical abuse that does not implicate the contemporary church. The errors were real but they are past. What has that to do with us?

I want to answer this honestly, because it deserves a real answer.

The errors are not past. The legal structures built on those errors continue to operate. The land was taken under a doctrine the church produced, the dispossession was total and is unreversed, and the communities that bear the consequences — Native communities with some of the highest rates of poverty, suicide, and preventable death in the United States [cite — confirm] — are not historical abstractions. They are people living with the present-tense results of five centuries of Christian-justified theft. When the church says that is not our problem because we did not personally do it, the church is claiming a kind of individual innocence that its own theology does not permit. The prophets did not repent only for their own individual sins. They repented for the sins of the nation, including the sins committed before they were born. Daniel prayed: "We have sinned and done wrong and acted wickedly and rebelled, turning aside from your commandments and rules" (Daniel 9:5). The *we* is not an individual confession. It is a communal one.

There is also the question of ongoing benefit. The land on which American churches stand was, in many cases, taken under policies that derived their legitimacy from the Doctrine of Discovery. The theological argument that authorized the taking was produced by the institution that now occupies the land. The church that prays on land taken in the name of Christ and then says the taking has nothing to do with it is claiming a disconnection between its theology and its history that it would not accept in any other context.

What does reckoning look like? I do not pretend this is simple, and I am not interested in answers that cost nothing. The beginning is knowledge. Most American Christians do not know what the Doctrine of Discovery is. Most American Christians do not know that Supreme Court land decisions affecting Native communities are still being decided on its basis. Most American Christians have never heard of the *Requerimiento*, have never read the text of *Inter caetera*, have never learned the specific theology that was used to justify the dispossession of the people who lived on this continent before European Christianity arrived.

Ignorance is not neutral. It is a choice, repeated across generations, to not know things that are difficult to know. The church that does not know its own history cannot confess it. The church that does not confess it cannot be released from it.

In Numbers 35, God establishes cities of refuge — places where the person who has caused death unintentionally can flee and find protection from the avenger of blood. The mechanism acknowledges that some forms of harm require institutional response, not merely individual guilt. The harm caused by the Doctrine of Discovery is not accidental — it was deliberately constructed theology — but the principle matters: some harms require communities to take protective, restorative action rather than simply declaring their personal innocence.

The prophetic tradition, from Amos to Isaiah to Micah, never permits the faithful community to declare neutrality on behalf of the exploited. "Seek justice, rebuke the oppressor, defend the fatherless, plead for the widow" (Isaiah 1:17). The tradition does not say: seek justice unless the injustice happened before you were born, or unless the systems of oppression are legally complex, or unless reckoning would be uncomfortable. The tradition simply says: seek justice.

James 2:14–17 makes the connection between confession and action in terms that the American church should find inescapable: "If a brother or sister is poorly clothed and lacking in daily food, and one of you says to them, 'Go in peace, be warmed and filled,' without giving them the things needed for the body, what good is that?" Saying the right things about historical injustice without acting toward present-tense repair is the precise dynamic James is describing. The faith that does not work is dead. The repentance that does not produce fruit is not repentance.

I am not standing outside this indictment. I have stood in church buildings on land with a complicated history and led worship without asking what that history was. I have preached on the justice of God in the abstract while not knowing who stood on the ground I was standing on before there was a church building there. That ignorance was not innocent. It was comfortable.

The Doctrine of Discovery requires the American church to do something it has been reluctant to do in any sustained way: reckon with the specific theological arguments it produced that caused specific, documented harm to specific people who continue to bear those consequences. Not as an exercise in guilt management. Not as a progressive political gesture. As theological work — the hard, necessary work of a community that takes seriously the claim that God is just and that injustice done in God's name matters to God.

The church that produced the Doctrine of Discovery is also the church that produced the prophetic tradition of confessional repentance. Both are part of the inheritance. The question is which one we live from.

That question has not yet been answered.

The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, adopted in 2007 with the United States initially voting against it [cite — confirm], affirms that indigenous peoples have the right to the lands, territories, and resources they have traditionally owned, occupied, or used. The American church — which claims to care about human rights as a theological category, which invokes the imago Dei in abortion debates and poverty debates and immigration debates — has been largely silent about a declaration that directly addresses the ongoing consequences of the theology it produced.

The silence is itself a theological statement. What the church will not speak is also a position.

Nehemiah 9 is the great confessional prayer of the returned exiles — a sustained recounting of the sins of the people across generations, naming specific failures, owning specific histories, asking for mercy not as individuals but as a people with a people's history. "We are in great distress," the prayer concludes (Nehemiah 9:37). Not: *they* are in great distress because of what *our ancestors* did. We. The communal confession does not create temporal distance from the harm. It closes it.

The church that produced the Doctrine of Discovery can also produce that prayer. Not as a performance. As the beginning of repair.
