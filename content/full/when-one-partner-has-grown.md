It starts as something you cannot quite name. A slow awareness that the conversations you are having with your spouse are not the conversations you are having anywhere else. That the person you have become does not map cleanly onto the person you were when you made your vows. That you have moved — in your faith, your understanding of yourself, your capacity for self-awareness — and the distance between where you started and where you are now is not the same distance your spouse has traveled.

This is one of the loneliest positions in a marriage. And it is one of the least talked about. Not because it is rare. Because naming it feels like an accusation. To say "I have grown and you haven't" sounds like arrogance dressed as self-awareness. So people carry it quietly, or they drop hints that land as criticism, or they slowly withdraw into the parts of their lives where they feel understood — and the marriage quietly hollows.

## The Language Worth Having

The New Testament gives us a word worth sitting with here: sunergos — a co-laborer, one who works alongside. Paul uses it repeatedly for his closest ministry companions in Romans 16:3, Philippians 2:25, and elsewhere. The image embedded in it is not sameness but shared direction. What makes two people sunergos is not that they are at the same place; it is that they are moving the same way.

That distinction matters. The question in a marriage where one person has grown is not "why hasn't my spouse caught up" — it is "are we still moving in the same direction, and am I willing to do the slow work of bringing them with me rather than leaving them behind?"

The answer to that question determines whether what is happening is a growth gap or a growing apart. They feel the same. They are not the same.

**Growing faster than your spouse is not the problem. Growing in a direction that no longer includes them is.**

## The Pastoral Reality

A woman I'll call Sarah came to faith seriously in her mid-thirties — the kind of faith that reorganized her priorities, her relationships, her sense of what her life was for. Her husband was not hostile to her faith, but he did not share it. They had been married for eleven years. The woman who had made those vows was a different person than the one now sitting across from him at dinner.

She described the gap not as theological but as dimensional. "He is a good man," she said. "He just doesn't want to go anywhere. And I can't stop going."

What she needed to hear — and eventually did — was that her growth had introduced an obligation, not just a gap. Growth that does not remain in relationship is not spiritual maturity; it is spiritual isolation with better vocabulary.

This is the part that the books about spiritual growth rarely say clearly. The growth becomes a problem not when it happens, but when the person who has grown begins to use the growth as a category that places them above the one who hasn't. Superiority is not the same as depth. The person who has genuinely wrestled with hard things and come out different should have, among the things they came out with, a more honest accounting of their own limitations — not a measuring stick for their spouse.

## The Historical Arc of the Problem

The marriage covenant in the biblical tradition was never premised on two people being at the same place spiritually or intellectually. Ruth and Boaz were at very different places in terms of their formation and their social position when the covenant was made. Hosea's marriage was built on a disparity so profound that it became a theological metaphor for God's relationship to Israel. Priscilla and Aquila, in Acts 18, appear to have had different gifts — she is sometimes listed first, which in ancient convention often signified greater standing in the relationship's given context.

What these portraits share is not equivalence but covenant — the decision to remain bound to another person, to continue moving in the same direction even when the pace is unequal, to extend to the other person the same patient investment that the relationship itself has required from the beginning.

The problem arises not from difference but from what difference does to the posture of the one who is ahead. Philippians 2:3 says: "in humility count others more significant than yourselves." This is one of the most demanding verses in the New Testament, and it is particularly demanding in the context of a marriage where one person has done significant interior work and the other has not. The person who has done the work has access to evidence that they are more self-aware, more emotionally present, more spiritually developed than their partner. The temptation to count that evidence and draw the obvious conclusion is real. The instruction is to not.

## The Counter-Position Worth Engaging

There is a view — not uncommon in certain Christian circles — that interprets the 1 Corinthians 7 passage about the believing spouse and the unbelieving spouse to mean that significant spiritual disparity in a marriage is grounds, or at least permission, for dissolution: "But if the unbelieving partner separates, let it be so. In such cases the brother or sister is not enslaved" (1 Corinthians 7:15). On this reading, the person who has grown spiritually is in a position analogous to the believer married to an unbeliever, and the distance between them is sufficient cause for departure.

This reading requires more than the text can bear. Paul is describing a specific case — a pagan spouse who chooses to leave the marriage upon their partner's conversion to Christianity. He is not providing a general principle that any significant spiritual gap justifies dissolution. The verse that follows is even clearer: "For how do you know, wife, whether you will save your husband? Or how do you know, husband, whether you will save your wife?" (1 Corinthians 7:16). Paul's actual prescription is to remain — and to remain with the specific intention that the covenant relationship might itself become the means of the other person's growth.

The growth gap, then, is not a problem to be resolved by departure. It is a situation in which the one who has grown has a particular responsibility — not superiority, not distance, but the patient presence that Paul describes when he says in Galatians 6:2: "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ."

## What Growth Actually Requires of the Person Who Has Done It

Genuine growth produces humility, not hierarchy. The person who has done real work on themselves knows how much they did not know, how many blindspots remain, how much of what they now see was invisible to them five years ago. If growth is producing impatience with a spouse who hasn't arrived yet, something else is happening. The growth is being wielded as a weapon rather than offered as invitation.

Proverbs 31:26 says wisdom's words are kind. Kindness is not the soft option — it is the harder one. It is easier to perform growth than to bring someone with you through it.

What the partner who has grown owes the marriage: continued investment, honest naming of the gap without contempt, patient invitation rather than ultimatum, and a genuine willingness to examine whether their own growth has produced in them something the other person would actually want to pursue. I have sat with people who described their spiritual growth in terms that would repel anyone listening carefully — a constant performance of depth, an impatience with anyone who didn't speak the same language, a spiritual sophistication that had somehow produced less warmth rather than more. That is not growth. That is acquisition without integration.

What the partner who has not grown owes the marriage: honesty about whether they are unwilling or simply uninvited. The two look identical from the outside. They are not identical in their implications. The person who is genuinely uninterested in growth requires a different kind of conversation than the person who has not been invited into the growth that their partner is experiencing. Many spouses who appear to have stopped growing are actually waiting to be included — waiting for an invitation that has not come because the growing partner has assumed the gap is permanent rather than simply uncrossed.

## What This Looks Like Over Time

The couples who survive this do not erase the gap. They build a bridge across it — with enough patience and enough honesty and enough refusal to let the silence become permanent. They stay curious about each other in a direction that the growth gap itself can illuminate. The growing partner learns from the one who seems less developed: often the person who has not engaged in formal therapeutic or spiritual formation carries a kind of practical wisdom, a groundedness, a capacity for ordinary fidelity that the more sophisticated partner has bypassed in their upward movement. The less visibly developed partner learns from the one who has done the work: that growth is possible, that the interior matters, that the conversation can go deeper.

## Three Questions for This Week

If you are the one who has grown: has your growth made you easier or harder to be married to? Would your spouse's answer match yours?

If you are the one who feels left behind: is the distance about direction or pace? Are you resisting the same destination, or just the pressure to arrive faster?

For both: when did you last learn something from each other? What would need to be true for that to happen again?

**This week:** one conversation where the person who has grown asks to be taught something — not performed humility, but genuine curiosity about what the other person knows that they don't.

The gap does not close by being ignored. It does not close by one person waiting for the other to catch up. It closes, slowly, by two people who are still choosing each other — who are willing to do the patient, unglamorous work of building a bridge rather than standing on their respective banks and watching the distance widen.

It is harder than leaving. It is also more worth it.
