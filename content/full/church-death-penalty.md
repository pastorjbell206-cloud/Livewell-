The United States executes people in the name of the state. It does so with the broad support of white evangelical Christianity, which for decades has been among the strongest institutional voices for capital punishment in American public life. A 2021 Pew Research survey found that white evangelical Protestants favor the death penalty at higher rates than any other religious group in the country [cite — confirm]. The same community that preaches the infinite worth of every human soul, the redemptive power of the gospel, and the possibility of genuine transformation votes, and prays, and advocates for the killing of people by the government.

This is not a minor inconsistency. It is a theological fracture that runs through the center of evangelical witness in America, and the church has largely chosen not to name it.

The argument for capital punishment from within the Christian tradition is not trivial. It deserves honest engagement. Genesis 9:6 — *whoever sheds human blood, by humans shall their blood be shed, for in the image of God has God made mankind* — is the foundational text, and it carries real weight. The argument runs that the taking of human life is so grave an act, so fundamental a violation of the imago Dei, that it demands a commensurate response. Romans 13:4 describes the governing authority as the servant of God, *an agent of wrath to bring punishment on the wrongdoer* — which has been read by serious theologians, from Augustine to Luther to Calvin [cite — confirm], as providing a biblical basis for the state's power over life and death. This is not a fringe position. It is the majority position of Christian theology for most of the tradition's history.

I have held versions of this position. I understand the internal logic. I am not dismissing it.

But the logic breaks under the weight of what capital punishment actually is and actually does in the United States of America in 2026.

The imago Dei argument for execution rests on the premise that execution honors the sacred worth of human life by taking seriously the gravity of its violation. The problem is that the system as actually practiced does not honor the sacred worth of all human life. It honors it selectively, in a pattern that follows the same lines of race and class that run through every other form of injustice in this country. Since 1976, when executions resumed after the Supreme Court reinstated capital punishment, Black defendants have been executed at rates disproportionate to their representation in the general population [cite — confirm]. The race of the victim matters even more: defendants convicted of killing white victims are significantly more likely to receive death sentences than defendants convicted of killing Black victims [cite — confirm]. The Innocence Project has documented more than 185 death row exonerations since 1989 [cite — confirm] — meaning that the system has sentenced to death, at minimum, scores of people who did not commit the crimes for which they were to be executed.

An execution that kills an innocent person is not a defense of the imago Dei. It is a violation of it. There is no theological framework in which the killing of an innocent person by the state honors the sanctity of human life. And a system that has produced this outcome at this rate is not a system that can be redeemed by appealing to the principle that undergirds it.

The deterrence argument fares no better under scrutiny. States with the death penalty do not have meaningfully lower murder rates than states without it [cite — confirm]. The certainty of punishment, not its severity, is what the criminological evidence suggests affects criminal behavior. The death penalty, as practiced, is not a deterrent. It is a ritual of vengeance that satisfies something in the public — a sense that justice demands an ultimate response to the ultimate violation — while doing none of the things that justice actually requires: accountability, repair, the protection of future victims.

Vengeance is mine, says the Lord (Romans 12:19). Paul quotes Deuteronomy in the context of a command to do the opposite — to feed the enemy, to refuse personal retribution, to overcome evil with good. The text is not a charter for state execution. It is a claim that vengeance belongs to God, which is a reason not to claim it for the state, not a reason to outsource it to the state.

The tradition of restorative justice — a framework rooted in part in biblical shalom, the Hebrew vision of right relationships restored — represents a serious alternative that the church has been slow to embrace [cite — confirm]. Restorative justice does not deny the reality of harm or minimize the suffering of victims. It asks a different set of questions: what has been broken, and what would it take to repair it? Who has been harmed, and what do they need? What responsibility does the offender bear, and how can that responsibility be taken seriously in a way that does not destroy the possibility of redemption?

The gospel's account of redemption is directly relevant here. The argument that some people are beyond redemption — that some crimes disqualify a person from the possibility of transformation — is not a Christian argument. It is a claim that the grace of God has limits, that there are people the cross cannot reach. That argument cannot be made on Christian grounds without fundamentally altering what the cross means.

Paul was a murderer before his conversion. He stood at the execution of Stephen, approving and holding the coats (Acts 7:58). By the logic that some crimes place a person beyond the reach of redemption, Paul's story should have ended at Damascus. It did not. The same Paul who approved Stephen's death wrote a substantial portion of the New Testament. I am not arguing that this means murderers should not face accountability. I am arguing that a theology that makes death the required response to death has not fully reckoned with the implications of the resurrection.

The specific gravity of the death penalty debate in the United States is inseparable from the history of American racial violence. Lynching — the extrajudicial killing of Black Americans by white mobs, predominantly in the South from Reconstruction through the mid-twentieth century — was not simply mob violence. It was a system of terror with explicit theological sanction from many white Southern churches [cite — confirm]. The continuity between lynching and capital punishment is not a rhetorical move. It is a factual claim about the demographics of who the system kills and what purposes it serves in maintaining racial hierarchy. Bryan Stevenson's work at the Equal Justice Initiative has documented this continuity at length [cite — confirm]. The church that has not reckoned with this history cannot offer a theologically serious account of capital punishment.

What I am not saying is that the victims of violent crime deserve less than full acknowledgment. The loss of a child to murder. The violation of the body. The grief that does not resolve. These deserve naming, fully. The impulse toward accountability — toward ensuring that serious harm is met with serious consequence — is not simply vengeance. It is a real dimension of the human need for justice. The pastoral failure would be to treat that impulse as only base and never legitimate.

But there is a difference between accountability and execution. A sentence of life without parole is serious consequence. It takes seriously the gravity of the harm. It protects future victims. It does so without the irreversibility of death — an irreversibility that becomes a catastrophe when the system makes errors, which it does. The imago Dei argument, pressed to its actual conclusion, demands that we take the image of God seriously in every person involved in the criminal system: the victim, the offender, the innocent person wrongly convicted, the executioner.

The church that supports capital punishment without engaging the racial disparities, without engaging the wrongful conviction data, without engaging the deterrence evidence, has not thought theologically about the matter. It has absorbed a cultural position, wrapped it in a proof-text, and called it Christian.

I have done this. I have preached positions I absorbed from the culture and held with the grip of conviction without having examined them carefully. The death penalty was one of them. The examination changed me. It will not change everyone. But the church cannot afford to exempt its most consequential positions from theological examination.

The question the gospel presses is not whether violent crime deserves serious response. It does. The question is whether the state's taking of human life is the response that honors the image of God in every person the system touches. Given the evidence of what capital punishment in America actually is and actually does — who it kills, how often it kills wrongly, what it costs, what it does not deter — the answer that emerges from honest theological engagement is no.

That answer will cost something to say. The evangelical culture that has treated capital punishment as a Christian position will receive this as a betrayal. But the church that refuses to examine its political positions against the full weight of what it claims to believe has not protected the gospel. It has protected its comfort.

The stakes are literal. People are being executed. Some of them did not commit the crimes for which they were condemned. All of them bear the image of God. The church has something to say about that, and the time to say it is not after the execution.
