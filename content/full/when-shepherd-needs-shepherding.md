You were never designed to carry the weight of ministry alone — and the Gospel you preach proves it. You know how to shepherd. You've sat with people in hospital rooms at midnight. You have driven across town to sit with a family that just got the worst phone call of their lives. You have stood at graves in February and said the words that hold people together when nothing else will.

So let me ask you a question, and I want you to sit with it before you answer: Who shepherds you?

Most pastors I talk to go silent when I ask that. Some give an answer — naming a spouse, a mentor, a close friend — and when I probe it, what I find is that the relationship they are describing is one where they process the work of ministry. The logistical difficulties. The difficult people. The institutional frustrations. But not the interior of their own souls. There is a difference. The person who listens to you talk about your board is not necessarily the person who is watching your soul.

## What the Gospel You Preach Actually Says

You were never designed to carry the weight of ministry alone. The Gospel you preach says so, clearly and without qualification. It says that the human self in isolation is not the design — it is the fracture. "It is not good for man to be alone" (Genesis 2:18) is not a comment about marriage, at least not exclusively. It is a description of the structure of human existence. We are made for dependence on God and for interdependence with one another. The pastor who has constructed a ministry life in which he is the one who gives care and never the one who receives it has not achieved spiritual maturity. He has reproduced in himself the very isolation the Gospel came to address.

Paul asked for prayer. Repeatedly. The man who wrote "I can do all things through him who strengthens me" (Philippians 4:13) also wrote "pray for us" (1 Thessalonians 5:25) and "I appeal to you, brothers, by our Lord Jesus Christ and by the love of the Spirit, to strive together with me in your prayers to God on my behalf" (Romans 15:30). He was not performing humility. He was naming a real dependence. The apostle to the Gentiles, the man who planted churches across the Mediterranean, publicly named his need for the prayers of the people he served. The relationship was mutual, not hierarchical. He gave them the Gospel and they gave him their prayers, and neither one was optional.

The pastor who cannot ask for prayer — who cannot name his own need to the people he leads — has confused professional distance with spiritual strength. They are not the same thing.

## What the Role Does Over Time

The loneliness of pastoral leadership is structurally produced. The congregation needs you to be the strong one, the settled presence in the storm, the person with answers. That role creates a relentless pull toward the management of appearances — toward performing stability even when the interior is not stable, toward projecting competence even when the situation is genuinely difficult.

This does not happen suddenly. It develops slowly, across years. As the weight of holding confidences separates you from the community. As the role creates distance that feels like maturity but is actually isolation. As the fear of appearing weak makes you progressively less willing to name what is actually happening inside you. As the performance becomes so practiced that you begin to lose track of the difference between the performance and the person.

And then one day you look up and you realize that you have no one. No one who knows the actual state of your marriage, not just the pastoral-care version of it. No one who knows that you have been carrying the same doubt for three years. No one who would notice if you stopped praying, because no one is close enough to see. The isolation is comfortable in the way that numbness is comfortable — you stop feeling the lack because you have adapted to it.

I have been in that place. I have sat in my study after a Sunday morning where everything went well — the service was strong, the people were encouraged, the feedback was warm — and felt something close to despair, because the performance of pastoral health and actual pastoral health are two entirely different things, and I had been performing long enough that I had lost track of which one I was doing. The congregation received something that looked fine. I was not fine.

## What Ezekiel 34 Names

Ezekiel 34 is the prophet's extended indictment of the shepherds of Israel — the leaders who have failed the flock. He describes them as shepherds who have fed themselves and neglected the sheep, who "have not strengthened the weak or healed the sick or bound up the injured" (Ezekiel 34:4). The passage is usually preached as a warning to pastors about neglecting their congregations. That reading is correct and important.

But there is another side of the passage that is less often addressed. The God who indicts the failed shepherds also promises: "I myself will tend my sheep and have them lie down, declares the Sovereign Lord. I will search for the lost and bring back the strayed. I will bind up the injured and strengthen the weak" (Ezekiel 34:15-16). The shepherd image runs in both directions. The congregation needs the pastor to shepherd. The pastor needs God to shepherd him. The failure mode Ezekiel is describing — the shepherd who consumes what the flock provides without tending the flock — is the failure mode of a man who has stopped receiving what he needs, which is why he is taking what he should be giving.

The pastor who is not being shepherded will eventually become one of two things: a consumer or a hollow man. The consumer takes from the congregation what was meant to be given to them — attention, affirmation, the emotional supply that comes from being needed — because he has no other source of nourishment. The hollow man goes through the motions without the interior life that the motions are supposed to express, because the interior life has not been tended by anyone.

## What Shepherding the Shepherd Requires

It requires someone who will not treat you primarily as a pastor. A peer, a spiritual director, a counselor who has no stake in the church's success, a friend whose relationship to you predates the ministry and exists outside it. Someone who will ask the real question — "How are you, actually?" — and wait for the real answer. Not "how's the church going" but "what is actually happening in you right now."

It requires that you stop protecting the image long enough to answer that question honestly. Not for the sake of emotional processing. For the sake of your soul. The pastor whose interior life has not been named and witnessed by another human being in years is a pastor whose interior life is likely deteriorating in ways that will eventually show up in the pulpit, in the marriage, in the slow erosion of the capacity for genuine pastoral care.

David had Nathan. Nathan was the one who walked into the room after David's worst decision — the arranged death of Uriah, the taking of Bathsheba, the abuse of a position of power so comprehensive that it reads as the full catalogue of what unchecked power does — and said: "You are the man" (2 Samuel 12:7). The prophet named what the king could not see about himself because the king had no one close enough to tell him. Nathan was not an enabler. He was not a flatterer. He was not someone who needed David to remain strong. He was the one who told the truth.

You need a Nathan. Not someone who needs you to be well so that their own faith is stable. Someone who will look at you clearly and say what they see.

The Psalms gave David a space to name what was actually happening inside him — the rage, the grief, the abandonment, the hope, the despair, the vindication — in all its violence and darkness, and put it before God. The fact that those prayers are in the canon is not incidental. God received them. He did not require David to perform stability before he would listen. He received the real thing, including the parts that were not edifying.

You are permitted to do the same. The God who received Psalm 88 — which ends without resolution, without comfort, with the speaker still in the dark — is the God who will receive your actual interior life and not only the version you have edited for public consumption.

## Who Is That Person for You

Who is that person for you right now? If you can name someone immediately, without hesitation — someone who knows you well enough to see you, who has the relationship with you to tell you the truth, who is not dependent on your performance remaining strong — then you have something worth protecting. Protect it.

If you cannot name anyone, that is the most important pastoral problem you currently have. More important than the difficult elder. More important than the declining attendance numbers. More important than the budget. The ministry that is running on the fumes of a depleted interior will eventually stop, and the stopping will be more damaging than anything the difficult elder or the budget shortfall could produce.

The shepherd needs shepherding. The Gospel you preach has always known this. The question is whether you have the honesty to apply to yourself the same truth you preach to everyone else.

You know how to sit with people in their hardest moments. This is one of yours. Sit with it.
