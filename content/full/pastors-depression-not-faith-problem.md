Charles Spurgeon preached to thousands of people at the Metropolitan Tabernacle on Sunday mornings and spent significant portions of his life unable to get out of bed [cite — confirm]. He wrote about his depression with the specificity of a man who was not performing vulnerability for an audience — he wrote about it because it was real, because it was sometimes disabling, because it returned when he had done nothing to invite it back. He called it a "fainting fit." He did not call it a faith problem. He knew better.

The tradition of preachers who have wrestled with what we now call depression is not a footnote. It is a primary document. Spurgeon. William Cowper, the hymn-writer, who experienced episodes of such severity that he twice attempted to take his own life and spent years in an asylum, and who wrote "God moves in a mysterious way, His wonders to perform" from inside a darkness most of his readers never knew he inhabited. Martin Luther, who described periods of such terror and abandonment that he believed himself condemned — what he called Anfechtung, a word that has no exact English equivalent but that includes something of what we would recognize as severe depression and spiritual crisis. The prophet Elijah, who after the defeat of the prophets of Baal sat under a juniper tree in the wilderness and asked God to take his life (1 Kings 19:4). The author of Psalm 88, who ends without resolution, without the familiar turn back to praise, with simply this: "darkness is my closest friend."

These are not spiritual failures. These are the testimonies of people who took God seriously enough to tell the truth about what it cost.

---

The theological confusion that identifies depression with lack of faith is not an ancient confusion. It is largely a modern one, and it has a specific genealogy.

It grows, in the American context, from a broad cultural narrative that equates Christian life with emotional wellness — that maps the abundant life Jesus promises in John 10:10 onto the categories of positive psychology and middle-class flourishing. In this frame, depression becomes diagnostic evidence of something wrong in the spiritual life: sin unrepented, prayer insufficiently urgent, trust inadequately formed, faith not big enough to claim the promise.

This is not the theology of the Psalms. The Psalms contain lament as a primary genre — prayers of abandonment, prayers of accusation, prayers that name darkness and do not exit it within the psalm's own frame. Psalm 13 opens "How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever?" Psalm 22 opens with the words Jesus quoted from the cross: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" These are not failures of faith. They are exercises of faith — the specific faith that brings the full weight of the actual experience to God, rather than the curated version.

The theology that equates depression with faithlessness is not the theology of the Psalms. It is not the theology of Paul, who wrote from prison, who had a thorn in the flesh that was not removed despite repeated petition (2 Corinthians 12:7-9), who catalogued his suffering in 2 Corinthians 11 without euphemism. It is not the theology of Jeremiah, who cursed the day of his birth (Jeremiah 20:14). It is a theology of the Sunday morning marketing department. It is a theology that has confused the hope of the resurrection with the present experience of the resurrection, and that confusion has done real damage to real people who needed the Psalms and got cheerfulness instead.

---

Depression in ministry is not rare. And it does not present the way the pastoral culture expects it to present, which is part of why it goes unaddressed.

The pastor does not, in most cases, stop functioning when he is depressed. He continues to preach. He continues to perform the ritual of pastoral care. He continues to appear, from the outside, to be doing the job. The depression is the weight underneath the performance — the gray that drains the color from everything, the inability to feel the things he speaks about, the private doubt that what he is doing means anything, the 3 a.m. hour when the voices are loudest and sleep is furthest.

The specific pressures of pastoral life create conditions in which depression is not a surprising outcome. Sustained exposure to grief and trauma — funerals, hospital visits, the slow weight of other people's suffering carried repeatedly without adequate processing — depletes the emotional resources that sustain resilience. The isolation of visible leadership means the pastor's peer relationships are structurally different from those of people around him — he is the one people come to with their worst, and he has few relationships in which he can be the one who goes to someone else. The entanglement of identity with congregational performance creates a particular kind of anxiety that never fully disengages. The absence of genuine community — despite being surrounded by people at all times — is one of the more specific features of pastoral depression, and one of the more under-discussed.

And then there is the biology. Depression is a clinical reality that involves brain chemistry, neurological function, genetic predisposition, and the kinds of factors that respond to correct doctrine about as well as a broken leg responds to correct doctrine. To say this is not to reduce human beings to their neurology. It is to take seriously what we know about how human beings are made — that we are embodied creatures, that our spiritual lives are lived in bodies, that the body's health and the soul's experience are not fully separable, and that therefore the body sometimes needs clinical attention that supplements rather than replaces the spiritual.

---

The practical reality for pastors dealing with depression is that the professional mask makes it harder to name.

You continue to function, continue to preach, continue to pray for others' healing while secretly doubting whether healing is available for you. The congregation cannot tell. The elders cannot tell. And the shame that accompanies the depression — the certainty that a man of faith should not feel this, that if he really believed what he preaches he would not be lying awake at 3 a.m. wondering if any of it is true — keeps him from seeking the help that would make naming it possible.

The shame is the specific pastoral version of the problem, and it is worth treating as its own obstacle. A depressed lay person can, in many churches, speak about their struggle and receive some measure of support. A depressed pastor faces the additional layer of anxiety about what disclosure would mean for his position, his authority, his congregation's confidence in his leadership. The fear is not irrational — pastoral culture has not always responded well to pastoral vulnerability. But the cost of the silence is usually higher than the cost of the disclosure, because the silence allows the depression to deepen while the reasons for secrecy remain.

What the depressed pastor needs is not, first of all, a better theology of suffering, though that may help. What he needs first is a safe relationship — one person, outside his professional responsibilities, who knows what is actually happening and will not use it against him. A counselor. A trusted peer pastor. A physician who understands that the presenting symptoms have a biological dimension and will not address only the spiritual. A spouse who has been invited into the reality rather than protected from it.

---

Here is the counter-argument, stated honestly: doesn't faith in God's sovereignty include trust that God can and does heal depression? Isn't there something to the tradition of prayer and spiritual battle and the authority of the believer that is worth bringing to bear here?

Yes. Fully yes. The spiritual resources of the tradition are real, and the pastor who is depressed and prays, who confesses, who reads Scripture as an act of faith when faith itself feels like performance — that practice is not irrelevant. It is not sufficient by itself, but it is not irrelevant. The Psalms were not written to be analyzed as literature. They were written to be prayed, and the pastor who prays Psalm 88 when he is in Psalm 88 is doing something that matters.

But the argument that prayer is the only resource — that medication represents a failure of faith, that professional counseling replaces rather than supplements the spiritual — is a theological position with a very thin biblical basis and a very long history of leaving suffering people without the help they need. The tradition that insists on prayer while denying the reality of clinical depression has a problem with the Psalms. It has a problem with Elijah. It has a problem with the incarnation's insistence that God's engagement with human suffering is material and physical and specific, not merely spiritual and abstract.

---

The practical implications are direct.

If you are a pastor reading this who is depressed, the first step is to say so — to someone. Not to the congregation, necessarily, but to someone. A counselor. A physician. A trusted peer. Name it. The naming breaks the isolation, which is itself part of the condition. Do not measure your faith by whether you feel it. Feelings are not the foundation. The foundation is the God who met Elijah under the juniper tree and said, before anything else, "arise and eat."

If you lead pastors — if you are a denomination executive, a bishop, a network leader, a PCN board member — build the structures that make disclosure possible without threatening a man's livelihood. The pastor who fears that admitting depression will cost him his call will not admit it until the concealment costs more than the disclosure. Most of the time, that moment arrives too late.

It is not a faith problem. It never was.

The prophets knew this. Spurgeon knew this. The author of Psalm 88 knew this, and wrote it down so that every generation after him would have a prayer that does not demand recovery by the final stanza.

The darkness is named in the canonical record. The tradition holds it.

So does the God of the tradition.
