David wrote Psalm 22 from a place no one who had not been there could fully identify. "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from saving me, so far from my cries of anguish?" He was not performing grief. He was reporting it. And the first movement of that psalm is solitary in a way that only someone who has carried weight in the dark can recognize — a man calling out and hearing nothing return, surrounded by people who do not understand what he is holding.

That is not only a spiritual portrait. It is a leadership portrait.

The loneliness of leading a congregation is structural. It is built into the role itself, not a symptom of doing it wrong. You hold information people gave you in confidence — the couple whose marriage is collapsing under the surface of their Sunday smiles, the elder whose son is in serious trouble, the woman in the third row who told you something she has never told another person. You carry that knowledge into every room. You make decisions that affect people's lives, and you often cannot explain why you made them without breaching the trust that made you worth telling in the first place. You see the institution from above and below and inside simultaneously, in a way that no individual member sees it, and the full view is not something you can share over lunch or unload in a board meeting or process in a sermon.

This is not complaint. This is description. Leadership requires the willingness to bear weight that cannot be distributed.

But I have come to understand something harder: the loneliness that is structural is one thing. The loneliness that is self-imposed is another, and it is the more dangerous kind.

I have watched men in ministry seal themselves off — not because the role demanded it, but because they decided that asking for help was a vulnerability they could not afford, that admitting struggle was inconsistent with the authority they needed to lead. They confused the necessary aloneness of decision-making with a total isolation that was not required by anything except their own fear of being known. They built a persona that performed competence, maintained it for years, and one day were not fine — and no one around them had any idea it was coming, because they had made absolutely sure no one would.

The result is almost always the same. A man who seemed fine, who was producing results and delivering sermons and meeting with people, and then one day was not. The congregation was blindsided. The family was blindsided. The man himself was, in some sense, the last to know how close to the edge he had been walking, because he had been too busy performing health to notice its absence.

Moses carried the disputes of an entire nation alone until his father-in-law Jethro came from Midian and saw it and told him plainly: "What you are doing is not good. You and these people who come to you will only wear yourselves out. The work is too heavy for you; you cannot handle it alone" (Exodus 18:17–18). That assessment came from outside Moses. Moses was too close to see it. He had been doing it this way long enough that the doing-it-alone had become invisible to him, the water he swam in. He needed someone positioned differently — someone with no stake in his performance, someone who loved him without needing him to be impressive — to name what the situation required.

Jethro's advice was not theological. It was organizational: appoint capable leaders, delegate the ordinary disputes, reserve yourself for the extraordinary ones. But behind the organization was a more fundamental claim — that the pretense of handling everything alone was not faithfulness. It was unsustainability dressed as dedication.

The pastoral tradition has never held up total self-sufficiency as a virtue. The elders of Acts 20 gathered together, not as isolated leaders in isolated churches but as a network of people who carried related weight. Paul wrote to his communities and received reports from trusted colleagues about what those communities were actually experiencing. The epistles are not the product of a solitary genius — they are the product of a man in constant correspondence, constantly informed by Epaphroditus and Timothy and Titus and the household of Chloe, constantly accompanied by a network of co-laborers who told him the truth about what was happening on the ground. Paul's authority was not compromised by his dependence on that network. It was expressed through it.

The tradition that produces the isolated pastor-hero is not the New Testament. It is a cultural inheritance — partly American, partly masculine, partly peculiar to the clergy class — that equates need with weakness and self-disclosure with inadequacy. It is the tradition that produces men who go twenty years without telling anyone the truth about what their ministry is costing them, and then leaves the profession in a condition of spiritual and emotional exhaustion that could have been interrupted if anyone had known.

I know this from the inside. I know what it is to walk into a Sunday service carrying weight from the week that I have told no one about, and to preach the words I believed and to feel the distance between what I was saying and what I was living. Not hypocrisy exactly — I believed it. But a kind of unsustained solitude that had become the default mode of my interior life. That default is not heroic. It is a slow withdrawal from the kind of connection that would have made me a better pastor and a more grounded human being.

Eugene Peterson, whose work shaped my understanding of pastoral ministry more than any other, wrote repeatedly about the pastor's need for friends who are not parishioners — people outside the pastoral relationship whose connection to you is not mediated by role or need or institutional expectation. [cite — confirm Peterson's specific treatment of this in *The Contemplative Pastor* or similar] The pastor who has only pastoral relationships has surrounded himself with people he must always, in some sense, perform for. He has no one who knows him without the title. And a man who is never known without the title eventually loses the ability to find the person underneath it.

That person is the one who can lead sustainably. The persona can lead for a while — often impressively, convincingly, long enough to build something substantial. But it runs on borrowed energy, and when the energy runs out, what is left is either collapse or a quiet, grinding diminishment that looks like leadership from the outside and feels like nothing much from the inside.

You are not called to carry this alone. You are called to lead well, and leading well requires honesty with at least a small circle of people about what it is actually costing you. A spiritual director who meets with you regularly and asks the questions you will not ask yourself. A peer pastor who carries the same kind of weight and will tell you when you are losing your way. A therapist who understands what ministry does to the interior life over time. The specific form matters less than the substance: someone who knows what you are actually carrying, not the performance you present on Sunday morning.

The peer network is the particular gift that the Pastors Connection Network was built to provide — not an organization of pastors performing success at each other, but a community of pastors telling the truth to each other. The difference matters enormously. Success performance is everywhere. Truth-telling is rare, and rare things have extraordinary value.

The loneliness of leadership is real. It is also, in significant part, a choice. The choice to remain alone with the weight is not protection and it is not dedication. It is, at its root, a form of pride — the belief that the need for companionship and accountability is a sign of inadequacy. The pastor who cannot be known is not protecting his congregation. He is protecting an image of himself. And the protection eventually becomes the thing that breaks him.

Psalm 22, the psalm of desolation that Jesus quoted from the cross, does not end in isolation. It moves — slowly, at great cost — from the cry of the forsaken to a community of witnesses: "For he has not despised or scorned the suffering of the afflicted one; he has not hidden his face from him but has listened to his cry for help" (Psalm 22:24). The voice that cried into silence was heard. The isolation was real, and it was not the final word.

It is not the final word for you either. But you have to be willing to say the thing out loud to someone who is actually in the room.

Find the people who can hold what you are holding. Not to fix it. To witness it. To stand with you in it long enough for the weight to be shared rather than performed.

That is often enough. And it is not as available as it should be, which is why the isolation continues — not because the company is absent, but because the asking for it requires the kind of courage that the role trains you to avoid.

The question is whether you will pay that cost, or whether you will wait until a larger one is due.
