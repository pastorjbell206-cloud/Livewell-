Ask the average pastor how his church is doing, and he will tell you a number. Attendance, usually. Sometimes giving. Sometimes both, in a ratio that signals whether things are moving in the right direction. These are the metrics the denomination wants, the metrics that appear on the annual report, the metrics that determine whether a pastor is perceived as effective. They are also, largely, the wrong metrics.

Not because they do not matter. They do — an empty room is a real problem, and a church that cannot fund its operations is a church in genuine crisis. But attendance and giving are lag indicators. They tell you what happened, not what is happening. They measure the output of a system whose real activity is largely invisible to any spreadsheet.

The church growth movement that reshaped American evangelicalism in the latter half of the twentieth century made a contribution that was genuine and a distortion that was significant. The genuine contribution was the insistence that the gospel ought to reach people and that failing to reach people should be examined rather than excused. The distortion was the reduction of "reaching people" to a headcount, and the consequent elevation of attendance as the primary measure of faithfulness. The pastor who filled seats was the pastor who was succeeding. The pastor whose seats were not filling was the pastor who had something to learn. This framework is so embedded in the average pastor's self-evaluation that it operates unconsciously — the Sunday morning number is not just a data point. It is a verdict on the week.

That verdict measures the wrong things.

The first question a pastor ought to be able to answer honestly is this: are people in this congregation becoming more like Jesus? Not more committed to the church's programs. Not more knowledgeable about the Bible's content — though that matters too. More like Christ — more generous with their material resources, more patient in conflict, more willing to extend mercy to people who have not earned it, more honest about their own failures. This is formation. It is the stated purpose of the church's existence — "to equip his people for works of service, so that the body of Christ may be built up until we all reach unity in the faith and in the knowledge of the Son of God and become mature, attaining to the whole measure of the fullness of Christ" (Ephesians 4:12-13).

Formation is almost never measured, because it is very difficult to measure, and the difficulty is not a reason to stop asking. The pastor who wants to assess his congregation's formation does not need a survey instrument. He needs to know his congregation well enough to answer the question from direct observation — whether the people he has been preaching to for five years treat their money differently, handle conflict differently, respond to suffering differently than they did when they arrived. If he does not know his people well enough to answer that question, the attendance numbers are irrelevant. He does not actually know how his church is doing.

A second honest question is whether the people in this congregation actually know each other. Not know of each other. Know each other — their names, their children, their anxieties, the specific weight they carry that is not visible on a Sunday morning. The early church in Jerusalem is described in Acts 2:44-45 as a community that held goods in common and distributed to anyone who had need. That kind of generosity requires a level of mutual knowledge that is not produced by sitting in the same room once a week. It requires community that has gone deeper than attendance.

Acts 2:42 describes the community as devoted to four things: the apostles' teaching, fellowship, the breaking of bread, and prayer. The fellowship — koinonia — is not a program. It is a quality of mutual life that makes it impossible for someone to have a need that goes unmet because no one knows about it. The church that reports healthy attendance but where the families who are struggling keep their struggles private, where the marriages in crisis are managing their presentation, where the person in financial collapse is tithing because they are terrified to admit the truth — that church has numbers but not koinonia. It has an audience, not a community.

A third question is harder: are people being sent from this congregation into the world with a genuine sense of vocation? The church that produces spiritually formed, deeply connected members who stay inside the church's walls and serve only its programs has not completed its mission. The measure of a healthy church is not only what happens inside its gathering, but what its people carry into their work, their neighborhoods, their families, their civic lives.

The New Testament does not describe the church as an end in itself. The gathering in Acts 2 is immediately followed by the scattering in Acts 8 — the persecution that sends believers throughout Judea and Samaria and, in Luke's framing, begins the fulfillment of the Great Commission. The pattern is: gather, be formed, be sent. The church that gathers and forms but does not send has stopped at the second movement of a three-movement piece. It is producing Christians who are theologically informed and relationally connected but whose formation never makes contact with the world the gospel is sent to.

The steel-man of the numbers-driven approach is real and should be heard. Without adequate attendance, no pastor long remains to form anyone. Without adequate giving, programs that serve the vulnerable cannot be sustained. Institutional viability is not nothing — it is a precondition for everything else. The church that is so focused on formation and fellowship that it fails to reach anyone new has become a private club with good theology. Growth matters. Mission matters. The congregation that has stopped caring whether anyone outside its current membership encounters the gospel has lost something essential.

This is true. And it does not refute the argument being made here. The question is not whether a church should want to grow. The question is whether the growth metrics are measuring growth in the right things. A congregation that grows in size while declining in generosity, in mutual accountability, in its engagement with the world outside its walls — that congregation is not growing in any sense that the New Testament would recognize. A congregation that grows in formation, deepens in genuine community, and increases in the number of its members who are living out their faith in their neighborhoods and workplaces — that congregation may or may not be growing in attendance, but it is growing in the things that matter.

The pastor who reports good numbers to his board while privately knowing that his congregation is unchanged, unformed, and barely acquainted with each other is reporting on the wrong things. He is also, likely, carrying a private weight that the numbers cannot address — because the numbers look right and something still feels wrong, and he cannot say what it is to people who are reading the spreadsheet.

What feels wrong is that the metrics he is using cannot see what he is actually responsible for.

The metrics the New Testament offers for congregational health are qualitative, relational, and slow to develop. Formation takes years. Community takes sustained investment. Vocation develops through the long process of integrating faith with work and life. None of these are measurable in the way that Sunday attendance is measurable, and none of them will appear on the annual report.

But they are what the church is for.

Name what you are measuring. Then ask whether those measurements are capturing the right thing. If they are not, change the measurements first — and be willing to defend to your board why the new measurements matter, which requires being able to articulate what the church is actually for.

That conversation, between a pastor and his governing board, about what success actually means, is one of the most important conversations most churches have never had.

It is not too late to have it.
