There is a particular dread that arrives on Thursday afternoon. The text is open. The commentary stack is there. And nothing comes — not the electricity that used to arrive, not the sense that this passage is hunting you, that it needs to be preached. Just words. Just obligation. Just the crushing awareness that Sunday is three days away and you have nothing that will cost you anything to say.

Every honest preacher knows that chair. The question is not whether you will sit in it, but whether you will tell yourself the truth about why you are there.

---

Stale preaching is not primarily a craft problem. It is a soul problem — and most preachers try to fix the craft because the soul examination is harder to bear. They buy a new commentary. They switch series. They try a different structure. They discover a preacher they envy and borrow his moves. None of it works for long, because the real problem is not what is in the books. The real problem is that somewhere in the accumulated weight of ministry — the funerals, the board meetings, the pastoral emergencies at ten o'clock on a Saturday night, the decade of Sunday mornings in the same room — the preacher stopped being a person before God and became a professional before a congregation.

The difference is audible from the third row. A sermon produced by a man who is processing the text publicly, for people who need it, sounds different from a sermon produced by a professional who is executing a performance. The congregation cannot always name the difference, but they feel it. There is a quality of weight that comes from the preacher who is genuinely wrestling — who is speaking from the middle of a question he has not fully resolved, who is offering what the text has cost him rather than what the text yields under efficient extraction. When that quality is absent, the words remain grammatically correct and theologically defensible and existentially thin.

The history of Christian preaching is instructive here. Augustine's sermons in Hippo were famous for their rawness — he often preached from a text he chose that morning, with his congregation responding aloud, interrupting, questioning [cite — confirm]. It was not efficient. It was alive. John Chrysostom, preaching in Antioch and later in Constantinople in the late fourth century, was called "the golden-mouthed" not because of his technique but because of the sense his hearers had that he was preaching from a place of genuine conviction about what he was saying [cite — confirm]. The craft was there, but the craft was in service of something that came from elsewhere. When craft becomes the primary thing — when the preacher is executing a method rather than opening a wound — the congregation notices, even without the vocabulary to say what they are noticing.

---

The Psalms model something the modern pulpit has mostly forgotten. The poets of Israel did not write from composure. They wrote from the middle — from inside the confusion, the grief, the bewilderment that God was silent. Psalm 22 opens not with a sermon point but with a cry: *My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from saving me, so far from my cries of anguish?* (Psalm 22:1). This is not a rhetorical device. It is a man in genuine distress asking a genuine question about whether God is present. The psalm does not resolve the question quickly. It holds the tension for twenty verses before arriving at any kind of confidence, and even then the confidence is hard-won, not simply assumed.

The preacher who has not spent time in that territory — not as a rhetorical exercise but as a genuine description of his own inner life — has nothing to offer the congregation member who sits there in exactly that condition every week. There is one in the third row right now. One in the back who has not slept in three months because of a diagnosis. One near the exit whose marriage is ending. They do not need a man who has managed the text into a clean three-point structure. They need a man who has been in the dark and is willing to say so.

When preaching goes stale, the first and most important question is not *what technique can I recover* but *when did I stop going to the dark places?* When did I start reading the text only as material to be processed for Sunday rather than as a message addressed to me, specifically, about my own life with God? The preacher whose personal devotional life has become indistinguishable from his sermon preparation is a preacher who has stopped eating and started cooking. He handles food all day. He is starving.

---

The structural problem underneath stale preaching is often isolation. Elijah, exhausted after Carmel, did not need a vision or a new strategy. He needed sleep, bread, water, and someone who said *the journey is too great for you* (1 Kings 19:7). What he received was presence. The preacher who preaches to community without living inside community of his own will eventually run out of the kind of deep knowing that makes preaching land somewhere other than the surface.

Preaching is always, on some level, the articulation of what has been lived. When a pastor is living his whole life inside the church building — managing, administering, counseling, troubleshooting, performing the pastoral presence that the congregation expects — and has no life outside that frame that is genuinely his own, the preaching will eventually reflect the deprivation. There is nothing coming in that is not immediately being processed for consumption. The well cannot give what it is not receiving.

This is why the ancient practice of *lectio divina* — the slow, prayerful reading of Scripture as a reader rather than as a preacher — is not a luxury. It is a survival requirement. Not reading to find next week's sermon. Reading because God might say something to you, specifically, today. Eugene Peterson argued consistently across his career that the pastor who had no life before God apart from ministry preparation was not a pastor but a religious professional, and that the distinction mattered for the congregation as much as it mattered for the pastor himself [cite — confirm]. The congregation gets what the pastor is. When the pastor is empty, the congregation gets emptiness dressed in competence.

---

Steel-manning the drift is important here: there are legitimate explanations for a season of staleness that have nothing to do with spiritual failure. Grief does this. Protracted conflict does this. The kind of exhaustion that accumulates across fifteen years of sustained giving without adequate replenishment does this too. Sometimes the well is not polluted. It is simply empty. And an empty well needs to be filled, not blamed.

But even that diagnosis points to the same answer. The filling does not happen through ministry productivity. It happens through the kind of sustained, unhurried encounter with the living God that the ministry machine is designed, inadvertently, to prevent. The meeting is never done. The email never goes to zero. The need is never fully met. And so the pastor who waits for his schedule to clear before he will seek God — before he will take the retreat, before he will sit with the text without an agenda, before he will let himself be prayed for rather than always being the one who prays — will wait forever, and slowly die in the waiting.

The most practically urgent thing a preacher can do when his preaching goes stale is to stop trying to fix the preaching and start asking what has happened to the preacher. Trace the moment when the obligation arrived and the encounter left. When did Sunday become primarily a performance event rather than a moment of genuine witness? When did the text stop being the place where God addresses you and become the material from which you construct addresses to others?

Go back to that moment. Not with self-condemnation — self-condemnation is another form of productivity, another way of staying busy in the soul's house rather than opening the windows. Go back to it with curiosity. Something changed. Name what it was. Was it a loss? A conflict? A season of success that subtly shifted your identity from servant to performer? The sermon preparation cannot carry you forward until the preacher himself is moving in the right direction.

---

There is also the community question, which the isolated pastor never asks. The stale preacher almost always preaches into a vacuum — he does not know what is actually happening in the lives of the people he is preaching to. He knows the aggregate, the demographic, the general categories of suffering that his congregation contains. He does not know the specific person in the third row who is going to hear the passage he is preaching on Sunday. He has stopped making hospital visits and started managing systems. The pastoral texture of his ministry has thinned, and the preaching has thinned with it, because preaching at its best is always a response to the actual people actually present, and you cannot respond well to people you do not know.

This is not a call to emotional exploitation — the sermon that mines pastoral encounters for illustrative material without the parishioner's knowledge is a violation of trust, not an example of good preaching. It is a call to sustained presence among the people being served, so that the preacher knows from the inside what the congregation is carrying, and can speak to it with the specificity that distinguishes prophetic preaching from generic religious content.

The verdict: you cannot give what you have stopped receiving. The fix is not more preparation. The fix is more exposure — to text you are reading for yourself, to people who know you past your title, to the God who called you into this work in the first place.

The sermon that matters next Sunday is not built in the study on Thursday. It is built in the accumulated faithfulness of a life before God across the whole week. When that faithfulness is present, even a preacher who is uncertain and unpolished will find that what he says carries weight — not because he performed it well, but because it is true, and truth has mass.

The fire is not gone. But you may need to stop running long enough to find where it went.
