Most people have two options they cycle between when conflict enters their marriage. Option one: engage with full intensity, let the argument expand until it has absorbed every grievance from the past six months, and end somewhere both people feel worse. Option two: avoid the conflict entirely, manage the surface, let the unaddressed thing burrow underground where it will surface again in three weeks wearing a different face.

Neither of these is conflict. The first is escalation. The second is suppression. Both are ways of not dealing with what is actually happening.

Healthy conflict looks different from both, and it is worth describing carefully, because most people have never seen it modeled. Not in their families of origin. Often not in their faith communities. Almost never in the culture at large, where conflict either becomes performance or gets resolved in the last five minutes of a thirty-minute episode.

## The Biblical Architecture of Conflict

Proverbs 15:1 — "A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger" — is not an instruction to be passive. The word translated "soft" in Hebrew is *rak*: gentle, tender, responsive to pressure. The proverb is describing the capacity to stay regulated in a moment of heat, which is one of the most demanding skills a human being can develop. It is not the instruction to have no answer. It is the instruction to shape the answer in a way that does not pour fuel on what is already burning.

James 1:19 adds the architecture: "quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger." This is a sequence, not a suggestion. It places listening before speaking and before anger. The person who does this in a conflict is not performing patience. They are refusing to let their nervous system override their values.

Both texts are describing something that has to be learned. Nobody does this naturally. The default human response to perceived threat — which is what conflict triggers — is some version of fight or flight. The fight version produces escalation. The flight version produces suppression. Neither produces resolution.

Matthew 18:15-17 provides Jesus's own framework for conflict within the community: "if your brother sins against you, go and tell him his fault, between you and him alone." The instruction is direct — go, tell, address. Not manage, not suppress, not wait and hope the situation resolves itself. The purpose of the direct conversation is stated in the same verse: "if he listens to you, you have gained your brother." The goal is restoration. Not victory. Not vindication. Restoration.

## What Research Shows About How Couples Fight

**The goal of conflict in a marriage is not to win. It is to be understood and to understand. Those two goals require entirely different strategies.**

John Gottman's forty years of research on couples produced a distinction that belongs in every premarital counseling curriculum [cite — confirm]. The difference between couples who stay together and couples who don't is not the absence of conflict. Couples who last fight as much as couples who don't — sometimes more. The difference is how they fight.

The couples who handle conflict well do several identifiable things. They attack the problem, not the person. They state what they need rather than cataloguing what the other person has failed to do. They take breaks when the nervous system is flooded, rather than continuing a conversation that has moved past the point of productive communication. And they repair — quickly, specifically, without requiring the other person to grovel.

The couples who don't handle it well use what Gottman calls the Four Horsemen: criticism (attacking character rather than behavior), contempt (communicating disgust or superiority), defensiveness (refusing to receive feedback), and stonewalling (shutting down entirely). These are not dramatic moves. They are ordinary habits. And they are learnable in reverse.

Contempt is the most corrosive. It carries within it the evaluation that the other person is beneath the contempt-holder — less than, not worth engaging. Gottman's research identified contempt as the single most reliable predictor of a marriage's end [cite — confirm]. Not conflict frequency. Not conflict intensity. The presence of contempt in how two people engage each other.

## The Historical and Theological Weight of This

The church has not always served married couples well in this area. The theological emphasis on forbearance — "bearing with one another in love" (Ephesians 4:2) — has sometimes been taught in ways that discourage legitimate conflict rather than shape it. The result is couples who have learned to perform peace rather than achieve it. They sit in the pew together every Sunday and will not look at each other in the parking lot.

Forbearance is not the suppression of legitimate needs. It is the patient willingness to engage those needs without explosion, to bring them into the relationship rather than performing as though they do not exist. The same letter that commands forbearance also commands "speaking the truth in love" (Ephesians 4:15) — a phrase that requires both things simultaneously, not alternating between them.

The household codes in Ephesians are not a description of a conflict-free marriage. They are a description of how two people oriented toward each other's flourishing handle the inevitable friction of two human beings living a shared life. Friction is built into the structure. What is not built in is the particular form friction takes.

## The Counter-Position Worth Hearing

Some pastoral and counseling traditions emphasize that the spiritual discipline of self-denial is the primary tool for conflict in marriage — that the husband who dies to self, the wife who chooses forbearance over expression, the couple who brings their grievances to prayer before bringing them to conversation are practicing the more excellent way. The concern is that a therapeutic framework for conflict management imports secular psychology into what is fundamentally a spiritual problem.

This concern is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing. There is a real way in which the therapeutic language of "flooding" and "repair attempts" can displace the theological language of repentance, forgiveness, and genuine self-offering. The question of whether a conversation needs a twenty-minute break for regulation or a twenty-minute prayer for humility is not always the same question.

The answer is that both are true and neither replaces the other. The neurological reality of flooding — that the human nervous system past a certain threshold of activation cannot engage in productive communication — is not a therapeutic construct. It is a physical fact that the Holy Spirit has not suspended. The person who insists on continuing a conversation when both people are past the point of productive engagement, because ending the conversation feels like spiritual failure, is not practicing faithfulness. They are practicing willfulness, and the conversation will produce damage rather than repair.

The disciplines of prayer, self-offering, and genuine repentance operate before and after the conversation. They shape the person who enters the room. They do not substitute for the concrete skills of staying in the room.

## The Specific Moves That Change Things

First: distinguish between the presenting issue and the underlying need. Most conflicts are not about what they appear to be about. "You never listen to me" is almost never a statement about the specific conversation happening right now. It is a bid for reassurance about being known and valued. Addressing the underlying need — "you matter to me, tell me what you actually need from this conversation" — often resolves what the argument never could.

Second: take the break before you need it, not after. The neurological threshold for flooding arrives faster than most people realize, and past it, nothing useful happens. Saying "I need twenty minutes" before you hit the wall is not avoidance. It is responsible self-regulation. The twenty minutes is not a rejection of the conversation. It is the preparation required to have it honestly.

Third: repair. Small, specific, quick. "That came out wrong. What I meant was..." The repair attempt does not need to be perfect. Research shows that the willingness to attempt repair matters more than the quality of the attempt. The couple that attempts repair badly is in better shape than the couple that waits for perfect repair and waits too long.

## Three Questions for This Week

What is the pattern of conflict in your marriage — does it escalate, avoid, or move between both? What triggers the escalation?

In your last significant argument, what were you actually trying to communicate underneath the words you used? Did your spouse receive that message?

Do you know your own early warning signs of flooding — the physical and emotional signals that tell you the nervous system is past productive engagement?

**This week:** the next time a low-stakes irritant comes up, practice the soft answer. Not capitulation — a regulated, honest, specific response. Notice what happens.

Conflict is not the problem in a marriage. Conflict is the curriculum. The couple that learns to fight well is building a skill that will serve them in every other area of the relationship. Safety grows where you know the hard conversation will not destroy what you have.

That safety is built in exactly the places it feels most threatened. Not by avoiding the hard conversation. By learning to have it without losing the person.
