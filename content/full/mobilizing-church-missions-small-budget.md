The assumption lives in almost every small church I have encountered: that world missions is something larger churches do. That the congregation of 85 or 120 or 200 — the one running on a lean budget, the one where the pastor wears multiple hats and the building fund is always one step behind — that congregation is not a missions-sending church. It is a missions-giving church, perhaps. It will write a check to a denomination, attend a conference, receive a missionary's prayer letter. But sending? That is for churches with the infrastructure to do it properly.

I want to argue against that assumption with considerable force, because it has left entire categories of churches — and the people those churches would have reached — in an impoverished arrangement that serves neither the church nor the mission.

---

The assumption begins from a wrong premise: that missions is primarily a financial operation. That what moves people from where they are to where God is calling them is money, and that without substantial money, the movement cannot happen. This premise is historically false and theologically confused.

The New Testament missions movement was not capitalized by wealthy institutions. Paul was tentmaking (Acts 18:3) — working his trade in Corinth to fund his own ministry, receiving the occasional gift from Philippi (Philippians 4:15-16) with enormous gratitude precisely because such support was not guaranteed. He lists his credentials in 2 Corinthians 11 not as institutional backing but as suffering: *Five times I received at the hands of the Jews the forty lashes less one. Three times I was beaten with rods. Once I was stoned. Three times I was shipwrecked* (2 Corinthians 11:24-25). What propelled Paul's missionary movement was not a sending church with a well-funded missions budget. It was conviction, flexibility, adaptability, and a theology of the cross that understood suffering as the normal shape of apostolic ministry rather than a problem to be solved by adequate funding.

The early church in Antioch — the first deliberate sending church we see in Acts — was itself a church planted by scattered believers who were not apostles and not the Jerusalem leadership (Acts 11:19-21). They were unnamed, ordinary disciples driven out by the persecution following Stephen's death, who arrived in Antioch and started talking to Greeks about the Lord Jesus, and a large number believed. Out of that congregation, still young, the Spirit called Barnabas and Saul (Acts 13:2). There is no indication in the text that the Antioch church had the financial resources to support what it was being asked to send. It sent anyway.

---

The theological problem with waiting for a large budget is that it substitutes institutional readiness for spiritual readiness, and the two do not track together the way we assume. The congregation that says *when we have the resources, we will be a missions-sending church* is making a claim about causality that the New Testament does not support. The causality in Acts runs the other direction: the sending precedes the abundance, and the abundance — of faith, of partnership, of effectiveness — follows the obedience.

This is not a prosperity-theology argument. It is a missiology argument rooted in the pattern of how God has consistently worked in the history of Christian witness. The Welsh Revival, which shaped global evangelicalism in the early twentieth century, came from a context of mining villages and working-class poverty, not institutional wealth [cite — confirm]. The explosive growth of the church in the Global South over the past fifty years has been largely driven by churches in contexts of genuine material poverty that have nevertheless sent missionaries across their own countries and into neighboring ones [cite — confirm]. The premise that money is the primary catalyst for missions is a Western projection, not a universal principle.

---

So what does a small-budget church actually need to mobilize for world missions? The answer is simpler and more demanding than a budget line.

It needs a pastor who is personally captivated by the global scope of what God is doing. This is not optional. The church will not care about what the pastor does not care about — not in the way that mobilizes people toward genuine sacrifice. A sermon about missions from a pastor who is himself genuinely gripped by the stories coming out of unreached communities in Central Asia or the church planting movements among the Maasai or the house church networks in Iran is different from a missions Sunday designed to fulfill a denominational expectation. The congregation can tell which one they are in. The first produces engagement. The second produces polite attendance.

The pastor who is personally connected — not just informationally aware — to a missionary, a national pastor, or a mission organization through a genuine relationship rather than a mailing list, will preach with different weight. He will be able to say: I spoke to this specific person last week, and here is what he told me. That specificity is irreplaceable. It transforms missions from a category into a community.

---

Relationships are the budget substitute. What small churches lack in financial capital, they can often provide in relational and prayer capital, and those are not consolation prizes. They are primary goods.

The missionary who has a church of 80 people who know her name, who pray for her specifically, who will respond when she sends an urgent prayer request at 2 a.m. from a hard situation in a difficult field — that missionary is better resourced than the one whose support comes from a hundred churches who write checks and occasionally include a form letter. The relational investment is not supplemental to the financial support. In many cases it is more sustaining.

This means that mobilizing for missions is partly about building a congregation that reads the prayer letters rather than filing them, that learns the names of the missionary's children, that keeps the map on the wall and refers to it in prayer. It means annual visits when possible — not tourism, but genuine presence — so that the congregation is sending someone they know rather than supporting an abstraction. It means treating the missionary relationship the way you would treat any relationship that matters: with attention, consistency, and the kind of specificity that tells the other person they are actually seen.

The church that genuinely cares for one missionary is doing more for that missionary than ten churches that send money without names.

---

Small churches also have capacity that large churches frequently lose: flexibility, directness, and the willingness to try things without a committee approval process. The congregation of 85 can make a decision at a Sunday evening business meeting and act on it the following week. The congregation of 2,000 has layers of governance that can make rapid mobilization difficult. The small church's governance structure, which is often seen as a limitation, is in the context of missions a considerable advantage.

This matters because missions mobilization often requires responding to specific opportunities that appear with short time horizons. A national pastor in Kenya needs funds to train twelve church planters before the rainy season makes the roads impassable. A refugee church in a European city needs someone to come and spend two weeks doing specific training. A church-planting team in a creative-access country needs a particular technical skill that someone in your congregation happens to have. The small church that is paying attention and has built the relationships can respond to those specific needs with agility that no large-church bureaucracy can match.

---

What about the genuinely limited financial reality? This is where creativity is required, not as a workaround but as a genuine theological discipline. The question is not *what can we afford?* but *what are we willing to sacrifice for?*

A church of 100 that designates 15% of its giving to missions — a figure that many small churches have achieved by making the decision and preaching it clearly — has something real to work with, even if the absolute dollar amount is modest. A church that commits to a specific co-funding model with a sending organization or denomination can multiply its financial impact beyond its proportional giving. A church that combines giving with sending — even if the "sending" is one person for one month — creates a more complete form of participation in the mission than writing checks alone.

The model of short-term sending with long-term follow-through is particularly suited to small churches. You send one person for two weeks. He comes back and spends the following two years keeping the congregation informed about what he saw, praying specifically, maintaining the relationship he built. The ongoing investment is entirely within the church's capacity because it costs time and attention rather than money. The short-term investment builds the relationship that makes the long-term investment possible.

---

There is a form of paralysis that small churches need to name and resist: the paralysis of comparison. The church that measures its missions engagement against what the megachurch across town is doing will always feel inadequate, because the comparison point is chosen specifically to produce inadequacy. The relevant comparison is not what someone else is doing — it is what faithful obedience looks like for this congregation, in this context, with these people and these resources.

The widow in Mark 12 gave two small coins. Jesus did not say: she gave more than all the others because she gave proportionally more. He said: she gave more than all the others (Mark 12:43). The measure is not the amount. The measure is the sacrifice — the willingness to give out of poverty rather than out of surplus.

A church of 85 people, mobilized for missions by genuine conviction, keeping genuine relationships, praying with genuine knowledge, sending when they can and giving what they have — that church is participating in the mission of God in a way that has no budget requirement. The requirement is something harder and more renewable than money: it is attention, love, and the willingness to believe that the world beyond this congregation's zip code is within the scope of their calling.

That is what God has always required for missions. Not abundance. Obedience.

The budget follows the obedience. It has always been that way.
