The map in the missions conference has not changed. It still shows the Western hemisphere as the sending base and the Global South as the receiving field — the arrows running outward from North America and Europe toward Africa, Asia, and Latin America, carrying the gospel from places of relative plenty to places of relative need. The map is not wrong as an artifact of history. It is increasingly wrong as a description of reality.

The future of missions is not being planned in mission headquarters in Colorado Springs or London. It is already happening in Lagos and Seoul and São Paulo and Nairobi. The church that does not understand this is not only missing an extraordinary moment in the history of Christianity. It is organizing itself for a missionary role that the global church has largely moved past.

**The Numbers, First**

Christianity is not a declining religion. It is declining in Europe and North America, which have dominated the Western imagination of what Christianity looks like. It is expanding at rates without historical precedent in sub-Saharan Africa, across Latin America, in South Korea, in China, in Iran. [cite — confirm] The majority of the world's Christians now live in the Global South — in Africa, Latin America, and Asia. The majority of the world's church growth is happening in those regions. The majority of the world's missionaries being sent across cultural lines are now being sent by churches in those regions.

Nigeria sends missionaries to England. South Korea sends missionaries to the Middle East and Central Asia. Brazil sends missionaries to the Portuguese-speaking world and beyond. The Philippines sends missionaries across Southeast Asia. China, despite active state opposition to religious expression, has a domestic church of tens of millions and mission movements that are reaching ethnic minorities and neighboring nations. [cite — confirm]

The direction of the missionary flow has not simply shifted. It has inverted. The once-receiving field is now the sending base. The regions that European and North American missions regarded as the mission field a century ago are now the most vital missionary-sending communities in the world.

**What Made This Possible**

The Pentecostal movement, often traced to the Azusa Street Revival of 1906 in Los Angeles, carried a set of theological convictions about the immediacy of the Spirit's work that proved extraordinarily transferable across cultural contexts. The expectation of healing, of direct encounter with God, of a faith that was experiential and corporate and available to anyone regardless of literacy or institutional connection — these convictions resonated in communities where the distance between daily precarity and the need for divine intervention was not abstract. When the gospel arrived in the Global South through Pentecostal and later charismatic streams, it arrived in a form that engaged the immediate and visceral concerns of people whose lives demanded more than doctrinal instruction.

The African church also received and transformed the gospel through the lens of African theological categories — concepts of community, ancestors, the spiritual dimensions of physical life, the corporate nature of personhood — that European Christianity had largely suppressed or ignored. The church that emerged was not simply European Christianity in African dress. It was something genuinely new: a Christianity that had passed through African intellectual and spiritual tradition and emerged changed, carrying insights about the God of Scripture that the European tradition had not fully seen.

The same is true of the Korean church. The theology of *han* — the Korean concept of grief, injustice, and collective suffering that is simultaneously a wound and a source of resilience — produced a Christian spirituality of prayer and lament that the comfortable Western church had largely lost. Korean Christians pray differently because they have suffered differently. Their missionary enterprise, which is now one of the largest in the world per capita of Christian population, carries a theology forged in persecution, division, and the experience of a church that knows the cost of faith is real. **What the Western Church Is Missing**

The Western missionary enterprise has not processed the implications of this shift. Many mission organizations still operate on models developed in the colonial period — models that assume the Western church is the primary sending agency, that national churches are junior partners requiring supervision, that Western theological training is the credential that legitimates leadership. These models are not simply inefficient. They are theologically confused.

A church that insists on Western oversight of national leaders in countries where the church is larger, older in faith, and more deeply embedded in the culture than anything the Western mission organization can build — that church is practicing a patronizing arrangement it calls partnership but that the national church often experiences as control. The financial dependency that many mission relationships create — in which the national church's budget is underwritten by Western donors who therefore hold structural authority over its decisions — is not partnership. It is a funding arrangement with theological consequences.

The Western church also has much to receive from the global church that it has not been positioned to receive because it has organized itself as the sender. The church in the Global South has sustained faith under conditions of persecution, poverty, political instability, and social marginalization that the Western church has not faced in living memory. That sustained faith has produced theological resources — on prayer, on suffering, on community, on eschatological hope that is not merely theoretical — that the Western church badly needs.

I have been in rooms where Nigerian pastors spoke about prayer with a quality of expectation that made my own prayer practice feel anemic. I have heard Korean Christians describe fasting in ways that challenged my assumptions about what a spiritual discipline was for. I have read African theology that reframed questions I had inherited from Western systematic theology and revealed their cultural partiality. None of these are exotic curiosities. They are gifts the church has been trying to offer and the Western church has been too confident in its own frameworks to receive.

**What Partnership Actually Requires**

Genuine partnership — not the charity-and-control arrangement that has historically defined many mission relationships, but actual partnership — requires a willingness to enter the relationship as a learner as well as a contributor.

The Western church has financial resources the global church often lacks. Those resources are genuinely useful. But financial resources come with structural power, and that power without intentional accountability becomes dominance. The partner relationship must name that dynamic explicitly and build structures that prevent the financial reality from determining the theological agenda. The national church must own its mission. It must set its theological priorities without Western supervision. It must build its own leadership pipeline that does not require a Western institution to certify its validity.

This also means rethinking what Western missionaries do in contexts where the national church exists and is healthy. The role is not replacement — it is not the Western missionary pastoring the Nigerian church because he was sent with funding. The role, if there is one, is service: coming alongside a national church movement to offer specific capacities that are genuinely lacking, with full authority resting with the national leadership, with a clear endpoint, and with the conscious intention of building what is already there rather than building something parallel that the Western organization controls.

The model of the future is not the Western missionary to the Global South. It is the global church collaborating across its own regional and cultural contexts — Nigerians partnering with South Africans, Koreans partnering with Chinese, Brazilians partnering with Portuguese-speakers in Africa and Europe — with the Western church as one participant among many, learning to receive as well as to give, and discovering that the body of Christ is not organized around Western centers in the way it was organized a century ago.

**What the Pastor in Fenton Needs to Know**

I pastor a church in Fenton, Michigan. The world's mission does not feel immediate from here. But the global church is not abstract from Fenton. There are Nigerian families and South Korean families and families from the Philippines within an hour's drive of our church building. The global church is arriving in American cities and towns in the form of immigrants and refugees whose faith has been formed under conditions that the American church has not experienced. They are not mission objects. They are mission partners.

What they carry — the theology of suffering, the practice of prayer, the ecclesiology of community, the expectation of God's direct intervention in daily life — is something the American church needs and has often failed to recognize as such. The first move of a serious mission engagement in the current moment is not to organize another trip to the Global South. It is to sit down with the Nigerian pastor two towns over and to receive, with genuine humility, what he knows about following Christ that you do not.

The future of missions is already there. It has been there for some time. The question is whether the Western church is willing to relinquish enough of its self-appointed centrality to notice.
