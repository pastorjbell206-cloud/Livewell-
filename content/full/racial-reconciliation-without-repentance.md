The photographs from multi-ethnic Sunday services are real. The warmth in those rooms is real. No one who has experienced genuine worship across racial lines would dismiss what happens there. It is a glimpse — a real glimpse — of something the New Testament insists is central to the church's identity: that the dividing wall of hostility between peoples has been broken down in Christ (Ephesians 2:14). That is not a minor theological claim. Paul names it as the demonstration — the visible, material, social demonstration — that the powers of division and enmity have met something they could not overcome.

But a photograph of diversity is not reconciliation. A moment of warmth is not reconciliation. Reconciliation is a specific theological movement — from estrangement to restored relationship — and it has a specific requirement that the American church has largely tried to satisfy without meeting.

The requirement is repentance.

Not acknowledged wrongdoing. Not regret about how things turned out. Not the careful wording that expresses sadness without any agent who caused the sadness. Repentance — the Hebrew *shuv*, the Greek *metanoia* — is a turning. A turning away from the patterns that produced the estrangement, toward a different way of being that demonstrates in practice that something has actually changed. Without the turning, what you have is apology without amendment. The relationship does not change. The power arrangements do not change. The systems that produced the injury continue to produce it. Only the language about them has shifted.

And that, precisely, is the shape of what the American church has mostly offered.

The history is not disputed among historians who have examined it. American Christianity — Protestant and Catholic — has a documented record of institutional choices made consistently across centuries in the service of racial hierarchy: white churches that provided theological justification for chattel slavery, drawing on Scripture with a confidence matched only by their misuse of it; that remained largely silent during the lynching era, which took between 4,000 and 5,000 lives in the American South between 1877 and 1950 [cite — confirm these figures]; that organized opposition to school integration in the 1950s and 1960s in the name of biblical principle; that used the structures of religious affiliation to protect social arrangements that the prophets would have recognized — without difficulty — as the exploitation of the weak by the powerful. The documentation exists. It is not a polemical construction. It is the record.

The response to that record that the American church has most commonly attempted is reconciliation-without-turning. Diverse programming, cross-cultural preaching events, statements of acknowledgment, partnerships that are more symbolic than structural. Worship teams that reflect diversity. Guest preachers from communities that have been historically excluded. Language that expresses sorrow without specifying what the sorrow requires. These things are not nothing. They are not nothing. But they ask the people who were harmed to do the emotional labor of performing reconciliation for the benefit of the institution that harmed them, and they leave the institutional structures unchanged.

Genuine repentance would require something harder and more specific.

It would require white congregations to ask — honestly, without the retreat into abstraction that makes the question painless — what they have materially benefited from through the historical arrangements they now apologize for. The wealth that passed through hands kept in order by the threat of violence. The land. The educational advantage. The social standing. The ability to build equity while redlining prevented their neighbors from doing the same. These are not ancient grievances without contemporary relevance. They are the mechanisms by which the gap between white and Black family wealth in America came to be approximately ten to one, [cite — confirm current figures] a gap that was not produced by individual choices and cannot be closed by individual choices, because it was produced by coordinated institutional action over generations.

Acknowledging the gap and asking what restoration might require is not a political move. It is a theological move — it is what Zacchaeus did (Luke 19:8). It is what Leviticus 6 requires of the person who has defrauded another: restitution plus a fifth. It is what the Jubilee laws in Leviticus 25 were designed to accomplish at the structural level — the periodic correction of the accumulations of advantage that market economies reliably produce. The theology of restoration is not new. It is woven through the canon.

It would also require those same congregations to examine their present structures — not their histories but their present realities. Who holds power in this congregation? Who makes the decisions that determine the congregation's direction and resource allocation? Whose cultural preferences shape the worship, the programming, the aesthetics, the social norms? Whose comfort is protected when conflict about these things arises? Whose interpretation of the faith is treated as the default, requiring no defense, against which other interpretations must prove themselves?

These are not accusatory questions. They are diagnostic ones. A congregation that examines them honestly will often discover that the answer is, across the board: the same demographic that has historically held power in American institutions. That is not surprising. It is the predictable result of institutional history. What would be surprising is a congregation that saw this and responded with something other than defensive assertion that the current arrangement is simply the result of who showed up.

I am not writing about other churches. I am writing about my own failures in this territory, which are real. I have been in rooms where reconciliation language was fluent and structural change was absent. I have been part of conversations that produced warmth and produced nothing else. I have preached texts about unity across difference without examining the ways my own congregation's culture was organized around the comfort of people who were already comfortable there. I have called this hospitality when it was closer to invitation-without-change — the message that you are welcome to join what we have built and to find your place within it, rather than the message that your presence changes what we are.

That is not neutral. It is complicity in a milder register, and the mildness does not make it innocent. It makes it harder to see.

The New Testament's vision in Galatians 3:28 — neither Jew nor Greek, neither slave nor free, neither male nor female, all one in Christ — was not describing a feeling shared at a gathering. It was describing an economic and social reality that was, in the communities Paul was writing to, visibly and practically true: the wealthy sharing meals with the poor, the educated with the uneducated, the Jew with the Gentile, in ways that violated every social expectation of first-century Mediterranean culture. The church was scandalous precisely because the reconciliation was actual. It changed who sat at whose table. It changed who was treated as family. It changed the material arrangements of daily life.

That is the standard. Not a better photograph. An actual turning.

Willie James Jennings, in *The Christian Imagination*, argues that the failure of Western Christianity to produce genuine multiracial communion is not a peripheral failure — it is a failure at the center of the church's understanding of itself. [cite — confirm Jennings's argument] The church that produced racial hierarchy did not produce it despite its theology. It produced it by deploying a theology that separated the spiritual from the social, the interior from the structural, in ways that made the gospel compatible with any existing arrangement of power. That is the theological problem that reconciliation language without structural change reproduces in updated form.

The word for reconciliation that costs nothing is not reconciliation. The word for warmth without turning is not healing. The word for diverse photographs that cover unchanged power structures is the one already named: marketing.

Until the turning, the language is something other than what it claims to be. And the people who have been waiting for the turning — who have extended grace and good faith across decades of reconciliation conversations that produced warmth and produced nothing else — those people are not wrong to be skeptical. They have simply been paying attention to what the institutions have actually done, which is the only reliable guide to what the institutions actually intend.

What does actual turning look like? It looks like the congregation that examines its elder board and asks not "why don't more people of color want to lead here?" but "what about how we operate makes it difficult for people who are not from our culture to hold authority here, and what are we willing to change?" It looks like the denomination that moves from a statement acknowledging its history with slavery to a specific, material commitment — funded, accountable, with a timeline — to address the educational and economic disparities its predecessor churches helped create. It looks like the pastor who names from the pulpit that his tradition carries a specific debt, and invites his congregation into the discomfort of asking what payment looks like, rather than smoothing the discomfort in the interest of institutional peace.

These are not hypothetical demands. They are what repentance has always looked like. The Zacchaeus moment in Luke 19 did not require extended discernment about what justice asked. Zacchaeus announced, without prompting, that he would give half his possessions to the poor and repay fourfold anyone he had defrauded. The specificity was the repentance. Vague contrition is not a turning. It is the preparation for a turning that never arrives.

The church is not incapable of this. It has done harder things. The communities that organized the underground railroad, the congregations that defied the Fugitive Slave Act at risk to their members, the white ministers who marched across the Edmund Pettus Bridge and absorbed the consequences — these were real, costly, specific acts of institutional turning. [cite — confirm] They were insufficient to undo the whole history, but they were not nothing. They were the beginning of the kind of witness that produces credibility.

Repentance is specific. It names what it is turning from, describes what it is turning toward, and demonstrates the change in action. It is costly exactly in proportion to the harm it is addressing.

Nothing less will do what reconciliation requires. And anything less, however warmly expressed, is asking the people who were harmed to settle for the appearance of a thing they were promised and have not yet received.
