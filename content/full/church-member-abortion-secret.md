She has been sitting in your congregation for years. She sings on the worship team, or she volunteers in the nursery, or she teaches your daughter's Sunday school class. And she has never told anyone — cannot tell anyone — because the culture of the church has made it clear what the response would be.

The silence is not indifference. It is the most rational response to an environment that has taught her what the cost of honesty would be. She has watched what happens when people in her church are vulnerable about the wrong things. She has calibrated accordingly.

And so she carries it. Every pro-life Sunday, every political sermon, every casual conversation where someone says something breezy and devastating about women who have made the choice she made — she carries it alone, in the building that is supposed to be the place where no one has to carry anything alone.

## The Scene in John 8

The Gospel of John, chapter 8, records a scene that has never stopped being relevant. A woman caught in an act the law condemned is brought into the center of the room. The people around her are not conflicted. They have their stones ready. They have a clear position and they are prepared to act on it.

And Jesus bends down and writes in the dirt — a gesture whose content we are not told and whose significance we cannot reduce. Then he stands and offers something no one in the crowd was offering: the possibility that there is more to say about her than what she did.

"Neither do I condemn you," he says. And then: "Go and sin no more." Both sentences matter. In that order. The absence of condemnation comes before the call to change. The welcome precedes the instruction. The space to be human is opened before the moral direction is given.

This is not sentimentality. Jesus does not pretend that what she has done is nothing. The "go and sin no more" is as real as the "neither do I condemn you." But the sequence matters enormously, and the church has often reversed it — leading with the moral verdict and wondering why the people who most need the welcome never arrive.

## What the Culture of a Church Actually Communicates

Church culture is not what appears in the mission statement. It is what people learn by observation — what they hear casually said about others, what they notice when someone is vulnerable in the wrong way, what happens to people who confess the things the congregation is not prepared to receive.

A church culture that speaks casually and confidently about abortion — in sermons, in hallway conversation, in the tone of the bulletin inserts around election season — is communicating something to every woman in the room who is carrying this. It is telling her that her secret is not safe here. It is telling her that the thing she most needs to bring into the light is the thing most likely to get her expelled from the community. It is making the silence obligatory.

I have led church services where the political language was clear and the pastoral consequences were invisible to me. I did not see the women in the room for whom that language was not abstract. That is my failure to name, not someone else's.

The pastoral question is not whether the church has a conviction about abortion. The pastoral question is whether the church's expression of that conviction has made space for the people whose lives are the subject of the conviction — or whether it has made that space impossible.

## What Silence Does Over Time

The silence required by this kind of church culture does not stay contained. It spreads. When a person learns that one category of her life must be hidden from her community, she learns that the community is not actually safe. She brings less of herself over time. She maintains the surface performance of belonging — attendance, service, generosity — while the real person recedes behind the performance. Eventually the distance between the person and the performance becomes unsustainable. She leaves, and the congregation notices the departure without understanding the cause.

Or she stays, and the weight of the accumulated silence becomes the architecture of her spiritual life. Not a relationship with God conducted in honesty and freedom, but a relationship shaped by what she cannot say. The Gospel that was supposed to set her free has become, in practice, another form of management.

Neither of these is what the church is for.

## The Counter-Argument and Its Limits

There is an honest pastoral concern on the other side: that creating a culture of openness about abortion risks communicating that the church has no convictions about it. That if women can speak freely about their abortions in the congregation, the implicit message is that abortion is not a serious moral matter. That pastoral welcome and theological conviction cannot both be maximized, and that a church with strong pro-life convictions will inevitably create environments where women who have had abortions feel some tension.

This concern is not entirely without merit. Pastoral welcome cannot dissolve theological conviction without destroying the thing it is trying to protect. A church that communicates "your choices have no moral weight" has not offered grace; it has offered something smaller than the Gospel.

But the concern overreaches when it assumes that the only alternative to shame is approval. Jesus did not approve of the woman's conduct. He also did not stone her. The space between approval and condemnation is large, and it is in that space that genuine pastoral ministry lives. A church can hold a clear pro-life conviction and still be a place where the woman who has not lived up to that conviction can be honestly present, honestly grieved, honestly received.

What is required is not the abandonment of convictions. It is the abandonment of the casual cruelty — the offhand political comment, the breezy certainty, the congregation-wide declaration that this particular category of person is not safe to be honest here.

## What She Actually Needs

She does not need another sermon about abortion. She has heard them. They are part of what she carries.

She needs what every person in the room with a serious, shameful secret needs: evidence that the community can hold what she is holding. That the God who is described in Psalm 34:18 as "near to the brokenhearted" is actually accessible in this building, not only in the privacy of her car on the way home. That there is a pastor or a trusted person in the congregation who will not be destroyed by the information, who will not immediately become her confessor's liability, who knows the difference between receiving a person's story and administering a verdict on it.

This requires actual pastoral infrastructure — not a program, but a culture. A culture where hard confessions are received well. Where vulnerability is protected. Where the conversation that begins with "I need to tell you something" does not end with that person on the front of a prayer chain.

Creating that culture requires the pastor to be honest about his own limits. I am not always capable of being the person whose response does not wound. I have said things in response to confession that made the confession a mistake. That is the starting point for pastoral self-knowledge, not a disqualification from trying.

## The Gospel Has a Different Shape

A church culture where someone can confess the thing that is eating her alive — not to receive a lecture, not to receive a theology lesson, but to receive the thing the Gospel actually offers — is not a church that has abandoned its convictions. It is a church that has understood them well enough to practice them.

The thing the Gospel offers is not tolerance. It is the possibility of being fully known and not finally condemned. It is the removal of the necessity of the performance. It is the reality that God's knowledge of you precedes his judgment of you, and that what he does with that knowledge is something other than what the people with stones ready would do.

"There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus," Paul writes to the Romans (Romans 8:1). That sentence does not apply to everyone in the abstract. It applies to specific people in specific rooms carrying specific things. It applies to her, sitting in the third row, singing the words she means and carrying the thing she cannot say.

The question is whether the church she is sitting in has created the conditions under which she can discover that.

The question is not whether abortion is wrong. The question is whether the women who have had one are allowed to be human inside your building. If the answer is no, you have built a church for people who do not need one. The people who need it are the ones outside the gate, standing at a distance they did not choose, having learned that the cost of coming closer is too high.

They deserve better than that distance. So does the Gospel.

That weight does not resolve. It asks something.
