The question the lawyer asked is the one we all ask: "And who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29)

It sounds like a sincere request for clarification. It is not. The text tells us what it was: the lawyer, having already affirmed the two great commandments, "desiring to justify himself" asked the follow-up question. He was not confused about who his neighbor was. He was hoping for a definition narrow enough that he was already in compliance. A neighbor as someone who lives adjacent. A neighbor as someone who shares my ethnicity, my class, my religious tradition. A neighbor as someone I have explicitly chosen to be in relationship with. Any of these definitions would have left the man feeling satisfied that the law's demands on him were already met.

Jesus told a story instead of giving a definition. That is significant. A definition would have answered the question on the lawyer's terms. The story answers it on entirely different terms — and the terms matter more than the answer.

## The World the Parable Assumes

The road from Jerusalem to Jericho descended approximately 3,300 feet over seventeen miles through terrain that provided cover for bandits. [cite — confirm the geographical details] It was genuinely dangerous, genuinely isolated, a road where a man could be robbed and left for dead and no one would know for hours. The parable is not set in a hypothetical location. It is set on an actual road that every person in Jesus's audience would have walked or known.

The man who falls among robbers is unspecified. He has no name, no ethnicity, no religious identity. He is a person in need, and his need is total — he is stripped, beaten, and left half dead. He cannot advocate for himself. He cannot explain his background or establish his credentials. He is simply a body on the road, and the question of what his life is worth will be answered entirely by the choices of the next people who pass.

The first two who pass are not chosen arbitrarily. The priest and the Levite are figures of the highest religious standing in first-century Judaism — the ones most educated in the law's demands, most publicly associated with God's covenant community, most familiar with the texts that commanded care for the vulnerable. If anyone should have known what the law required, it was them. And they pass.

The reasons they pass are not given explicitly, which is more honest than explicit villainy would be. The priest may have been concerned with ritual purity — contact with a body that might be dead would have rendered him unable to perform his temple duties until he had completed a purification process that took time and cost social standing. The Levite may have calculated the same. These were not heartless men calculating self-interest crudely. They were people managing competing obligations within a religious system that had created categories that made the choice defensible.

That is the precise indictment. The religious system had generated categories of obligation that made it possible to step past a dying man while remaining, within the system's own logic, in compliance with the law. The priest did not violate a commandment by walking past. He honored the subset of commandments that governed priestly purity while violating the one that governed neighbor-love — and the system's categories made the first set visible and the second set invisible.

## The Samaritan and the Choice of the Protagonist

The Samaritan is not a random choice. In the ethnic and religious politics of first-century Judea, Samaritans and Jews maintained a hostility that was centuries old. The division traces to the Assyrian conquest and resettlement of the northern kingdom in 722 BC — when the Assyrians depopulated Israel and imported other peoples, the resulting mixed community developed a religious tradition that diverged from the Jerusalem tradition around the question of the correct place of worship (the Samaritans worshiped on Mount Gerizim), among other things. [cite — confirm the Assyrian resettlement details] By the first century, the hostility was embedded. Jews and Samaritans did not associate (John 4:9). The disciples were shocked when Jesus spoke to the Samaritan woman at the well.

When a Jew in Jesus's audience heard "Samaritan" they heard: the wrong kind of person. Not the enemy exactly — that word might carry respect along with hostility. The wrong kind. The theologically deficient, the racially compromised, the one whose very presence in the story signals that something is going to be overturned.

And then this wrong kind of person sees the injured man, and the Greek text says something worth pausing on: he was *splanchnizomai* — moved in his viscera (Luke 10:33). The English translations say "had compassion," which is accurate but too soft. The Greek word points to the gut, the internal organs, the physical sensation of a body responding before the mind has completed its calculation. He felt it in his body before he decided anything. And then he decided: he bound the wounds, poured oil and wine, loaded the man on his own animal — which meant he walked — brought him to an inn, paid for his care, and promised to return and cover any additional expenses.

Every detail is costly. Every detail is specific. The Samaritan did not feel sorry for the man and continue on his way. He crossed every category that would have justified walking past.

## What Jesus Dismantles

Jesus does not then ask: "Which of these three was the true neighbor?" He asks: "Which of these three do you think was a neighbor to the man who fell into the hands of robbers?" (Luke 10:36). The shift matters. The first question would have confirmed the lawyer's frame: here are the candidates, tell me which one qualifies as a "real" neighbor. The second question dismantles the frame entirely.

The answer is not about identity. It is about action. The neighbor is not a category applied to persons based on their prior relationship to you. The neighbor is constituted in the act of neighborliness. The question is not "who is my neighbor?" — who qualifies for my care based on prior designation. The question is "to whom was this man a neighbor?" — who performed the neighborly act. The category is produced by the action, not the other way around.

The lawyer cannot say "the Samaritan." He says "the one who showed mercy." He cannot even speak the name of the category he has spent his life avoiding. And Jesus says: go and do likewise.

Go and do likewise is not an affirmation that the lawyer has understood correctly and now needs to execute. It is a displacement. The lawyer came to justify himself. He leaves with a command that will not be completed until he has stopped calculating whether the people in front of him qualify for his concern and started asking what their need requires of him.

## The Structural Argument

The parable is not about individual charity. It is an argument about the structure of moral obligation.

The question "who is my neighbor?" assumes that neighborliness is a category I apply to some people and withhold from others on the basis of prior relationship or shared identity. The parable deconstructs that assumption entirely. The neighbor is not a category I apply. The neighbor is the one in front of me who needs. My obligation is not defined by who they are. It is defined by what they face and what I have.

The church has spent centuries finding manageable definitions of "neighbor." We draw the lines at national borders, at zip codes, at church membership, at doctrinal agreement. Each of these definitions does for us what the lawyer was trying to get Jesus to do: it narrows the circle of obligation to a size we can already satisfy. It converts the command into a status we already hold.

The parable does not authorize any of those definitions. It authorizes one criterion only: need, encountered, and what you did about it.

The road from Jerusalem to Jericho runs through every neighborhood, through every policy debate, through every demographic category we have erected to manage the distribution of our concern. The man lying on it has no name and no ethnicity because Jesus did not want us to be able to ask whether he qualified. He lies there specifically so that the question cannot be the lawyer's question.

## The Honest Accounting

I have walked past on the other side. Not dramatically. Not with any clear moment of decision. But I have organized my life, my time, my resources, and my attention in ways that kept me on a road where I would not encounter the man who had fallen among robbers. I have lived in places and moved through schedules that made the question "who is my neighbor?" effectively answerable as "people like me." That is not neutrality. That is a choice. The parable names it.

The priest and the Levite were not bad people. They were people whose system had given them permission to walk past without being bad people, and they took the permission. The church does the same thing when it constructs programs that address the needs of the poor from a safe institutional distance, when it organizes ministry to the vulnerable in ways that do not require the minister to become personally inconvenienced, when it can report on its service projects without any individual member having had their life genuinely disrupted by the encounter.

The Samaritan's care cost him his animal, his time, his money, and his promise of return. Not proportionally. Not strategically. Not within a budget allocation for community service. He gave what the situation required until the situation did not require any more. That is the model.

The boundary Jesus will not let the lawyer draw is the one we keep trying to draw. Every generation finds its version of the priest-and-Levite calculation — the theological framework, the institutional structure, the political alliance, the cultural preference that makes the walk-past defensible. Every generation produces its version of the injured man on the road, stripped by systems that left him without protection and without advocate. And every generation has to answer the question that is not really the lawyer's question at all.

To whom was this man a neighbor?

Go and do likewise.

The road is still the same road.
