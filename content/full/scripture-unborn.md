The Bible does not contain the word abortion. It also does not contain a direct prohibition of it. These two facts are used — badly — by people on both sides of the argument, usually to prove more than they actually establish.

The person who says "the Bible doesn't mention abortion, so it has no view on it" has committed a category error. The Bible does not mention nuclear weapons either. The absence of a term does not constitute a theological vacancy on the subject the term addresses. The question is whether the Bible's settled convictions about human dignity, the nature of persons, and the character of God have bearing on the question — and they do.

The person who presents Psalm 139 as a proof text that ends the conversation has committed a different error. The text is real, its implications are serious, and a careful reading of it bears directly on any theology of unborn life. But turning a poem about divine intimacy into a constitutional argument requires more exegetical work than most invocations of the passage actually perform.

Both errors are worth naming, because the conversation deserves better than either.

## What Psalm 139 Actually Claims

Psalm 139:13-16 is the passage most frequently cited in this conversation, and it deserves a careful reading rather than a rapid deployment. "For you created my inward parts; you knitted me together in my mother's womb. I praise you because I am fearfully and wonderfully made; your works are wonderful, I know that full well. My frame was not hidden from you when I was made in the secret place, when I was woven together in the depths of the earth. Your eyes saw my unformed body; all the days ordained for me were written in your book before one of them came to be."

This is a theological claim, not a biological one. The psalmist is not describing fetal development; he is describing God's knowledge and care. The point is not that the fetus has a certain developmental status — it is that God is present and active in the formation of a person from the earliest moment. The phrase "your eyes saw my unformed body" — the Hebrew golem, a word used nowhere else in Scripture — refers to the unformed substance, the raw material of a person before the form is complete. God sees it. God is there. God is not indifferent.

That is a claim with enormous implications. If God is present and purposeful in the formation of the unborn person, that person is not nothing. The dignity of the unborn is not dependent on any particular developmental threshold — it is grounded in God's knowledge of and presence with the person from the beginning.

What the passage does not establish is a specific legal framework, a particular definition of personhood, or a direct prohibition of abortion. Those applications require more argument than the passage itself supplies. But the passage supplies the foundation that makes the argument theologically serious, and dismissing it as a mere poem is not honest interpretation.

## Jeremiah and John

Jeremiah 1:5 records God's word to the prophet: "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you, before you were born I set you apart; I appointed you as a prophet to the nations." The argument from this text for fetal personhood is frequently made, and it is frequently made badly. Jeremiah 1:5 is not, primarily, a general statement about when personhood begins. It is a specific claim about God's election of a prophet — a claim about divine calling that extends before birth and before conception.

But the text does resist a casual dismissal. If God's knowledge of and purpose for a person extends before formation in the womb, the argument that the unborn person has no standing before God requires a separate and much harder case to make. The text does not prove what its advocates most often claim it proves. It does suggest that the theological terrain here is not as simple as either side's preferred position assumes.

Luke 1:41-44 presents a different kind of evidence. "When Elizabeth heard Mary's greeting, the baby leaped in her womb, and Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit." Luke, a physician [cite — confirm], records John the Baptist responding to the presence of the incarnate Jesus while both are still in utero. Luke includes this detail without apology. Whether John's response is described in terms that require full personhood is debated; what is not debated is that Luke presents the encounter as spiritually significant, not as the interaction of two masses of tissue.

## The Exodus 21 Problem

The text that honest interpreters must reckon with — and that is almost always avoided on the pro-life side of the argument — is Exodus 21:22-25. The text describes the penalties for striking a pregnant woman. The rendering matters enormously here, because the Hebrew and its translation have been contested for centuries.

Some translations render the passage such that causing a premature birth results in a fine, while causing the woman's death results in "life for life" — suggesting a distinction in legal standing between the woman and the unborn child. Other translations read the passage as describing two outcomes: premature birth that results in no permanent injury (fine), and injury that results in death — of the woman, the child, or both — invoking the lex talionis.

Honest interpreters acknowledge that this text is genuinely disputed and that reading it as a settled proof text in either direction is overconfident. The Septuagint — the Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures — renders the passage differently from the Masoretic text, and early Christian interpreters disagreed significantly about its implications. A person who says "Exodus 21 proves that the Bible does not regard the unborn as fully human" and a person who says "Exodus 21 proves nothing of the kind" are both running ahead of what the text can bear.

## The Counter-Position That Wants an Answer

The honest counter-position is this: the passages above are all theologically poetry, prophetic calling, or narrative — none of them is the kind of legal or moral code that directly addresses the question. And in the absence of direct scriptural address, the tradition of the church has no uniform position to point to. The early church debated animation — when the soul entered the body — and different traditions set that threshold at different points [cite — confirm]. Aquinas, drawing on Aristotle, held a position on delayed ensoulment that is significantly different from the position now held by the Catholic Church [cite — confirm]. The claim that Scripture speaks with a single clear voice on the beginning of personhood is harder to sustain historically than its proponents suggest.

This is a serious argument. The history of Christian thought on fetal personhood is more complex than contemporary debates acknowledge. The confident claim that "the Bible is clear" on the exact moment personhood begins is not supported by the actual diversity of Christian reflection on the question.

What the counter-position does not establish is indifference. The complexity of the question does not empty it of moral weight. It means the answer requires careful argument — not proof-texting, not confident dismissal, but the kind of careful theological reasoning that takes the full biblical witness seriously, including the passages that resist easy deployment.

## What Scripture Actually Establishes

What Scripture establishes clearly is this: the unborn person is not nothing. The image of God is not something conferred at birth like a certificate. It is borne from the beginning, or it is not the image of God.

The God of the Old Testament knows persons before they are formed. The God of the New Testament works through persons before they are born. The biblical imagination does not treat the womb as a morally neutral space or the unborn as a category beneath theological concern.

What Scripture does not establish, with the same clarity, is every application of that conviction. The threshold of legal personhood, the specific circumstances under which the competing claims of the woman and the unborn should be adjudicated, the exact developmental moment at which all the implications of the Imago Dei attach — these applications require wisdom, context, and the kind of careful reasoning that proof-texting shortcuts.

The church owes the people in its pews — and the people watching from a distance, deciding whether the church is worth trusting — an honest account of what Scripture actually establishes versus what has been claimed in Scripture's name. The conflation of those two things has produced both bad theology and bad pastoral practice: people who abandon a conviction because it was overstated, and people who defend a position with more certainty than the texts actually warrant. Both failures damage the people the argument is meant to serve.

The conviction is biblical. Some of the certainty claimed in its name is not. Knowing the difference between those two things is not an act of theological weakness. It is what honest interpretation requires.

And honest interpretation is the only kind that can actually serve the people whose lives depend on getting this right.
