The Protestant Reformation is one of the most consequential events in Western history — religious, cultural, political, economic. It is also one of the most misunderstood by the people who claim its inheritance, including me for many years. We tend to remember it as a clean story: a corrupt church, a brave monk, a door, and the recovery of the gospel. The real thing was richer than that, and more double-edged.

What the Reformation actually recovered was real, and worth recovering. At its center was a question that had been buried under centuries of institutional accretion: how does a guilty person stand before a holy God? The answer the Reformers pressed — *sola gratia, sola fide*, justification by grace through faith alone — was not a novelty. It was an excavation. It was Paul's argument in Romans 3–5, the same Paul who had been read for a thousand years but read through the interpretive grid of a system that had made merit, indulgence, and ecclesiastical mediation the practical mechanisms of salvation. Martin Luther's break in 1517 was not primarily about corruption, though the corruption was real enough. It was about whether the gospel itself had been recovered or replaced. Alongside justification came a second conviction that changed everything about the structure of Christian life: *sola scriptura*, the authority of Scripture alone over the tradition of the institution. This meant the ordinary believer, reading a Bible in his own language, possessed a source of authority that outranked the pronouncements of councils and popes. William Tyndale's insistence on producing an English New Testament — which got him strangled and burned in 1536 [cite — confirm] — was the logical consequence of Luther's premise. If Scripture is the final authority, then every person who can read Scripture has access to that authority. The priesthood of all believers was not a metaphor. It was a restructuring of the whole economy of grace.

That much, I will defend. The recovery of grace is not a sixteenth-century opinion. It is the gospel, and the church needed it back.

But here is where the triumphal Protestant account stops short, and where honesty requires going further. The Reformation set loose something it did not fully intend, and we are still living inside it.

When it placed the Bible in every hand and made the individual conscience the seat of authority before God, it also removed the institutional mechanism that had kept the church — for all its failures — in one room. The principle that each believer could read Scripture and discern truth carried no built-in brake. Luther intended the principle to free Christians from institutional error; he did not intend it to produce a Christianity in which every disagreement became grounds for separation. But ideas outrun their authors, and within Luther's own lifetime the principle had already produced the Peasants' War, the Anabaptist movement, and a fractured German church that horrified him. By 1527 he was writing against the very sectarianism his theology had made possible. [cite — confirm]

The dividing did not stop. Lutheran from Reformed, Reformed from Arminian, Arminian from Baptist, Baptist from Baptist, until the present condition of tens of thousands of Christian denominations, each certain it has read the text rightly, each occupying a different room and often treating the other rooms with polite incomprehension or open suspicion. The specific count is contested [cite — confirm], but the phenomenon is not. Protestantism is a tradition with a magnificent gift for biblical seriousness and a chronic inability to stay together.

This is the thing the Reformation missed, or rather the thing it could not prevent: it recovered grace and underprovided for unity. It was strong on *truth against corruption* and weak on *how the body holds together once everyone can read for himself*. The assumption that the conscience, properly illuminated by Scripture, would converge on the truth underestimated the extent to which the conscience, however illuminated, is also a product of history, culture, class, and the particular community that has shaped it. Two equally serious, equally Spirit-filled readers of Ephesians have arrived at incompatible conclusions about baptism for five hundred years. Scripture alone did not resolve it.

Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age*, traces the way the Reformation's turn toward the individual conscience contributed over centuries to the disenchantment of the public world — the slow withdrawal of a shared sacred order from the space of common life. [cite — confirm Taylor's argument here] That is a long argument and a contested one, but the directional claim seems right: when the final authority is relocated from the institution to the individual, not just in religion but eventually in every domain of meaning, the public world loses the capacity to hold shared conviction. The Reformation did not cause Western secularism by itself. But it prepared the ground in ways its architects did not foresee.

The counter-argument deserves full weight. The medieval church whose unity the Reformation broke was not a benign institution that merely had some corruption at the edges. It was an institution that had used its monopoly on spiritual access to accumulate land, power, and wealth; that had burned people it disagreed with; that had produced the Crusades and the Inquisition along with the cathedrals and the care of the poor. Hilaire Belloc's romantic picture of medieval Catholic unity, popular in some quarters, sanitizes a record that included spectacular institutional cruelty. The Reformers were not breaking something that was whole. They were breaking something that had already broken itself in more fundamental ways. The question is not whether the break was justified. It is whether what followed was the only possible consequence of it.

I do not think it was inevitable. I think it was a consequence of a particular answer to the question of authority — an answer that placed the individual above the community in matters of final discernment — and that a different answer might have produced a different history. But I am not making an argument for returning to Rome. I am making an argument for honesty about our own inheritance.

The Protestant who has never reckoned seriously with the ecclesial cost of his founding tradition — who celebrates *sola scriptura* without examining the five hundred years of division it enabled — is not reading his own history. And the Protestant who encounters a challenge to his conclusions and reaches for the founding principle, insisting that his reading of Scripture is the plain one and everyone else has been corrupted by tradition, is replaying the same move that has produced every subsequent division. He is doing exactly what Luther did, and what every schismatic from Luther to the present has done, with the same certainty and often with the same blindness about what the move costs.

There is something admirable in the Reformation's refusal to let institutional authority silence the truth. Bonhoeffer's willingness to stand against the German state church in the 1930s — which had accommodated itself to National Socialism and required theological justification for doing so — was only possible because his tradition had given him the tools to say: the institution does not determine the gospel, and when the institution contradicts the gospel, the gospel wins. [cite — confirm Bonhoeffer's theological framework here] That is the Reformation's gift, and it is not a small gift.

But Bonhoeffer also knew that the church could not survive as a community of individuals each reading Scripture alone. The Confessing Church he helped organize was, among other things, an attempt to recover a form of ecclesial accountability — a community of discernment that stood between the solitary conscience and the corrupt institution, holding both. His book *Life Together*, written in 1938 [cite — confirm] at the Finkenwalde seminary, is a sustained argument that Christian life cannot be lived alone, that the solitary Christian and the community of Christians need each other in ways that neither can fully substitute for.

That is the recovery the Reformation left incomplete. Not the recovery of grace — that it accomplished. The recovery of what it means to be the body of Christ together, across difference, across time, across the endless temptation to separate from the person whose conclusions we find difficult. The Reformation gave us the Bible in every hand and the conscience free before God. It did not give us — could not give us, because of the logic of its own founding — a robust account of why the church that disagrees with itself is more faithful than the church that keeps fragmenting until the disagreement disappears.

We are the inheritors of both halves of that legacy. The recovery of grace belongs to us, and we should not surrender it. The fragmentation belongs to us too, and we should not pretend it is a virtue. A movement can recover something essential and lose something essential at the same time, and the faithful response is not to choose a half. It is to hold the recovery of grace with gratitude, and to take seriously the wound the recovery left — to be Protestants who still ache for the unity our own founding made harder, who refuse to treat our endless dividing as though it were a mark of faithfulness rather than a cost, and who labor toward the thing that Jesus prayed for on the last night before they killed him: that they might be one.

The Reformation was about grace, and Scripture, and the conscience set free before God. It was also about a fracture that has not healed. Own both.

The first is a gift you should not surrender. The second is a debt the church is still paying — and pretending otherwise is how we keep paying it.
