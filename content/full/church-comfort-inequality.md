The American church has made peace with a level of economic inequality that the prophets would have named without hesitation. We have not named it. We have organized our congregations around it, built our worship experiences for the people who benefit from it, and developed a theology that, if not actively defending it, at least refuses to disturb it.

This requires explanation, because the Scriptures do not cooperate.

---

Amos was not a radical. He was a farmer from Tekoa, a southern shepherd with no particular interest in politics. God sent him north to Israel during the reign of Jeroboam II in the eighth century BC [cite — confirm] — a period of relative prosperity in which, as Amos describes it, the wealthy were selling the poor for a pair of sandals and trampling the heads of the poor into the dust of the earth (Amos 2:6-7). The surface of Israelite society in this period was impressive: military expansion, economic growth, religious observance, functioning institutions. Beneath the surface, the poor were being consumed by those who ran the system.

Amos did not argue that poverty was regrettable or that the wealthy should be more generous with their surplus. He argued that God would not forget what was being done. That the worship of a people who oppressed the poor was an offense to God — that the incense and the music and the offerings were not only worthless but actively disgusting to the God who had heard the cries of the poor. "I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies. Even though you offer me your burnt offerings and grain offerings, I will not accept them; and the peace offerings of your fattened animals, I will not look upon them. Take away from me the noise of your songs; to the melody of your harps I will not listen. But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:21-24).

The question is why the contemporary church reads Amos and concludes that his indictment was for a different era, a different people, a different arrangement.

---

I have been comfortable with inequality. I have preached in buildings that cost more than the median household income in my county, to congregations whose combined assets were significant, about a God whose concern for the poor I named without implicating myself or the community I was leading in the structures that perpetuate poverty. I have preached sermons about generosity that were, functionally, invitations to give a little more from the surplus while the arrangements producing the surplus went unexamined. This is a kind of homiletical cowardice I have practiced long enough that I mistook it for pastoral wisdom.

The early church was not comfortable with inequality. Acts 2 and Acts 4 describe a community where "no one said that any of the things that belonged to him was his own, but they had everything in common" (Acts 4:32), where "distribution was made to each as any had need" (Acts 2:45). The interpreters who spend most of their energy arguing that this was not communism and was not prescriptive for all time are, in one sense, correct — and in another sense, missing the point. The early community's economic practice was not a political program. It was the community's natural response to the resurrection — the recognition that if death has been defeated, then the anxious accumulation of security against death has also been defeated, and something different is possible. The economic life of the community followed from the theology of the community.

The point is not that every church must hold all property in common. The point is that the early community's ease with radical redistribution across class lines was not felt as an exceptional demand. It was felt as the obvious implication of the gospel they had received. They had not yet developed the theological apparatus required to make it optional.

---

By the fourth century, when Constantine had made Christianity safe and then powerful, the church's relationship to wealth had changed substantially. John Chrysostom, Bishop of Constantinople in the late fourth century, preached against the wealth of the church with a ferocity that embarrasses most contemporary preachers [cite — confirm]. He called the person who has more than they need and does not give it away not a person who falls short of the ideal — a thief. The comparison was not rhetorical excess. It was a careful theological argument: if the goods of the earth belong to God and are given for the use of all human beings, then keeping what you do not need while others starve is a form of taking what does not belong to you.

Chrysostom was not considered a radical voice from outside the tradition. He was considered orthodox, standing squarely in the trajectory of the prophets and the apostles. The tradition that now treats prophetic challenge to wealth as politically motivated overreach has simply forgotten where it came from.

What changed was not the theology but the sociology. A church that is powerful, that owns property, that has wealthy benefactors whose giving sustains its institutional life, has structural incentives to develop a theology that protects those relationships. The prophetic edge gets dulled not through a single decision to compromise but through a thousand small decisions not to go quite that far this week — not to challenge this arrangement, not to name this exploitation, not to ask the question that would make the important donor uncomfortable.

---

What is the theological justification for the church's comfort with inequality? Several have been proposed, and they deserve honest engagement rather than dismissal.

The prosperity tradition argues that wealth is evidence of divine blessing — that God rewards faithfulness with material prosperity and that the wealthy have, in some sense, earned their position through virtue or effort. This theology has deep roots in American Protestantism, particularly in its Calvinist strains, where the doctrine of election was gradually connected to the sociological observation that the elect seemed disproportionately to flourish economically. The prosperity gospel of the twenty-first century is not an aberration of this tradition. It is its logical conclusion.

The biblical response to this argument is not gentle. Amos preached to a prosperous society. He did not take its prosperity as evidence of divine approval. Jesus warned that it is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God (Matthew 19:24) — a warning addressed to disciples who had just watched a wealthy man walk away from discipleship rather than relinquish his wealth. The disciples' response was astonishment: "Who then can be saved?" Their astonishment reveals that they shared the prosperity assumption that wealth was a sign of divine favor. Jesus challenged the assumption directly.

A second justification appeals to Matthew 26:11: "You will always have the poor among you." This verse is used with some frequency to suggest that poverty is a permanent and therefore acceptable feature of human society — that Jesus himself acknowledged that poverty cannot be eliminated and therefore should not be treated as a justice crisis requiring structural response. This reading requires ignoring the fact that Jesus is quoting Deuteronomy 15:11 — a passage that, in its original context, commands generous provision for the poor precisely because the poor will always be present. "For there will never cease to be poor in the land. Therefore I command you, 'You shall open wide your hand to your brother, to the needy and to the poor, in your land.'" The text Jesus cites is a mandate for ongoing obligation, not a license for indifference.

---

The more honest account of the American church's comfort with inequality is not theological but institutional and sociological. The church's financial survival depends on the generosity of its wealthiest members. The twenty percent of givers who provide the largest proportion of any congregation's budget tend to be people for whom the full prophetic tradition on wealth is most costly. A pastor who preaches Amos 5 and Micah 3 and James 5 with genuine force is preaching to the people whose giving determines whether the staff are paid next month.

This is not a reason to avoid the preaching. It is a reason to understand why the avoidance is so persistent and so rationalized. The preacher is not primarily a coward. He is a person embedded in institutional structures with real survival pressures, making the small accommodations that those pressures demand, and calling the accumulation of those accommodations prudence.

I have called it prudence. I am less convinced than I used to be.

The God announced by the prophets is not prudent in this sense. He does not adjust his requirements to the financial health of the institutions that bear his name. He does not evaluate the charge against Israel on the basis of whether naming it will disrupt the temple's revenue. He says to Amos — a farmer, with no institutional platform and nothing to protect — go to the capital and say the thing. And Amos goes.

---

The feast Isaiah describes in chapter 25 — the feast on this mountain for all peoples, rich food and well-aged wine, the best things, the things that the wealthy monopolize in every current arrangement — is not reserved for those who have succeeded under existing economic conditions. It is the feast that puts an end to existing conditions. "He will swallow up death forever; and the Lord God will wipe away tears from all faces, and the reproach of his people he will take away from all the earth" (Isaiah 25:8). The reproach of his people. The shame that comes from powerlessness, from being sorted into the category of those who do not count, from having the world's arrangements consistently demonstrate that you are expendable.

That shame is swallowed. That death is ended. That arrangement is over.

The church that has made peace with inequality has not yet made peace with the God who announced this feast. It has substituted a more manageable deity — one who blesses prosperity and asks for gratitude, rather than one who asks what you are doing with what he gave you and why the people at the bottom of your economy are still there. The comfortable church has a comfortable God, and the comfortable God is not the one the prophets announced.

The God of Amos and Isaiah and the early church is still pressing the charge. The mountains are still the witnesses. The arrangement being judged is still the arrangement in which we are deeply, institutionally, complicitly comfortable.

The church's comfort with inequality does not require explanation in the sense that it is hard to understand how it happened. The explanation is obvious. What it requires is the kind of explanation that is also a confession — the acknowledgment that we have organized our institutional life around the very arrangements that the God we claim to follow has consistently, unambiguously named as incompatible with his character.

The Scriptures are more uncomfortable than we have allowed them to be. We have allowed them to be less uncomfortable because the alternative is costly. The prophets did not find a way around that cost. Neither did the God who sent them.
