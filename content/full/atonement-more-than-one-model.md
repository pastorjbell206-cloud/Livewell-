The cross of Christ is the center of the Christian faith. Every tradition affirms this. But the question of what the cross actually accomplished — the theology of the atonement — is one of the areas where the Christian tradition has produced not one answer but several, and where the tendency to treat one model as the whole truth has consistently impoverished the church's understanding.

The fights over that question have been fierce. The mistake in most of those fights is the assumption that there must be one model, and that to hold one is to reject the others. The New Testament does not hold one. It reaches for many, and means all of them.

## What Scripture Actually Says

Consider the images Scripture itself uses. The cross is a *ransom* — a price paid to free those held captive (Mark 10:45). It is a *victory* — Christ disarming the principalities and powers and triumphing over them publicly (Colossians 2:15). It is a *sacrifice* — the Lamb of God, the once-for-all offering that the entire sacrificial system of Israel was anticipating and straining toward (Hebrews 9-10). It is *substitution* — the righteous for the unrighteous, the one who knew no sin being made sin so that we might become the righteousness of God (Isaiah 53:5; 2 Corinthians 5:21; 1 Peter 3:18). It is *reconciliation* — enemies made friends, the far brought near, the dividing wall broken down (Romans 5:10-11; Ephesians 2:13-16). It is *moral transformation* — the love of God displayed so that beholding it might break and remake the human heart (Romans 5:8; 1 John 4:10).

These are not competing descriptions of different possible crosses. They are multiple accounts of the same event, each emphasizing a different dimension of what God accomplished when the Son died. The ransom image addresses captivity. The victory image addresses the power of death and the principalities. The sacrifice image addresses the Old Testament's entire sacrificial economy and its fulfillment. The substitution image addresses guilt and the righteous requirements of a holy God. The reconciliation image addresses the broken relationship between humanity and God. Each account is accurate. Each account is partial. Together they begin to approach the scale of what happened.

## The History of the Models

The tradition gave these images theological precision in successive periods. The early church spoke extensively of *Christus Victor* — the cross as God's decisive defeat of sin, death, and the demonic powers that had held humanity in bondage. Gregory of Nyssa in the fourth century, and Irenaeus before him, developed this theme: the resurrection was the proof that death had been defeated at its own site [cite — confirm; Irenaeus *Against Heresies*, Gregory of Nyssa *Catechetical Oration*]. Anselm of Canterbury, writing in the late eleventh century, reframed the question in terms of medieval feudal honor and produced the *satisfaction* theory: sin is a violation of God's honor, and the debt incurred is one that human beings can neither quantify nor pay, but which God himself in Christ both satisfies and accepts [cite — confirm; Anselm, *Cur Deus Homo*, c. 1098]. The Protestant Reformers sharpened the substitution language into *penal substitution* — Christ bearing the legal penalty that justice required in our place, absorbing the wrath that our sin had accrued.

In the nineteenth century, P.T. Forsyth and others emphasized the *moral influence* theory: the cross is primarily a revelation of God's love, and it is the power of that revelation — the sheer, incomprehensible fact that God would go to such lengths — that transforms the human will and makes new life possible [cite — confirm; Forsyth, *The Cruciality of the Cross*, 1909].

Each of these accounts was developed in a particular historical moment, with a particular set of philosophical tools, addressing a particular set of pastoral and intellectual pressures. Anselm was working in a feudal world where the categories of honor and debt were immediately comprehensible to his congregation. The Reformers were working against a system of indulgences and earned merit that had come to obscure the radical gratuity of grace. Each framework illuminated something true about the cross. Each was also shaped by the available vocabulary of its era.

## The Error That Does the Most Damage

The problem is when one framework is elevated to the status of *the* Gospel and the others are treated as optional at best and suspect at worst.

Whole theological traditions have done this. Some have made penal substitution the entire account of the atonement — the courtroom image, the legal penalty, the substituted liability — and grown suspicious of anyone who speaks primarily of victory or moral transformation, as though leading with those images were a sign of softness on the cross. Others have recoiled from substitution entirely, embarrassed by its legal logic, uncomfortable with the notion of divine wrath, and in their recoil have set aside what Isaiah 53 and Romans 3 and 2 Corinthians 5 plainly say: that Christ took our place, bore what we deserved, became the thing that stood between us and the righteous requirement of a holy God.

Both errors make the same move: they take a true image, declare it the only image, and produce a distortion by amputation. The cross with only the courtroom is accurate but cold — it explains the forgiveness but cannot as easily account for why death itself was conquered, why creation is being restored, why the resurrection means what Paul says it means in Romans 8. The cross with only moral transformation may be warm but it cannot carry the weight of Isaiah 53 and the Passover lamb and the Day of Atonement — it explains God's love but leaves the question of what to do with human guilt and divine holiness unanswered.

I have argued these corners myself, with more heat than light. I defended one model as though the cross would collapse without my narrowness, and treated brothers who emphasized a different image as though they were softening the Gospel. What I was defending, most of the time, was the vocabulary I had been formed in — the tribal shibboleths of my tradition — not the fullness of what God actually did at Calvary. The cross did not need my defense. It needed me to grow large enough to hold what it contains.

## Why Multiple Models Are Not Theological Weakness

There is a temptation to read the plurality of atonement models as theological confusion — as though the church's inability to agree on one account is an embarrassment that more careful thinking could resolve. That reading is wrong in two ways.

First, it assumes that the reality of the cross is simple enough to be captured by one human image. It is not. The cross is the moment at which the eternal Son of God dies as a human being for the sin of the world, satisfying divine justice, defeating death and the powers, restoring the broken relationship between God and his image-bearers, and demonstrating a love large enough to include enemies. That is not a simple thing. The multiple models do not reflect theological failure. They reflect the inadequacy of any single human metaphor to carry the full weight of what God did.

Second, it ignores the fact that the New Testament itself deploys multiple models within the same letters, sometimes within the same argument. Romans 3-8 contains forensic justification language, sacrificial language, the language of liberation from bondage, and the language of participation in Christ's death and resurrection — not as competing accounts but as multiple angles on the same reality, each adding to the picture that the others cannot fully carry alone.

## What This Demands of Preaching

The practical implication for preaching is not that every sermon must cover every theory of the atonement. It is that the preacher who knows only one model will eventually preach that model as though the whole cross were contained in it, and the congregation will receive a smaller cross than Scripture proclaims.

The congregation needs to hear substitution because they need to know that their guilt has been addressed, that the righteous requirement has been met, that there is no remaining debt. They need to hear victory because they need to know that the forces that hold them — addiction, despair, the powers that operate through systems and structures — have been defeated at the cross and are operating in borrowed time. They need to hear reconciliation because they need to know that the relationship itself has been repaired, that they are not merely forgiven from a distance but brought near, made sons and daughters rather than enemies. They need to hear the revelation of love because they need to see, clearly and without mitigation, what God was willing to do for them.

None of these is the whole cross. All of them are true. The preacher who can hold them together — who can move between the courtroom and the battlefield and the altar and the embrace — is the preacher whose congregation is receiving what the New Testament actually says.

The cross is the center. It is also bigger than the center you were taught to stand on. Stand there still — the cross does not shrink from precision — but turn around, and see how much more of it there is.
