Socrates said the unexamined life is not worth living. He was speaking in philosophical terms — the life held up against questions of virtue, the good, the nature of justice and the soul [cite — confirm]. The line comes from the *Apology*, his defense at his trial, where he argued that a life spent in the pursuit of wisdom was the only life worth defending. His accusers disagreed. They gave him hemlock. The examined life is not, it turns out, always safe.

The church inherited this instinct through Augustine, who turned it inward in a way that Socrates never quite did: not simply the life examined for virtue, but the soul examined in the presence of God, held against what is actually true about the human condition and what is actually true about the one who made it. The *Confessions* are precisely this — not an autobiography but a sustained act of self-examination conducted in direct address to God. "Our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" is the first great sentence, and the entire volume that follows is the account of a man discovering, through painful examination, what the restlessness was actually about [cite — confirm].

Augustine does not flatter himself. He does not curate his failures for dramatic effect. He looks at what was actually happening in his interior life — the adolescent theft of pears that was not about the pears, the concubinage he could not leave, the ambition he dressed as philosophy — names each with precision, and holds it before God without flinching. The *Confessions* are not a genre exercise. They are the result of a man who believed that knowing himself honestly before God was not separate from knowing God.

The pastoral vocation requires this. Not as a personality preference or a temperamental gift, but as a structural necessity. The pastor who stands in a pulpit every week and speaks about sin, grace, pride, and self-deception had better have some actual experience of examining his own interior. Otherwise the sermon is not prophetic — it is merely critical. It has the form of truth-telling without the weight of someone who has actually looked at himself and been undone by what he found.

## What Happens Without It

I have been in that pulpit. I know what it feels like to preach about pride from a defensive position, to speak about self-deception while practicing it with considerable skill. The congregation sensed something, even if they could not name it. What they were sensing was the absence of self-implication — a sermon that pointed outward with precision but never turned inward with honesty.

The pastor who does not examine himself will, over time, project his unexamined material onto the congregation. The sermon about anger that comes most often from the pastor who is himself angry and will not look at it. The sermon about ambition that is actually about the speaker's own ambition, but preached as if from the outside looking in. The call for confession that does not sound like it comes from a man who confesses, but from one who requires it from others.

This is not a minor aesthetic problem. The congregation needs a pastor who has looked at himself and not run from what he saw. They are living lives that include darkness, failure, and self-deception. When they sit across from a pastor in a moment of genuine crisis and he is practicing concealment — even unconscious concealment — they sense it. The help they needed was the help of someone who has been honest with God about his own darkness. What they are getting instead is advice from a managed presentation.

Paul understood this. His letters are full of self-implication that is not performed vulnerability. "I do not do the good I want to do, but the evil I do not want to do — this I keep on doing" (Romans 7:19). Whether this is autobiographical in the psychological sense or pedagogical in the theological sense, it is not the voice of a man pointing at the congregation from a safe position. He is inside the indictment. The teaching carries weight because the teacher has not exempted himself.

## The Biblical Pattern

David's Psalms are, among other things, a practice of examined living before God. Psalm 51 — written, in the canonical framing, after Nathan confronted him about Bathsheba and Uriah — is a sustained act of interior examination: "For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me" (Psalm 51:3). The phrase "my sin is ever before me" is not merely forensic. It is descriptive of a man who has stopped looking away. The confrontation Nathan initiated forced an examination David had been avoiding. The psalm is what happened when he stopped avoiding it.

Psalm 139 is where the examined life becomes explicitly theological: "You have searched me and known me" (Psalm 139:1). The question is not whether God sees the interior life accurately. God's knowledge is total and prior. The question is whether the pastor himself is willing to look at what God sees. "Search me, God, and know my heart; test me and know my anxious thoughts" (Psalm 139:23). The prayer is not requesting information God does not have. It is requesting that the human person be given access to what God already knows — that the self-examination be honest enough to confirm what God sees rather than contradict it.

Jeremiah 17:9 is not a comfortable text: "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it?" The question is not rhetorical. It is pointing to the actual condition of the human interior — its capacity for self-justification, for rationalization, for constructing narratives that protect the self from honest assessment. The examined life is not a natural achievement. It requires practice, discipline, and a willingness to be surprised by what the examination reveals.

## The Practical Forms

The examined life, for a pastor, requires regular rhythms of deliberate reflection. Not the passive accumulation of experience over decades, which can happen without any real examination at all — merely aging — but the intentional, repeated practice of asking: What am I actually doing here? What are my real motives? Where am I avoiding what God is showing me? Where has my leadership served my own needs more than the congregation's?

A yearly retreat of several days alone with God and a journal. Not a conference. Not a vacation. Alone, with silence and Scripture and the willingness to write honestly about what is actually happening in the interior. I have done this and found it disturbing more often than reassuring. The examined life is not a comfortable practice. It is the practice of a person who believes that being known — truly known, including in the dark corners — is safer than being hidden.

A regular meeting with a spiritual director who is not afraid to ask hard questions. Not a therapist, though therapy is often needed. A spiritual director — someone whose vocation is helping other people pay attention to what God is doing in their interior life, and who is not invested in the pastoral performance the congregation receives. Someone who knows you as a person rather than as a pastor.

A practice of reading your own sermons back to yourself and asking what they reveal about the state of your soul, not just the quality of your exegesis. Where did I rush past a text? Where did I make the application uncomfortable for someone else and comfortable for myself? Where did the sermon end in a place that required nothing of the preacher?

## What It Produces

The pastor who takes this seriously will become, over time, a different kind of leader. Not wiser in the management sense — not more efficient, not more strategically effective, not better at the organizational metrics that denominations measure. Wiser in the biblical sense: the person who knows the difference between appearance and reality, who is not easily deceived about his own motivations, who leads from a place of honesty rather than projection.

Bonhoeffer, writing about the conditions of genuine Christian community, argued that the person who enters community without first having been alone before God brings illusions into the community rather than reality [cite — confirm]. The examined life is the prerequisite for the genuine community — the community that can bear honest speech and genuine confession because its leaders have practiced those things in private first.

The congregation needs that man. Not the performance of that man. The man who has actually sat in the silence and not run from what he found there, who has brought the darkness to God and discovered that the darkness was not hidden from God and was not the end of the relationship.

That is not a man who has achieved something. It is a man who has submitted to something — to the sustained, recurrent, often uncomfortable practice of allowing God to see what he already sees, and allowing that vision to inform how the pastor sees himself.

## What Remains

The ministry that lasts is the ministry built by a pastor who kept examining himself with the same unflinching honesty he brought to the text. Who did not allow the pulpit to become a place of safe external critique while the interior went unexamined. Who understood that the authority of his preaching was inseparable from the integrity of his self-knowledge before God.

I have not done this perfectly. I have skipped years. I have mistaken busy seasons for productive seasons and lost track of what was happening inside them. I have let the external ministry expand while the interior contracted, and eventually the contraction showed — in the sermons, in the pastoral conversations, in the marriage, in the way I was present to my sons.

Return there. Do not stop.

The unexamined life produces an unexamined ministry. The people in the room on Sunday morning deserve better than that. So does the God who called you to the room.

Start there.
