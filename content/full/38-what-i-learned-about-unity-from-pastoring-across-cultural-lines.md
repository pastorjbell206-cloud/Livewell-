I grew up in a church that was racially and culturally homogeneous — not by design, or at least not by conscious design, but by the accumulated effect of a thousand small choices about location, culture, language, and which community the church positioned itself to serve. It was a good church, and I am grateful for what I received there. But it was a church that never required me to be genuinely formed by anyone who was significantly different from me.

Pastoring a multiethnic congregation for the first time — which happened not because I sought it out but because the neighborhood around our church changed significantly while I was there — taught me more about the gospel than a decade of seminary had prepared me for. This article is an attempt to pass on some of what I learned, imperfectly and still in process.

## The Bible Reads Differently in a Diverse Room

The first and most jarring discovery was what happened to the Scriptures when they were read and discussed and preached across cultural lines. Passages I had read hundreds of times suddenly opened in ways I had never seen. The parable of the Good Samaritan, discussed with a congregation that included people who had personal experience of being the Samaritan — the one whose help was unexpected, whose community was the source of theological suspicion — broke open something I had missed for decades.

The Psalms of lament, which I had always engaged primarily as individual spiritual exercises, became communal in the mouth of a congregation where some members carried the specific, historical weight of generational injustice. The resurrection narratives, filtered through the stories of people who had come to faith in contexts where the gospel arrived with liberation rather than with cultural imperialism, had a different texture than the resurrection narratives I had been preaching to people whose theological heritage was primarily the tradition of the colonizers.

This was not relativism. The text does not mean different things to different cultures. But the text has depths that any single cultural reading will miss, because no single culture has the full range of human experience that the Scripture addresses. The congregation that is genuinely diverse is not a harder congregation to preach to — it is a richer one, because it surfaces the layers of the text that cultural homogeneity keeps buried.

Walter Brueggemann has argued for years that the prophetic imagination requires the church to hear the voices of those at the margins, because the margins are where the distortions of the center become visible [cite — confirm]. Pastoring a multiethnic congregation put that claim in my body in a way that reading Brueggemann alone could not. The voices I had not heard were the voices that knew something about the text that I did not.

## Conflict Had to Be Named Differently

The second discovery was that the conflict management skills I had developed in homogeneous ministry were inadequate for multicultural ministry. The assumptions I brought about how conflict should be expressed, what a productive conflict conversation looked like, how quickly resolution should be sought, and what a successful outcome felt like — all of these were culturally shaped in ways I had never examined, because I had never been in a context where they were challenged.

Learning to move through conflict across cultural lines required me to be genuinely curious about how my own cultural assumptions were shaping my approach, to listen more and conclude less quickly, and to create space for forms of conflict engagement that looked different from what I had been taught but were not for that reason less legitimate. The humility required to do this well was a different kind of humility than the pastoral humility I thought I already had.

I thought I was humble. I had been told I was humble. The first time a member of my congregation with a different cultural background told me, with specific gentleness, that my approach to a conflict felt like an extension of the dynamics she had experienced as a person of color in white-led institutions — that my confidence in my own conflict-resolution process was itself a form of cultural power — I did not receive it well. Not at first. The initial response was defensive. It took days to hear what she was actually saying: not that I was a bad person, but that my cultural location had given me tools that worked primarily for people like me, and the congregation I was now leading was not primarily composed of people like me.

That conversation was more formative than most of my seminary training. I am still being formed by it.

## The Steel-Man

The serious counterargument to the emphasis on multicultural ministry sounds like this: cultural homogeneity is not a failure — it is often the result of effective contextualization. The principle that people come to faith most readily through people who share their cultural background has substantial missiological support. Donald McGavran's church growth research argued extensively that homogeneous unit churches grow faster and reach their cultural communities more effectively than diverse ones [cite — confirm]. The emphasis on multicultural congregations as a theological ideal may be importing a social-justice framework into ecclesiology that is not native to it.

This deserves an honest answer. McGavran is not wrong that homogeneous communities often evangelize their own cultural group more effectively. But the New Testament does not present the homogeneous congregation as the ideal destination. It presents the multiethnic community as the eschatological reality that the church is called to embody now. Revelation 7:9 does not show every tribe in its own section of heaven. It shows every tribe, language, people, and nation standing together before the throne. Paul's insistence in Galatians 3:28 — "neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" — is not merely a future hope. It is a present claim about the community that has been formed by the cross.

Homogeneous churches may be more effective at certain kinds of evangelistic growth. They are less capable of demonstrating the specific thing that the New Testament says the church exists to demonstrate: that the divisions of the old humanity have been overcome in Christ.

## What Unity Actually Feels Like

The unity that a genuinely multicultural congregation produces is not the comfortable unity of a homogeneous community where everyone already agrees on the significant things. It is a harder-won, more surprising, and more genuinely beautiful unity — the unity that emerges from people who are genuinely different choosing to be bound together by something they share that is deeper than the things that divide them.

I have seen that unity in the grief that crosses cultural lines when someone in the congregation suffers. I have seen it in the shared prayer that is awkwardly multilingual and more moving for it. I have seen it in the table fellowship that includes foods and customs and languages from half a dozen backgrounds and that images, however imperfectly, the feast that Revelation promises.

There was a memorial service for a member of our congregation — a woman in her eighties who had grown up in Central America and spent the last thirty years of her life in our pew. The service included hymns in Spanish, prayers offered in English by people she had served with for decades, and a long, public silence that her family had asked for and that the rest of us did not know how to fill and then, slowly, found we could sit in together. That silence, shared across every cultural boundary represented in that room, was more theologically weighted than most sermons I have preached.

## The Formation It Requires

Pastoring across cultural lines requires the pastor to be a genuinely humble learner in a setting where the pastor is supposed to be the teacher — and to model that learning publicly, without pretending it is easy or that they have already mastered what they are trying to practice. The congregation that watches their pastor get it wrong, receive the correction, and grow from it is receiving a form of discipleship that the polished sermon cannot provide.

I have not mastered this. Fifteen years into pastoral ministry I am still discovering the cultural assumptions I carry that I have not yet examined — still finding the places where my approach to Scripture, to community, to conflict, and to God has been more shaped by my location in American culture than by the text I claim as my authority. The multicultural congregation is, among other things, the gift that keeps revealing that.

The unity it produces is not comfortable. But it is the kind the New Testament points toward. And I would not trade what I learned from pastoring across those lines for the comfort of a church that never required it of me.

That remains true even on the weeks when the requirement felt like more than I could carry.

Which is most weeks. And which may be the point.
