There is a sentence that sits near the foundation of everything the Christian tradition claims about human beings, and most of us have heard it so many times that we have stopped hearing it at all.

"So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27). The imago Dei. The image of God. We use it in seminary courses and ethics papers and statements of belief. We invoke it in discussions about human dignity and in arguments against abortion and in sermon series about identity and worth. And in using it that much, in that many contexts, for that many purposes, we have domesticated it until it no longer functions as the claim it actually is.

The claim it actually is would be unsettling if we heard it fresh. Let me try to say it plainly.

---

The ancient Near Eastern world in which Genesis was written had a specific and well-established meaning for the phrase "image of god" — tselem elohim. In the imperial theology of Egypt and Mesopotamia, the image of the god was the king [cite — confirm]. The pharaoh was the divine image on earth, the living representation of the divine through whom the god's authority was exercised and the god's will was made concrete in the world. The temples contained statues — images — of the deities, and these images were treated as the presence of the deity in that place. The king was the living image of the god: the mediator between the divine realm and the human, the one whose authority was the authority of the heavens enacted in the affairs of the earth.

Genesis takes this concept and democratizes it to a degree that should have been, in its original context, almost incomprehensible. Not the king. Not the priest. Not the religious specialists who maintained the cult and mediated access to the divine. Every human being. Male and female. All of them, without distinction of social class or political power or religious role, carry the image of God. The whole category of human being is the divine image on earth.

The political implications of that claim in a world organized around the divine status of kings were staggering. If every human being bears the image of the God who made them, then no human being can be legitimately treated as less than a person. The enslaved person whose labor built the temple carries the same image as the pharaoh who commanded the building. The widow who has no one to advocate for her in the city gate carries the same image as the judge who takes bribes from the powerful. The refugee crossing a border with nothing carries the same image as the official who decides whether to close it.

---

The Hebrew word tselem — image — and kavod — glory — are connected in the wisdom tradition in ways that have not been adequately explored in the preaching I have heard in most American churches. Psalm 8 asks: "What is man that you are mindful of him, and the son of man that you care for him? Yet you have made him a little lower than the heavenly beings and crowned him with glory and honor" (Psalm 8:4-5). Crowned with glory and honor. The Hebrew is kavod — the same word used for the divine glory, the weightiness of God's presence, the substance that belongs uniquely to the divine.

This is not a metaphor about feelings. It is a claim about the ontological status of every human being. To bear the image of God is not to carry a spiritual quality that can be recognized through certain kinds of behavior or preserved through certain kinds of belief. It is to carry the weight of the divine in the material constitution of your humanness. The image is not erased by poverty, not diminished by disability, not suspended by incarceration, not revoked by failure, not denied by race.

The tradition has argued, with various degrees of consistency, about whether the imago Dei is damaged by the fall. Augustine held that the image is marred, that human beings no longer bear the image in its original fullness and will not do so until the new creation [cite — confirm]. Calvin was more emphatic about the image's corruption. Others have argued for a structural image — something about human rationality or capacity for relationship — that remains intact even in fallen human beings.

But on one point the tradition has been consistent, even when its practice has not matched: the image of God in human beings is not conditional. It does not require a certain kind of life or a certain kind of belief to be present. It is present in the person in front of you, whoever they are.

---

This is why the treatment of the poor, the imprisoned, the marginalized is not merely a matter of compassion. It is a matter of what you do with the image of God.

Matthew 25:31-46 is the most demanding statement Jesus makes about the final judgment, and its criterion is not doctrinal accuracy. The criterion is what was done with the hungry person, the stranger, the naked, the sick, the imprisoned. "Truly, I say to you, as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." As you did it to them, you did it to me. The identification is total. The God who bears the image has identified himself with the person who bears it — with every person who bears it, including and especially the persons the world has sorted into the category of expendable.

This is not a minor ethical application of the doctrine. It is the doctrine itself, worked out to its conclusion. If every person bears the image of God, and if God has identified himself with the most vulnerable bearers of that image, then what you do to the vulnerable person you do to God. The hunger of the poor person is God's hunger. The imprisonment of the convicted person is God's imprisonment. The displacement of the refugee is God's displacement.

The church that treats these people as problems to be managed rather than as persons in whom the divine image resides has not simply failed an ethical test. It has failed a theological one.

---

The history of the Christian church's relationship to the imago Dei doctrine is not a clean story of consistent application. The same tradition that proclaimed the image of God in every human being built the theological justifications for chattel slavery. The same tradition that insisted on human dignity as a corollary of the imago Dei provided the theological architecture for apartheid. The same tradition that named the image of God as the foundation of human rights has regularly found reasons why particular classes of human beings — the enslaved, the colonized, people with certain disabilities, people with certain mental illnesses — did not fully meet the requirements of image-bearing that qualified them for those rights.

The standard account of this failure is that the theology was right but the practice was inconsistent — that people failed to live up to what they believed. That is partly true. But it is not the whole story. In many cases, the theology was revised to accommodate the practice. The image of God was qualified: certain human beings bore it more fully than others. Certain conditions diminished it. Certain failures forfeited it. The doctrine was stretched until it could cover the arrangements the powerful needed it to cover.

This is the pattern that the doctrine, rightly understood, is designed to prevent. The imago Dei is not a flexible concept. It does not expand and contract based on the requirements of the powerful. The person in front of you bears it. Full stop. The implications of that fact are not optional.

I have failed to receive this in full myself. I have treated the image of God as a theological affirmation while organizing my actual practices around a much more qualified version — spending more energy on people who seemed more likely to produce a return on that investment, more comfortable with the people who occupied roles in the social order I understood, less genuinely present to the people whose conditions demanded a kind of presence that would cost me something.

The doctrine demands more than I have given it.

---

Brueggemann argues that the Psalms give voice to the full range of human experience precisely because the whole of human experience belongs to God — that nothing the image-bearer experiences is outside the scope of the God whose image he bears [cite — confirm]. The person in the homeless shelter is having an experience that belongs to God. The person in the ICU is having an experience that belongs to God. The person in the cell block is having an experience that belongs to God. The worship of the church that does not connect to those experiences has not yet understood whose presence fills the earth.

Paul's description of the body of Christ in 1 Corinthians 12 is, in part, an application of the imago Dei to the community of faith. The parts that seem weaker are indispensable. The parts that seem less honorable are treated with greater honor. The God who made every human being in the divine image has distributed gifts across the whole body, not concentrated them in the parts the world considers impressive. The community that organizes itself around the impressive parts and marginalizes the rest has not understood what it means to be a body that reflects the image of the One who made it.

---

The imago Dei means every human being carries a weight that no social arrangement can legitimately cancel.

It means the person your society has sorted into the category of expendable is not expendable to the God who made them.

It means the treatment of the hungry, the stranger, the imprisoned is not a secondary ethical concern but the primary site of encounter with the God whose image they bear.

It means the church that claims this doctrine and tolerates the systems that crush the image-bearers it was designed to protect has not yet received what it claims to believe.

The image is there. In the person across from you in the unemployment line, across the border, across the street, in the locked unit, in the shelter. In the person your politics have sorted into the category of problem. In the person your economics have sorted into the category of inefficiency.

The image is there.

What you do with it is the question the doctrine has always been asking.
