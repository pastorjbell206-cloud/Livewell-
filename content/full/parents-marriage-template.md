You have a template. You may not have named it, you may actively resist it, you may have built your entire adult identity in reaction to it — but it is there. Every person who grew up watching two people be married absorbed a working model of what marriage is. What it looks like under pressure. What happens when there is not enough money. What people do with desire and with anger and with disappointment. What conflict sounds like. What love looks like on a Tuesday.

That model is running in your marriage right now. The only question is whether you know it is.

The people who do the most damage in their marriages are almost never the ones doing it on purpose. They are people doing exactly what was modeled — patterns so deeply familiar they do not register as patterns at all. They feel like reality.

## The Biblical Case for Looking Back

Ezekiel 18 contains a corrective that was radical in its context: "The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son" (v. 20). The prophetic tradition was pushing back against the fatalism of intergenerational curse — the assumption that what your father was, you will be. The exilic community was inclined to blame their destruction on their ancestors: "Our fathers ate sour grapes and our teeth are set on edge." The prophet's answer was a theological declaration of individual moral responsibility and, with it, the possibility of something genuinely new.

That corrective is necessary and important. But it does not mean the formation did not happen. It means the formation does not have to be determinative. There is a difference between "you will become your parents" and "you are shaped by what you watched." Ezekiel is insisting on the second while refusing the first. The shaping is real. The determination is not inevitable.

Paul's instruction in Romans 12:2 — "be transformed by the renewing of your mind" — uses the word *metamorphoo*, the complete-form-change word. What is being renewed is the *nous*: the mind, the frame, the operative structure through which reality gets interpreted. Your parents' marriage shaped your *nous*. Transformation is the possibility of seeing past that frame to something more true.

The text is not describing a painless process. Transformation of the *nous* requires first the acknowledgment that the frame exists — that what feels like reality is, in fact, a particular interpretation of reality that was installed by particular experiences in a particular family. Most people never get there. They carry the frame as if it were the window glass rather than the tint on the glass, and they interpret everything through it without knowing they are doing so.

## The Historical Weight of What We Inherit

The research in family systems theory, developed most comprehensively by Murray Bowen [cite — confirm] in the middle of the twentieth century, demonstrated that the emotional patterns of families transmit across generations with a reliability that feels almost genetic. Bowen called this "multigenerational transmission" — the process by which the emotional functioning of grandparents shapes parents shapes children, often without any of them knowing it is happening. The patterns do not require verbal instruction. They are absorbed through what is modeled, through what is rewarded, through what is never spoken about.

The silence is often the most powerful transmission. The things that were never said in your family of origin — about money, about desire, about anger, about grief — those silences taught you something. They taught you that those subjects are not safe, or that they are handled by not handling them, or that the appropriate response to them is performance rather than presence.

You brought those lessons into your marriage. And unless you have done the deliberate work of examining them, they are still operating.

## My Own Formation

My own formation on this is not distant. I grew up watching a marriage that was functional in its external structure and emotionally remote in its interior. Two people doing what was required of them. Nobody was harmed in any dramatic way. But what I absorbed — without knowing I was absorbing it — was that closeness was not available in a marriage, that emotional distance was the default, that you managed your interior life on your own.

I carried that into my marriage. Not as philosophy — as assumption. The work of naming it, of tracking it back to where it came from, of deciding that I did not want to give my wife a marriage built on my father's unexplained distance — that work took years and required help I did not initially want to ask for.

The relief of naming it accurately was significant. Not because naming it fixed it. But because a thing that has a name can be addressed. A thing that is invisible cannot.

I am one of five sons, and I am a father of five sons. What I pass on — in how I love their mother, in what I model when the money is short or the argument is real — that is not neutral. It is instruction. The transmission runs in both directions from where I stand.

## The Counter-Position Worth Hearing

There is an objection to this kind of family-of-origin work that deserves a serious answer. Some counselors and pastors argue that prolonged focus on what happened in your parents' marriage functions as an excuse — that it keeps people anchored to the past and diverts responsibility from the choices being made in the present. The concern is not groundless. There is a version of this analysis that produces paralysis rather than growth, that allows a person to remain permanently a victim of their formation rather than an agent capable of different choices.

That version is not what is being commended here. The goal of examining the template is not to produce a story about why the present difficulties are your parents' fault. It is to produce clarity — to make visible what is invisible, to name the patterns so they can be chosen or refused rather than enacted unconsciously. Ezekiel's insistence on individual responsibility is not in tension with this. It depends on it. You cannot be responsible for a choice you do not know you are making.

## The Patterns Worth Examining

What did conflict look like in your family of origin — explosive, suppressed, passive-aggressive, managed through humor or avoidance? You are probably using one of those approaches in your own marriage without having consciously chosen it.

What did affection look like? Was it expressed physically, verbally, through acts of service, through provision — or was it not expressed at all? The partner who grew up in a household where love was never named may be providing for their family at great cost and not understanding why their spouse feels unloved. The provision is real. The translation is missing.

What was the model for roles — who managed the money, who managed the emotions, who had authority, who deferred? Those models are running in your marriage. Some of them may be worth keeping. Some of them are worth examining with more honesty than they have received.

And: what did you conclude about yourself from watching your parents' marriage? The child of a marriage that modeled contempt often concludes they are not worth being treated well. The child of a marriage that modeled emotional absence often concludes that their own emotional needs are too much. Those conclusions shape the marriage you are in now — the level of care you accept, the needs you suppress, the patterns you normalize.

John Gottman's research on the "sound relationship house" [cite — confirm] identifies each partner's knowledge of their spouse's inner world as foundational to a healthy marriage. But before you can know your spouse's inner world deeply, you have to know your own. And knowing your own requires knowing how it was shaped.

## A Concrete Pastoral Scene

I sat with a couple last year — together for sixteen years, in genuine crisis. The presenting issue was a recurring argument about money, which is almost never about money. As we worked backward through the specific fight, it became clear that the husband's response to financial stress was to go silent and withdraw. His wife's response to his withdrawal was to escalate — to push harder for connection and conversation, to interpret the silence as abandonment.

He learned the silence from a father who managed every crisis by going quiet and working harder. In his template, silence in difficulty was strength, not withdrawal.

She learned the escalation from a mother who had been genuinely abandoned during a financial crisis — who had been left without information, without inclusion, without the basic dignity of knowing what was happening in her own household. In her template, a husband who went silent in a financial crisis was a husband who was about to disappear.

Neither of them knew they were enacting anything. Neither of them was wrong about what they had learned. Both of them were paying the cost of templates they had never examined.

Naming those templates in the room that day did not resolve the pattern immediately. But it changed the question. They stopped asking "why are you doing this to me" and started asking "what are we each bringing to this."

That is what the work of examining the template makes possible.

## Three Questions for This Week

What is one pattern in your marriage that you recognize from your parents' marriage — something you either replicated or overcorrected for?

What did you conclude about yourself, about love, or about marriage from what you watched? Are those conclusions still running, unchallenged, in your current relationship?

Have you ever talked with your spouse about both of your families of origin — not to assign blame, but to understand the templates each of you brought into the marriage?

**This week:** one conversation with your spouse about what each of your families of origin modeled. No blame, no defensiveness — just curiosity about the templates you are both running from.

Ezekiel's corrective stands: the son does not have to carry the father's iniquity. But the son who has never looked at the father's iniquity is almost certainly carrying it anyway.

See it clearly. Name it honestly. Then decide — deliberately, consciously, with your spouse — what you are choosing instead.

That is what renewal of the mind looks like in a marriage. Not the erasure of what was formed. The decision about what to keep.
