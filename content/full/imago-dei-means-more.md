In the ancient Near East, a king would place images of himself throughout the territories he governed. The image — the *tselem* — functioned as a claim of sovereignty: this territory belongs to the one whose image stands here. Stone figures of Pharaoh or Nebuchadnezzar in distant provinces announced that the power at the center reached this far. The image was not decorative. It was jurisdictional.

Genesis 1:26–27 enters this cultural world and overturns its logic entirely. The whole earth is God's territory. God's image — *imago Dei* — is not placed in stone in strategic locations. It is breathed into every human being, without qualification, without exception, without condition. Every person, in this account, is a living marker of divine sovereignty — not a representative of a political ruler but the bearer of the living God's own likeness, set in the middle of creation as its steward and crown.

This is not primarily a statement about human dignity, though it grounds human dignity. It is primarily a statement about God's sovereignty. And its implications run farther than most preaching ever reaches.

## The Logic of the Doctrine

If every human being bears the image of God, then every act of violence against a human being is an act against the image of the One whose claim extends over all creation. Genesis 9:6 makes this explicit in the context of law: "Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed, for God made man in his own image." The gravity of the crime is not merely that a life is ended. It is that an image of God has been destroyed. The prohibition against murder is grounded in theology, not merely in social utility.

Expand that logic. Not just murder. What about the slower violences — the poverty that shortens lives by decades, the incarceration that strips agency and dignity across years, the medical neglect of communities without insurance, the housing conditions that expose children to lead and mold and chronic stress? If these things diminish or destroy the people who bear God's image, what does the *imago Dei* doctrine demand of us in response?

The tradition has pressed this logic further than most contemporary preaching does. John Calvin, writing in the Institutes, argued that when we meet a neighbor in need, we have no business asking whether they deserve our help, because we see on their face the image of God and that is sufficient basis for action. [cite — confirm Calvin reference] The image is not a spiritual compliment we extend to people we already respect. It is an ontological status that precedes and overrides every category we impose upon people to justify our indifference toward them. The person sleeping under the bridge, the person convicted of a violent crime, the person whose immigration status is contested — they bear the image of God as fully as the person in the front pew who tithes faithfully. The doctrine does not recognize our sorting categories.

## The New Testament Radicalization

Jesus does not soften this logic. He radicalizes it. In the Sermon on the Mount, he extends the prohibition on murder to the prohibition on contempt: "Anyone who says to a brother or sister, 'Raca,' is answerable to the court" (Matthew 5:22). The word *raca* is a term of dismissal — the kind of word you use when you are communicating that someone does not deserve your serious attention, that they are beneath the dignity of genuine engagement. Jesus treats this as a violation nearly as serious as the violence that follows when contempt goes to its logical end. The contempt is where the violence begins, and the contempt is already a violation of the *imago Dei* in the person being dismissed.

Matthew 25 takes this further, to the place where the doctrine becomes almost unbearable in its demands. "Whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me" (Matthew 25:40). The person who is hungry, thirsty, a stranger, without clothing, sick, imprisoned — in each case, Christ says: this is where I am. To encounter that person's need and to walk past it is not merely a missed opportunity for charity. It is a failure to recognize Christ. The *imago Dei* has become, in the Gospel's account, identification. God is not merely the one in whose image the vulnerable person was made. God is the one present in that person's vulnerability, in a way that makes the encounter with their need an encounter with the divine presence.

This is not comfortable theology. It was not meant to be. The text does not invite us to feel good about the dignity of people like us. It demands that we see the dignity of people we have sorted into categories of lesser concern.

## The Consistency the Doctrine Requires

The *imago Dei* doctrine, consistently applied, prohibits a selective compassion that treats some people's suffering as worthy of prophetic attention and others' suffering as a matter of personal responsibility. It is common in American evangelical culture to affirm the *imago Dei* in the abstract while maintaining social arrangements that systematically deny it in practice — through housing patterns that concentrate poverty in some communities while protecting wealth in others, through criminal justice practices that treat the same crimes differently depending on the race and class of the accused, through political positions that advocate for the unborn while showing indifference to the conditions that will shape the lives of those born into poverty.

These inconsistencies are not incidental. They reveal a functional doctrine of human worth that is considerably narrower than the Genesis account warrants. If the *imago Dei* applies uniformly, then the death of a child from a preventable disease in a poor neighborhood carries the same theological weight as the death of a child from any other cause. The bearing of God's image is not diminished by poverty, by race, by disability, by age, by criminal history, by immigration status, or by any other category human societies have constructed to manage the distribution of dignity.

Disability theologians have pressed this point with particular sharpness in recent decades. The church that speaks of the *imago Dei* while designing worship spaces that exclude people in wheelchairs, programming that ignores sensory needs, and community that implicitly communicates that people with cognitive disabilities are objects of care rather than contributors to community — that church has believed the doctrine insufficiently. The image of God in the person with Down syndrome is not a diminished image. It is the full image. The church's practice must reflect that, not as an accommodation but as recognition of what has always been true.

## The Steel-Man Objection

The serious objection deserves a serious hearing: doctrine and policy are different things, and *imago Dei* does not automatically resolve contested questions about how best to structure social arrangements. This is true. The doctrine does not settle debates about the best mechanism for addressing poverty, the most effective approach to criminal justice reform, or the correct policy on immigration. Reasonable people who affirm the *imago Dei* fully can disagree about which policies best serve the flourishing of image-bearers.

But the objection cannot be used to soften the doctrine into irrelevance. While the *imago Dei* does not generate a specific legislative program, it does generate certain prohibitions and certain requirements. It prohibits treating any human being as expendable. It prohibits arrangements that systematically discard people — through poverty, through dehumanizing institutional treatment, through the legal stripping of legal standing — as if their image-bearing were conditional on their social usefulness. The distance between the doctrine's general claim and specific policy may be debated. The prohibition against contempt is not.

The church that can name the *imago Dei* on Sunday morning and spend the rest of the week in comfortable indifference toward the people whose image-bearing it has just affirmed has not applied the doctrine. It has invoked the language without bearing the cost.

## What It Demands Next

I have said "every person bears the image of God" in sermons. I have not always followed that sentence to where it leads. It is easier to affirm the doctrine than to reckon with what the doctrine requires of me when I pass the man sleeping in the parking garage or vote on policies that will affect the conditions of people I will never meet. The doctrine is not comfortable if you take it seriously. It is costly. It demands that people I would rather categorize as problems be seen instead as bearers of the same image I claim for myself.

The image cannot be erased. Genesis does not provide a mechanism for the loss of the *imago Dei* — not through sin, not through poverty, not through conviction, not through the categories any society constructs to manage the distribution of moral concern. Every person who has ever lived, and who is living now, and who will live — bears the image of the God who placed it in them before they had done anything to deserve it or forfeit it.

That is not comfortable theology. It is the foundation of all the other theology that follows. Get it wrong — narrow it, qualify it, apply it selectively — and everything else in the moral architecture of the faith tilts with it.

Get it right, and the woman at the shelter, the man in the cell, the child in the underfunded school, the refugee at the border — they are not problems to be solved. They are the image of the God you worship, and what you do to them is the question that will be waiting for you.

What it demands next is the harder question. One the doctrine will not let us defer indefinitely.
