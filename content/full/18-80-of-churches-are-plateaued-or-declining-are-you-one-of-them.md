The statistic from Tony Morgan and The Unstuck Group has become one of the most-cited in pastoral leadership conversations, and it has become that because it refuses to let the church pretend the situation is better than it is: eighty to eighty-five percent of churches in America are either plateaued or declining [cite — confirm]. Not growing — maintaining at best, losing ground at worst. And the pastors leading these churches often know something is wrong but cannot quite name what it is.

The first and hardest step toward addressing a plateau is the honest acknowledgment that it exists. This sounds obvious, but it is not easy in practice. The language of faith — of seasons, of God's timing, of faithfulness over fruitfulness — can become a sophisticated way of avoiding the honest assessment of whether the church is genuinely stuck and why. Seasons can be real. They can also be convenient. The pastor who cannot tell the difference between patient faithfulness and comfortable avoidance is at risk of leading a church that stays stuck.

## The Longer History of This Problem

The plateau problem is not new, though the scale of it is newly visible. The mainline Protestant denominations began their sustained numerical decline in the late 1960s, and sociologists at the time identified it as a structural shift rather than a temporary dip — a consequence of the loosening of the social pressures that had maintained religious affiliation across several generations [cite — confirm]. The evangelical church, which had largely grown through those same decades and interpreted its growth as the fruit of theological fidelity, began to face its own plateau in the early years of this century, and the metrics that emerged were sobering.

The church growth movement of the 1970s and 1980s — built on the insights of Donald McGavran and church growth researchers at Fuller Theological Seminary — had promised that the application of social science to ecclesiology would produce measurable growth [cite — confirm]. The experiments that followed produced some genuine fruit and also produced a generation of churches that were better at marketing than at discipleship, better at attraction than at formation. By the 1990s, Willow Creek Community Church itself had commissioned research suggesting that its seeker-sensitive model was producing large audiences without producing formed disciples, a conclusion that its own leadership publicly acknowledged with remarkable honesty [cite — confirm].

The plateau crisis is, in part, the harvest of several decades of church strategy that prioritized attendance over formation. A church that optimized for gathering people was not, necessarily, optimizing for the thing that produces growth in the New Testament sense: genuine disciples who make genuine disciples, who carry the gospel into their networks and communities in ways that generate genuine new life.

## What a Plateau Actually Looks Like

A plateau is not always a period of flat attendance. A plateau is the condition of a church that is not fulfilling its potential for the mission it has been given in the community it inhabits. A church can be numerically stable and deeply plateaued — maintaining its numbers through transfer growth while having no genuine impact on the unchurched people in its community. A church can even be growing numerically and still be plateaued in its actual discipleship impact, adding attendees without producing disciples.

The more honest diagnostic questions are not about attendance trajectory but about mission effectiveness. Is the church reaching people who were not previously connected to any church? Is there evidence of genuine life transformation in the congregation? Are people being sent from the church into the community and the world with the gospel, or is the church primarily organized around gathering and consuming? Is the leadership of the church deepening and multiplying, or is it dependent on the same small group of people doing the same things they have always done?

These are harder questions to answer than attendance questions, and they require a kind of honest self-examination that is uncomfortable for leaders who have invested their professional identity in the health of the institution they lead. I have sat in rooms with pastors who could describe every metric of their church's activity in precise detail and who had no idea whether any of it was producing genuine disciples. The metrics they tracked were not the metrics that mattered. That is a form of blindness that is very common and very hard to see from the inside.

## A Steel-Manned Counter-Position

The most serious pushback on plateau-focused pastoral leadership comes from a direction that deserves to be heard: the argument that the numerical growth framework itself is the problem, not the solution. This argument says that the preoccupation with growth metrics — attendance, giving, baptisms, small group enrollment — has distorted the church's understanding of its own mission. Faithfulness is not the same as fruitfulness. The prophets preached in declining Israel. Jeremiah's ministry produced no visible success by any metric and enormous personal suffering. Jesus himself watched his crowd thin dramatically in John 6 after the feeding of the five thousand and appeared to accept the departure as a consequence of faithfulness rather than a failure of strategy. Perhaps the plateaued church is not failing. Perhaps it is being faithful.

This is not a frivolous argument. It names something real: the church is not a business, growth is not the only measure of faithfulness, and the preoccupation with scale has produced genuine distortions in pastoral ministry. A pastor who leads a congregation of forty people faithfully for thirty years, knowing them deeply, loving them genuinely, and forming them slowly in the way of Jesus, has done something that cannot be dismissed because it does not show up well on a church growth chart.

But the argument overreaches in one specific direction. The same Jesus who watched the crowd thin in John 6 gave the Great Commission in Matthew 28. The same Paul who wrote about suffering and weakness as the condition of apostolic ministry also described his ambition to preach the gospel where it had not yet been heard. The church that uses the theology of faithfulness to exempt itself from the question of whether it is actually reaching its community has made faithfulness a justification for disengagement. That is not faithfulness. It is contentment with smallness dressed in theological language.

## Common Causes of Plateaus

Tony Morgan and the Unstuck Group have identified several patterns that appear consistently in plateaued churches [cite — confirm]. One is leadership that has not kept pace with the church's development — a pastor who led the church effectively at two hundred people but has not developed the skills and structures needed to lead at five hundred. Another is internal focus — a church whose energy and resources are predominantly directed inward, toward serving its own members, rather than outward, toward the community it was placed to reach. A third is structural rigidity — systems and processes that were designed for an earlier season and have become barriers to effectiveness in the current one.

A fourth, often overlooked, is cultural health. A church that has allowed toxic relational dynamics, unresolved conflict, or a culture of criticism to take root will struggle to grow regardless of the quality of its programs or preaching. Culture eats strategy for breakfast — and it also eats church growth methodology.

## A Concrete Scene

I know a pastor who led a congregation for eleven years while it plateaued quietly around two hundred and twenty people. He was not a bad pastor. He preached well, cared genuinely, and managed the church's internal life with competence. What he had never done was ask the question that most needed asking: Who is this church for? The congregation had a clear implicit answer to that question — it existed for the people who already attended — and everything it did reflected that answer. The pastoral care was for members. The programming was for families already connected. The Sunday experience was designed to serve the people already in the room.

He brought an outside consultant who spent two days asking the congregation that question in different forms. What emerged was not a program change. It was a reorientation of the church's self-understanding — from a community that served its members to a community sent to its neighborhood. The growth that followed was slow and not dramatic. But it was genuine: people who had no prior church connection, who had been in the neighborhood for years while the church focused elsewhere, who began to find a reason to walk through the door.

## Starting the Honest Conversation

If you suspect your church is plateaued, the most useful thing you can do is bring an outside perspective into the diagnosis. Not because you cannot see the situation accurately, but because the pastor is so embedded in the church's culture that certain things become invisible through familiarity. An outside coach, a trusted peer network, or a structured assessment process can surface what proximity conceals.

The Pastors Connection Network exists partly to give pastors access to this kind of perspective — not as a consulting firm, but as a community of peers who have navigated similar terrain and can offer the honest, experienced, relationally-grounded perspective that coaches are paid for but friends give freely. If your church is stuck, you do not need to figure it out alone.

The plateau is not a verdict. It is a condition. Conditions can change. But they do not change without honesty about what they are.
