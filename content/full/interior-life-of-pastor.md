There is a kind of ministry that looks like faithfulness from the outside and is hollow from the inside. I have practiced it. I know what it feels like to preach a sermon I no longer believed at the level of the gut, to sit with a grieving widow and speak the words of comfort while my own soul was somewhere else entirely — competent, practiced, professionally present, inwardly absent. Nobody knew. That is the most dangerous kind of drift.

The ancient desert fathers called it *acedia* — the noonday demon, the spiritual condition in which the monk goes through the motions of prayer while the interior of his life grows arid and cold. Evagrius of Pontus catalogued it in the fourth century as the most destructive of the spiritual vices precisely because it is not dramatic. [cite — confirm] It is not scandal. It is erosion. The pastor who falls into sexual immorality makes headlines. The pastor whose interior life quietly dries up for a decade while his church grows takes a long time to name what has happened, and by then the damage is wide.

The interior life of the pastor is not a private luxury. It is the aquifer from which the congregation drinks.

---

Paul, writing to Timothy, does not say "manage your congregation well" as the first commandment of pastoral formation. He says, "Watch your life and doctrine closely" (1 Timothy 4:16). Life first. Doctrine second. The order is not accidental. The theological content you preach is only as durable as the life from which it emerges. A man can preach justification by faith while practicing justification by performance in every corner of his interior world, and the congregation will eventually feel the gap even if they cannot name it.

There is a Christological pressure behind this that the pastoral tradition has sometimes noticed and more often forgotten. Jesus does not send the disciples out from emptiness. Before the feeding of the five thousand, he withdraws to a desolate place to pray (Matthew 14:13). Before choosing the twelve, he spends the night in prayer on the mountain (Luke 6:12). Before the cross, the garden. The withdrawal is not a failure to engage with the demands pressing on him from every direction. It is the condition of an engagement that is worth anything.

The structure of the Gospels keeps making this point, and we keep reading past it. We attend to the miracles and the teaching and the controversies. We do not attend to the pattern underneath them: the rhythm of outward action and inward return, the refusal to allow the demands of the crowd to crowd out the source from which Jesus draws his own capacity to meet those demands. If the one who had no sin needed this — needed the night in prayer, needed the desolate place, needed Gethsemane — what does it say about those of us who do not have his resources and yet pattern our ministries as if the source is inexhaustible?

---

The pastoral office compounds this problem in ways specific to its structure.

Ministry is relentlessly outward. There is always someone who needs something. The calendar runs Sunday to Sunday, and within that rhythm are crises that respect no calendar. The structural movement of pastoral calling is toward people — toward their pain, their questions, their marriages, their grief, their doubts. This is not a flaw in the calling. It is the calling. But it creates a centrifugal force that spins the pastor outward until the interior goes unattended for months, then years.

The congregation does not intend this. They are not taking anything that wasn't offered. The pastor who offers everything he has without ever returning to the source will eventually have nothing to offer — and will have no idea how long he has been empty, because the professional competencies of pastoral ministry can be performed without the interior life that gives them meaning. He will still preach the words. They will still be technically correct. But they will lack the specific weight that comes from someone speaking out of lived encounter with what he is preaching.

People feel this before they can describe it. The visit that is professional but not present. The sermon that is accurate but not alive. The pastoral prayer that is eloquent but not really from anywhere. These things register at a level beneath conscious analysis, and over time they shape a congregation's relationship to its own faith. A pastor whose interior life is empty is not neutral in his effect on the people he serves. He is gradually teaching them, by example, that the faith is something you perform rather than something you inhabit.

---

What does a pastor's interior life actually require? Not more productivity disciplines. Not a faster morning routine. Something harder: the willingness to be with yourself in the presence of God, which is another way of saying the willingness to stop performing even in private.

I have discovered — slowly, with resistance — that the most threatening spiritual discipline for me is not fasting or extended prayer. It is silence. The fasting ends and I feel righteous. The prayer fills time. But silence requires that I sit still long enough for the actual condition of my soul to surface, and what surfaces is not always what I want to see. The pride. The exhaustion dressed up as dedication. The low-grade resentment toward a congregation I claim to love. The fear that operates beneath the confidence I project. The distance between what I preach and what I privately inhabit.

Silence does not produce these things. It reveals them. That is exactly why we avoid it.

Bernard of Clairvaux wrote to Pope Eugenius III in the twelfth century about the spiritual danger of a man so consumed by the demands of his office that he had no time to attend to himself. He called it a form of hardness of heart — not cruelty, but the slow insensibility that develops when the interior is perpetually sacrificed to the exterior. [cite — confirm] Bernard was writing to a pope, but he was describing every pastor who has ever buried his interior life under the weight of his institutional responsibilities and called it faithfulness.

The older tradition called this *recessus* — the withdrawal, the return to the source. Not a vacation from the work, but the periodic, deliberate re-entry into the depths from which the work must come. The Benedictine rhythm of *ora et labora*, prayer and work, was not a schedule. It was a theology: the work emerges from the prayer, and the prayer disciplines the work, and neither can substitute for the other.

---

Eugene Peterson spent decades arguing that the pastoral vocation requires a countercultural stubbornness about the interior life. [cite — confirm] He was not describing a technique. He was describing a posture — the refusal to let the relentless demands of ministry crowd out the one thing that makes ministry possible: the pastor's own living relationship with the God he proclaims.

Peterson's particular genius was connecting this to the language of poetry and prayer — the slow, patient attention required to hear what is actually being said beneath what is being said. The Psalms as the school of honest prayer. The laments as permission to bring the actual condition of your soul before God rather than the performance of your soul that you offer in public. The pastor who only knows how to pray in public — how to pray the confident, eloquent prayer of someone who has everything together — has been deprived of the most basic resource of the interior life: the freedom to tell the truth about what is actually happening in him.

---

The objection that deserves honest acknowledgment here is this: the congregation needs what the pastor gives, and the congregation's needs are real and urgent. The pastor who is attending to his interior life is not attending to those needs. Is this self-indulgence dressed in spiritual language?

No. It is the same logic that instructs you to put on your own oxygen mask before helping the child beside you. Not because your life matters more than the child's. Because if you lose consciousness, you cannot help anyone. The pastor who depletes himself entirely in service of others is not more faithful than the one who replenishes. He is more dramatic in his failure, which is not the same thing.

The structural reality is that sustained pastoral ministry requires sustained attention to the pastor's own formation. Not as a luxury, not as a reward for faithful service, but as the ongoing condition of the ministry's possibility. You cannot give what you do not have. You cannot preach what you do not inhabit. You cannot offer the presence of Christ to someone in their darkest room if you have not been in the presence of Christ yourself recently enough to know what it actually feels like.

---

The health of your soul is not a personal concern separate from your public calling. It is your calling.

The congregation you serve deserves a pastor whose words come from somewhere real — from a life that has actually been lived in proximity to the God he is describing, from a prayer life that has included the dark nights and the silences and the moments when nothing seemed to be happening and he returned to the cell anyway, again and again, whether or not he felt anything. That is not performance. That is the interior life bearing fruit in the only way that ultimately matters.

Watch your life. Watch it before you watch your doctrine, because your doctrine will eventually go the direction your life has been going. The congregation is watching too, whether or not they know they are.

Go back to the source. Regularly. Whether or not you feel like it.

Especially when you don't.
