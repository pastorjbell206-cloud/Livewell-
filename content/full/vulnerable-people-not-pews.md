The man pushing the cart along the highway median at noon on a Wednesday is not going to find you. He does not have a car. He may not have a phone. He has learned, through the repeated experience of being handled and dismissed, to distrust the kind of institution that has a nice sign out front. He is not outside the church because he is indifferent to God. He is outside because the church he encountered treated him as a problem to be managed rather than a person to be known.

This is not a theory. It is the church's institutional history, and it is still operating today.

The most vulnerable people in your city — the homeless, the working poor, the undocumented, the recently incarcerated, the woman in the apartment above the nail salon who is one lost shift away from not making rent — are not in your pews. Not accidentally. The church's architecture, its culture, its economic assumptions, its programming calendar, its location in the neighborhood it chose when it could afford to choose — all of these are signals, and the vulnerable read them accurately. *This is not for you.* They don't always say it that way. They say it by not coming.

This is a theological problem before it is a pastoral problem.

The biblical witness about who God's concern runs toward is not ambiguous. It is among the most consistent data sets in the entire canon. In the Exodus narrative — the event that defines Israel's theological identity — God hears the cry of slaves before they have a theology, before they have a temple, before they have anything to offer except their suffering (Exodus 3:7). The hearing precedes the covenant. God is present to the oppressed not because they have earned presence but because oppression itself is an offense against the God who made them. The entire Sinai law code is structured around this: the widow, the orphan, the stranger appear not as charitable recipients but as the test of the community's faithfulness. *You shall not oppress a stranger; you know the heart of a stranger, for you were strangers in Egypt* (Exodus 23:9). The remembering is commanded because it shapes behavior. The community that forgets what it was rescued from will re-enact the oppression it was rescued from. Israel proved this several times. The church has proved it too.

The prophets named the failure with a precision that should unsettle every congregation that has built its programming around the comfortable. Amos directed his indictment not at pagan nations but at the covenant community — the people who brought their tithes, attended their festivals, sang their songs, and kept their religion entirely separate from the economic arrangements that crushed the poor at the gate (Amos 5:21-24). The prophetic critique is not that Israel was irreligious. It was that Israel was religious in ways that cost nothing, that made no contact with the social arrangements the religion was supposed to transform. God had given them a law that was, by ancient Near Eastern standards, a radical redistribution mechanism — gleaning laws, Jubilee, remission of debts, no interest charged to the poor. And they had made the religion work without any of it.

The American church stands in that line. Not because it is evil. Because it is comfortable, and comfort has its own institutional momentum.

Here is what comfort produces, ecclesially: congregations whose members are economically similar, whose programming serves the already-churched, whose evangelistic imagination runs toward the unchurched who look a great deal like the churched. The working poor are not hostile to God. Research consistently shows that religious practice is more common in low-income communities than in affluent ones [cite — confirm]. What the working poor are hostile to is the particular form of church that signals, in a hundred quiet ways, that they will need to dress differently, speak differently, and assume a kind of economic stability they do not have before they fully belong. The church's insularity is not an absence of evangelism. It is evangelism directed toward people who already share the church's cultural assumptions.

This is where the counter-argument has real weight. Some churches have, with genuine integrity, planted themselves in the hardest neighborhoods, adjusted their entire economic model to serve communities without resources, and paid the institutional cost of that commitment. There are pastors in cities across this country who have spent twenty years in the same block of a neighborhood that was never going to give them the growth metrics that would make them visible in the evangelical world. They are not famous. They are not platforms. They are doing what Jeremiah told the exiles to do: seeking the welfare of the city they were sent to, not the city they wished they inhabited (Jeremiah 29:7). Their witness is real and it is costly and I am not dismissing it.

But they are not the majority of the church's institutional weight. The majority of American church resources — buildings, budgets, staff, attention — is concentrated in a band of the population that is not the most vulnerable. That is not an accusation. It is an observation with theological implications.

What does it require, practically, to reorient toward the people who are not in the pews?

First, it requires going to them rather than waiting for them to come. The mission does not originate with the church's doors. Jesus did not wait in Capernaum for the sick to find him. He moved through the towns. He was in the places that the religious infrastructure had written off — the tax collector's house, the Samaritan well, the graveyard where the demoniac had been chained (Mark 5). The pattern is consistent enough to be a theological statement: the movement of the kingdom is toward the excluded, not from them toward the center. The church that is always waiting for the excluded to find the door has reversed the direction of the movement it claims to be part of.

Second, it requires listening before programming. The church's first instinct, confronted with a community's need, is to design a ministry to address it. The ministry becomes the church's project, organized around the church's assumptions about the need, run by the church's volunteers, measured by the church's metrics. This is how food pantries become the church's act of benevolence toward the poor rather than a relationship between neighbors. It is how after-school programs become evangelistic pipelines rather than genuine investments in children. The vulnerable people outside the pews do not need more programs. They need the church to know their names, to understand the actual conditions of their lives, and to let that knowledge change the church rather than simply adding a service line to the church's portfolio.

Third, it requires institutional honesty about why the vulnerable are not there. Not guilt — guilt that does not lead to changed behavior is just a feeling the comfortable church uses to feel less comfortable without actually becoming different. Honesty. Why is the parking lot arranged the way it is? Why is the dress culture what it is? Why are the small group times set when they are? Why is the financial ask structured the way it is? These are not trivial questions. They are the questions that reveal whether the institution has been shaped by the people it claims to serve or by the people it has been most successful at retaining.

Luke 14 contains a parable that should be read at every building committee meeting and every programming retreat. The great banquet from which the invited guests excuse themselves, and the host who sends his servants into the streets and alleys to bring in the poor, the crippled, the blind, the lame (Luke 14:21). The table gets full. It gets full with exactly the people who had been excluded from the original invitation list. The parable is not primarily about eschatology, though it has eschatological dimensions. It is about who belongs at the table and what happens when the original invitation is declined. The church that is waiting for the comfortable to accept its invitation may be missing the entire point of the table.

The most vulnerable people in your city are not indifferent to the Gospel. They are indifferent to the church's current form, and they have reasons. They have been told, in ways more persuasive than any sermon, that what the church offers is not for them. Changing that requires more than a targeted program. It requires the church to examine what it has become — for whom it has been shaped, whose presence it has optimized its existence around — and to ask whether that is the church Jesus described or a comfortable institution that has learned to use Jesus's language.

That examination is not pleasant. It implicates me. The churches I have led have been more accessible to the comfortable than to the vulnerable, in ways I was not always aware of and in ways I was sometimes aware of and chose not to address because addressing it would have cost something I was not ready to spend.

The question is not whether the vulnerable should find us. They are not required to find us. The question is whether we will find them — as the shepherd found the sheep, as the woman found the coin, as the father ran toward the son who was still a long way off (Luke 15). The running is always toward. That is the theological direction. The church that has reversed it and is sitting still, waiting for the excluded to close the distance, may be waiting for something that the Gospel does not promise will happen.

Go. Know names. Be changed by what you find. That is not a program. It is a posture. And it is the posture the most vulnerable people in your city have not yet seen from most of the churches in it.
