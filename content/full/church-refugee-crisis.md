Before the policy arguments and the border statistics and the think-tank reports, there is a child. Eight years old, or eleven, or four. Sleeping in a camp or a detention facility or a church basement three thousand miles from the city where she learned to walk. She did not choose the war that emptied her neighborhood. She did not choose the drought that made the crops fail or the gang that made the streets impassable after dark. She is here because staying was not survivable, and she is here because the alternatives were all terrible and this one was the least terrible. She is a refugee. And whether the church sees her as a theological problem or a theological test will say more about the church's actual convictions than fifty years of doctrinal statements.

The refugee is not a modern category. The Bible begins with displacement.

Adam and Eve are expelled from the garden. Cain becomes a fugitive and wanderer. Abraham is commanded to leave everything he knows for a land he has never seen. Jacob flees his brother's rage. Joseph is sold and transported as a slave. The people of Israel spend four hundred years as strangers in a land that is not theirs, in conditions that begin as hospitality and end as bondage. Ruth is a foreigner in a field that does not belong to her. Naomi is a woman with no land rights, surviving on the mercy of a system designed for people exactly like her. The Psalms are full of the language of exile: *By the rivers of Babylon we sat and wept* (Psalm 137:1). *How can we sing the songs of the Lord while in a foreign land?* (Psalm 137:4).

This is not background color to the biblical story. It is the story. The God of Israel is the God who knows what it means to be displaced, because the people who carried his story knew it in their bones. The law they were given at Sinai was shaped by that knowledge: *Do not oppress a foreigner; you yourselves know how it feels to be foreigners, because you were foreigners in Egypt* (Exodus 23:9). The repeated command is rooted not in abstract ethics but in remembered experience. You know this. You have been this. You will not do to others what was done to you.

The word translated "foreigner" or "stranger" in most English versions is the Hebrew *ger* — the resident alien, the one without full legal standing in the community, dependent on the community's willingness to extend the protections that citizens carry automatically [cite — confirm]. The Torah provisions for the *ger* are extensive: gleaning rights (Leviticus 19:9-10), legal protection (Deuteronomy 24:17), inclusion in the Sabbath rest (Exodus 20:10), participation in the covenant festivals (Deuteronomy 16:11). These are not charitable extras. They are built into the legal structure of the community. The *ger* is not an object of pity. The *ger* is a participant, protected by law, included in the life of the people.

When Jesus stands in the synagogue at Nazareth and reads from Isaiah 61 — *the Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he has anointed me to proclaim good news to the poor* — the list that follows is not random (Luke 4:18-19). The poor, the captive, the blind, the oppressed, the one in the year of the Lord's favor. These are the categories the prophetic tradition had always attached to God's deepest concern. When he tells the parable of the sheep and goats in Matthew 25, the test is not theological correctness. The test is whether the people fed the hungry, gave drink to the thirsty, welcomed the stranger (*xenos* — the foreign one, the one who does not belong), clothed the naked, cared for the sick, visited the imprisoned. *I was a stranger and you welcomed me* (Matthew 25:35). The incarnation runs precisely here: God is found in the face of the displaced person. Not symbolically. Literally.

The counter-argument is political and it deserves honest engagement. The state has legitimate interests in controlling borders. The church is not the state. Questions of immigration policy are genuinely complex, involving security concerns, economic effects, rule of law, and the management of finite resources. Christians can hold different views on specific policy questions and be faithful. The church should not pretend these complexities do not exist or that there is a single policy position that the Gospel mandates.

But there is a prior question, and that question is not political. It is theological. Do you believe that the person seeking refuge bears the image of God? Do you believe that God's stated concern for the displaced is normative for the people who bear God's name? Do you believe that Matthew 25 was written about real people in real conditions and that its claims have real consequences for how you behave? If you answer yes to those questions, the policy arguments begin from a different place. The question is no longer whether the church should respond but how — not whether the displaced person's humanity commands a response but what form that response should take given the complexities of the context.

The church in the first three centuries did not have the luxury of deciding this question abstractly. It was the church — not the empire, not the temples of the old gods — that built the first systematic infrastructure for caring for strangers in late antiquity [cite — confirm]. Julian the Apostate, the Roman emperor who tried to revive classical paganism in the fourth century, complained bitterly that the Christians were out-caring his priests: they supported not only their own poor but other people's poor as well. The reputation of the early church was not built on its theological precision, however real that was. It was built on the fact that when plague swept through a city, the Christians stayed and the others fled. When abandoned infants were left on the rubbish heaps of Rome — a common practice in the ancient world — the Christians picked them up. When the displaced arrived at the city's edge, the Christians had structures in place to receive them.

That reputation is worth recovering. Not as a strategic move for institutional credibility — though it would produce that. But because it is what bearing the name of Jesus requires.

What does it look like concretely?

It looks like the congregation that learns the difference between a refugee, an asylum seeker, and an undocumented immigrant — not because the legal category determines the worth of the person but because knowing the actual situation is the beginning of actually helping rather than performing concern. It looks like the pastor who has a relationship with the resettlement agency in his city and knows which families arrived last month and what they specifically need. It looks like the church that does not just donate to the food pantry that serves refugee families but sends its members to sit at the table, to learn names, to witness the specific shape of the specific loss.

It looks like the congregation that speaks, when speaking is costly. The political environment around immigration has become so charged that many churches have made a studied decision to say nothing, to avoid the controversy, to keep everyone in the room. The silence feels like wisdom. It is capitulation. When a community of people bearing the image of God is being dehumanized in public discourse, the church's silence is not neutrality. It is consent. The prophets did not choose silence because the political climate was unfavorable.

It looks like the pastor who has read Leviticus 19 carefully and knows that the command to love the stranger as yourself — *love him as yourself, for you were strangers in Egypt* (Leviticus 19:34) — is not a minor provision in an obscure law code. It is the same chapter that contains the command Jesus called the second greatest: love your neighbor as yourself (Leviticus 19:18). The stranger is the neighbor. The refugee is the neighbor. This is not a liberal political position. It is the text.

The global refugee crisis is not primarily a crisis of borders or policy. It is a crisis of imagination — a failure of the human imagination to hold the humanity of people who are far away, who speak differently, whose children do not look like our children. The church's peculiar resource in this moment is not political influence or institutional prestige. It is the Gospel's capacity to expand the imagination. To make real what policy has made abstract. To put a name on what statistics have made numerical. To say, with the specificity that only direct contact produces: this is Maria. This is her son. This is what they left and what it cost them to leave it. This is what they need from us.

The watching world is not persuaded by the church's doctrinal statements. It is persuaded by what the church does with its body and its resources when the cost of doing it is real.

Before the policy arguments, there is a child. The church's answer to that child will be the church's answer to the question Matthew 25 has already asked.
