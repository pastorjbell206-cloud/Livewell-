The pro-life movement began with a conviction worth defending: that human life, from its earliest form, bears the image of God and deserves protection. That conviction is right. What has happened to it is not.

Somewhere the movement narrowed. Opposition to abortion became the full expression of a pro-life ethic, rather than its starting point. And when the definition of pro-life stops at birth, you do not have a theology of life. You have a political position with theological vocabulary attached to it.

## The Biblical Logic That Does Not Stop at Birth

The Hebrew word for life — chayyim — is almost always plural in the Old Testament. Life, in the biblical imagination, is not a solitary thing. It exists in community, in relationship, in the web of conditions that allow human beings to flourish. When Isaiah calls Israel to "loose the bonds of wickedness" and "let the oppressed go free" (Isaiah 58:6), he is not describing a separate agenda from the care of persons. He is describing what care for persons actually requires.

Psalm 139 makes the case for the unborn with unambiguous clarity. "You formed my inward parts; you knitted me together in my mother's womb" (Psalm 139:13). That is a claim about God's presence and activity in the earliest stages of human life — a claim that the unborn person is not nothing, that God knows and forms and sees before others do.

Proverbs 31:8-9 makes a different but continuous case: "Speak up for those who cannot speak for themselves, for the rights of all who are destitute. Speak up and judge fairly; defend the rights of the poor and needy." The same call to speak for the voiceless that applies to the unborn applies to the child born into poverty, the prisoner facing execution, the refugee without legal standing, the elderly person whose care has become inconvenient.

James 1:27 makes the case for the widow and orphan — the categories of person in the ancient world who had the least structural protection. "Religion that God our Father accepts as pure and faultless is this: to look after orphans and widows in their distress."

These are not in tension with each other. They are the same argument. The logic of the Imago Dei — that every human being bears the image of God — does not produce a selective commitment to human dignity. It produces a comprehensive one, or it is not the argument it claims to be.

## What Consistency Actually Costs

A genuinely consistent pro-life ethic would be recognizable by what it costs. It would require the same passion for the child after birth that it claims for the child before it. It would demand attention to the maternal mortality rate in the United States — which is [cite — confirm] among the highest in the developed world. It would ask hard questions about the foster care system and the hundreds of thousands of children currently in it. It would care about the mothers who chose life in desperate circumstances and found themselves abandoned by the institutions that celebrated their choice.

It would ask uncomfortable questions about capital punishment — not as an automatic position, but as a question that the logic of the Imago Dei generates. If every human life bears God's image, what happens to that argument when the state executes a person? The question is not simple. The consistent pro-life ethic does not pretend it is. But it does not avoid the question either.

It would not stop at the delivery room door.

This is not a liberal critique of conservatism or a conservative critique of liberalism. It is a biblical critique of both. The left reduces the issue by refusing to grant moral weight to the unborn. The right stops too early by refusing to extend its logic to the born. The consistent pro-life position is harder than either side is willing to sustain, because it makes demands that cross every political coalition and comfort every demographic.

## The Counter-Position Stated Fairly

There is an honest argument on the other side, and it deserves to be engaged rather than caricatured. The argument goes like this: political movements require focus to be effective. The pro-life movement chose a specific, achievable goal — the legal protection of unborn life — and pursued it with the kind of singular focus that political organizing requires. Expanding the definition of pro-life to include every form of social support for existing persons is not a broadening of principle; it is a dilution of strategy. You cannot build a coalition around everything.

This argument is pragmatically serious. Political coalitions do require focus. The history of movements that tried to address everything simultaneously is largely a history of movements that accomplished nothing in particular.

But the argument concedes more than it intends to. When it says that the pro-life position is a strategic choice to focus on the unborn, it has already acknowledged that the position is strategic rather than theological. And a strategic position with theological language applied to it is something different from a genuine theology of life. The church's responsibility is not to manage a political strategy. It is to embody a theology. If the theology is comprehensive — and the Imago Dei is comprehensive — then the church's embodiment of it must be comprehensive, even when that is strategically inconvenient.

## Ezekiel's Indictment and the Selective Conscience

Ezekiel 16:49 records God's indictment of Sodom — and it is not the sin most people expect. "She and her daughters were arrogant, overfed and unconcerned; they did not help the poor and needy." The famous failure of Sodom, in God's own accounting, was not merely sexual sin. It was the failure of the prosperous to care for those who had nothing.

That indictment has an application to a movement that is passionate about the rights of the unborn and substantially unconcerned about the conditions into which those unborn children will be born. The church that celebrates a pro-life vote in November and offers nothing for the single mother who chose life in January has demonstrated its actual hierarchy of concerns.

I am inside this indictment. I have been quicker to speak about abortion than to examine my own congregation's material support for the families who are living with the consequences of the convictions I preach. The gap between conviction and practice is not a gap that belongs to the movement out there. It belongs to me.

## The Practical Shape of Consistency

A genuinely consistent pro-life ethic looks like specific things in a specific congregation. It looks like an adoption and foster care ministry that is as organized and funded as the political advocacy. It looks like material support for pregnant women who are afraid — not a pamphlet about pregnancy resources, but actual diapers, actual rent assistance, actual presence. It looks like a congregation that is as passionate about the child in the underfunded school as about the child in the womb.

It looks like a willingness to ask the hard political questions that the consistent ethic generates, even when those questions land on the wrong side of the congregation's preferred party. It looks like a pastor willing to preach from Isaiah 58 and Matthew 25 with the same urgency that he preaches against abortion — because they are, in the biblical logic, the same sermon.

None of this requires abandoning the commitment to the unborn. The conviction that the unborn person bears the image of God and deserves protection is not negotiable and should not be softened. What it requires is refusing to let that conviction be the whole of the story — refusing to let the pro-life label cover a posture that is actually quite selective about which lives it is willing to defend.

## The Question That Remains

The question is not whether you are pro-life. The question is whether your pro-life conviction is selective enough to be comfortable — or demanding enough to be true.

The consistent ethic does not leave you with a cleaner political identity. It leaves you with a more honest theological one — the identity of a person who has followed the logic of the Imago Dei wherever it leads, and who has found that it leads to obligations no single party is willing to fund and no single constituency is comfortable hearing. That person is harder to place on the political map. He is easier to recognize in the pages of the prophets.

Demanding enough to follow the logic wherever the Imago Dei leads. Demanding enough to support the child before birth and after it. Demanding enough to ask what the church owes the mother who chose life without any help from the people who celebrated her choice. Demanding enough to be uncomfortable in every political coalition, because no coalition is willing to pay the full price of a genuinely consistent theology of human dignity.

That version of the conviction costs something in every direction. It is accused by the right of being soft on abortion and by the left of being soft on choice. It aligns fully with no party platform. It generates enemies across the spectrum.

That is probably the clearest sign that it is right.

The logic of the Imago Dei does not stop at birth. It never did.
