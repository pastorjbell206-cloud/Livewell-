I have five sons. That fact is not incidental to anything I write. It is the weight that keeps my theology honest. Abstract arguments about intergenerational responsibility become very concrete when you can put five names to the generation that will inherit what you leave behind.

The Scripture's concern for those who come after is not a modern environmental sentiment grafted onto an ancient text. It is structural. The Hebrew word for covenant — *berith* — carries a temporal depth that extends past the parties present at its making. When God makes a covenant with Abraham in Genesis 15 and 17, the covenant is explicitly extended to his descendants for generations. The law given at Sinai is given to a generation who will die in the wilderness, for the sake of the generation that will enter the land. Deuteronomy is Moses's long sermon to the children of the people who heard the thunder at Horeb. The audience is already once-removed. The obligations are not diminished.

The Deuteronomic vision of faithfulness is explicitly generational: "These commandments that I give you today are to be on your hearts. Impress them on your children. Talk about them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up" (Deuteronomy 6:6-7). The covenant shapes parenting. It shapes pedagogy. It shapes the structure of daily life, because the stakes are multigenerational and the consequences of forgetting are borne not just by those who forgot but by those who follow.

## The Category the Church Has Avoided

The application of this to climate and to accumulated injustice is uncomfortable for segments of the church, and I want to acknowledge why before making the argument.

The discomfort is partly political. Climate change has become a partisan category in the American context, and any church that touches it risks being heard as aligning with one political party rather than making a theological claim. I understand the wariness. I have felt it. I have also come to believe that the wariness has caused us to surrender the theological category to the political one, which is exactly backwards. The question of what we owe to those who come after us is not a Democrat question or a Republican question. It is a question that Moses asked, that the prophets asked, that every serious theology of covenant has always required an answer to.

The discomfort is also partly economic. The cost of addressing climate change falls, at least in the short term, on people and industries that are part of many congregations' social and economic world. Coal country churches, agricultural congregations, families whose livelihoods are tied to industries whose practices are implicated — these are real pastoral constituencies, and simplistic moralism that ignores their economic reality is not faithfulness. It is cruelty dressed in prophetic language.

I want to steel-man the case for the current approach: maintain political neutrality on contested policy questions, minister to all sides, focus on what Scripture clearly says and leave the contested applications to individual conscience. That is not without wisdom. The church that takes strong institutional positions on every contested political question quickly becomes a partisan organization rather than a community of witness.

But the prophets did not maintain neutrality when the contested policy question was whether Israel would honor the covenant. They named what the generation in front of them was doing to the generations behind them. They named it specifically, with grief and without apology. Elijah named what Ahab's land-seizure policy was doing to Naboth's family (1 Kings 21). Amos named what the merchant class's economic practices were doing to the poor. The claim that prophetic witness must stop at the threshold of contested policy is a claim the prophets themselves did not accept.

## What the Prophets Said About Time

Isaiah's vision of redemption is consistently multigenerational. "You will be like a well-watered garden, like a spring whose waters never fail. Your people will rebuild the ancient ruins and will raise up the age-old foundations; you will be called Repairer of Broken Walls, Restorer of Streets with Dwellings" (Isaiah 58:11-12). The image is of a people who receive something broken, repair it, and pass it forward. The broken walls were left by the generation before. The restoration is for the generation after.

Psalm 78 is a long meditation on intergenerational faithfulness and failure. "We will not hide them from their descendants; we will tell the next generation the praiseworthy deeds of the Lord, his power, and the wonders he has done" (Psalm 78:4). And immediately following: the story of how every generation that forgot what God had done produced consequences that fell on the generation following. The Psalm is not a comfort. It is a warning. Forgetfulness compounds.

The language of stewardship in Genesis 1 and 2 — the command to tend and keep the garden — is language of responsibility extended through time. The Hebrew *shamar*, to keep or guard, is the same word used of the cherubim guarding Eden (Genesis 3:24) and of the Levites guarding the tabernacle (Numbers 1:53). The gardener does not exhaust the soil. The gardener guards it and passes it forward. The creation does not belong to any generation. It is entrusted to each generation for the sake of the next.

## Inherited Injustice

Systemic racism is the same category of problem, and it belongs in this essay alongside the environmental question because both are fundamentally about what we pass down.

The wealth gap between white and Black families in America is not the product of different choices made in the present tense by different people. It is the accumulated consequence of policy decisions made over generations — the systematic exclusion of Black veterans from the GI Bill's benefits, the redlining of mortgage markets that denied Black families the wealth-building mechanism that built the white middle class, the discriminatory application of New Deal programs that explicitly excluded categories of work dominated by Black laborers [cite — confirm]. The effects compound across time. A family excluded from the housing market in 1950 does not simply recover when the exclusion ends in 1968. The equity they did not build, the inheritance they cannot pass down, the educational investments they cannot make — these extend across three generations and more.

The generation that did not make those decisions inherits their effects and the obligation to address them. This is not guilt for what your grandfather did. It is responsibility for what you have received and what you will pass on.

Nehemiah understood this. When he arrived in Jerusalem and saw the ruined walls, he did not say: I did not tear those walls down, I bear no responsibility for rebuilding them. He said: "I and this people have sinned" (Nehemiah 1:6). He implicated himself in the failure of a generation before his own because he was the one standing in front of the ruins, with the capacity to address them, and the question of what he would do with that capacity was the question in front of him. The historical guilt was distributed. The present responsibility was his.

## What This Looks Like for the Church

The church that takes intergenerational responsibility seriously will not look like the church that operates primarily within a single generation's preferences and horizons. It will make decisions differently.

It will ask, before building a new facility: does this serve the mission of the next generation of this community, or does it serve our preference for comfort and our need for an institutional monument? It will ask, before remaining silent about environmental policy: are we being neutral, or are we protecting the interests of people in this room at the cost of the people who will inherit the damage? It will ask, before treating racial justice as too politically contested to address: are we protecting ourselves, or protecting arrangements that compound inherited disadvantage for children who had nothing to do with the choices that created them?

These are not comfortable questions. They are the questions that Moses was asking when he preached Deuteronomy to a generation that would inherit promises made before they were born and face consequences for choices made by their parents.

## What I Owe My Sons

When I think about what I am leaving for my five sons, I do not think primarily about financial assets or career opportunities or even theological inheritance, though I care about all of those things. I think about whether they will inherit a world where the poor are still crushed by systems we refused to examine, where the earth is more depleted than it needed to be because we chose convenience, where the injustices that were visible in our generation are still unaddressed in theirs because we were too comfortable to speak.

I have done things that belong to that category. I have been silent when silence was easier. I have benefited from arrangements I did not examine. I have let pastoral wariness about political controversy function as a reason to avoid the kinds of conversations that would have required something from me and from the congregation. That is not a confession I make to feel better. It is a statement of where the obligation begins.

The Deuteronomic tradition teaches that the most dangerous word in any generation is the word "forget." Forget what God did. Forget what it cost. Forget who you were before the rescue. Forget who is still in the condition you were delivered from. Forgetting is not neutral. It is the mechanism by which every generation produces the injustice that the next generation inherits.

The question before us is not whether we will leave a legacy. We will. Every generation does, in the decisions it makes and the decisions it refuses. The question is what kind — what we built, what we failed to build, what we repaired, what we damaged further, what we had the capacity to address and chose not to.

The ones who come after us will inherit the answer to that question.

They are coming. They are already here in some cases, carrying the consequences of choices made before they understood the choices.

It begins with me. It ends with the ones who come after.

The distance between those two points is the measure of what faithfulness requires.
