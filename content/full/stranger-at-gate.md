The Hebrew word is *ger* — the resident alien, the one who lives among the people of Israel without belonging to the kinship networks that provide protection and standing. Not a visitor passing through. Not an enemy combatant. The *ger* is the person who has come to live among you, who has made a life in the community, who is present and vulnerable, who has no one at the city gate to advocate for him in the legal disputes that determined whether you kept your land, your freedom, and your family.

In the ancient Near Eastern world, the city gate was where commerce was conducted, where legal disputes were adjudicated, where the elders sat to render judgment (Ruth 4:1-11). To have advocates at the gate was to have standing in society. The widow had lost hers when her husband died. The orphan had never had any. The *ger* — the resident alien — arrived without the kinship connections that made advocacy possible. He was present but not protected. In the community but not of it in the way that generated legal standing.

It is for this person that the law of Israel makes repeated, detailed, insistent provision.

## The Shape of the Law

Leviticus 19:33-34 states: "When a foreigner resides among you in your land, do not mistreat them. The foreigner residing among you must be treated as your native-born. Love them as yourself, for you were foreigners in Egypt. I am the Lord your God." This is not a general hospitality principle. It is a specific legal command with a theological rationale: you know what it means to be the stranger. You lived it in Egypt. That memory is the basis of the obligation.

Deuteronomy returns to this command so many times that it cannot be read as incidental. The tithe every third year includes provision for the *ger* alongside the widow and the orphan (Deuteronomy 14:29). The gleaning laws require that the edges of the field be left unharvested so that the *ger* can eat (Deuteronomy 24:19-21). The Sabbath rest applies to the *ger* as well as to Israel's own people (Deuteronomy 5:14) — which means the alien worker receives the same weekly rest that the Israelite landowner receives, a provision that directly limits the economic exploitation of vulnerable labor. The stranger is woven into the fabric of the covenant community's economic and social life, not as an object of charity, but as a participant with legal standing and legal protections.

The rationale is always the same: you were foreigners in Egypt. The Exodus is not merely a theological event. It is the founding memory that shapes Israel's ethical obligations for the rest of her history. The people who were enslaved in a foreign land, who were vulnerable and powerless, who had no advocate at the gate — those people were liberated by God. And because God liberated them, they are obligated to never become the kind of community that treats the *ger* the way Egypt treated them.

This theological logic is among the most demanding in the entire Old Testament. It is not asking Israel to be generous to strangers out of benevolence. It is asking Israel to remember its own history with enough clarity that the sight of a vulnerable stranger activates the same obligation God demonstrated toward them when they were in that position. Justice rooted in memory. The obligation flows from the story, and the story is not allowed to be forgotten.

## The Prophetic Indictment

When Israel forgets, the prophets name it. Ezekiel 22:7 includes the mistreatment of the foreigner in a list of sins that constitute the grounds for Jerusalem's destruction: "In you they have treated father and mother with contempt; in you they have oppressed the foreigner and mistreated the fatherless and the widow." The *ger* is mentioned in the same breath as the widow and orphan — the other figures without advocates at the gate. Jeremiah 7:6 places doing wrong to the foreigner alongside shedding innocent blood as conditions of God's continued presence in the temple. Zechariah 7:10 commands: "Do not oppress the widow or the fatherless, the foreigner or the poor."

The prophets are not speaking abstractly. They are naming specific economic practices that stripped the *ger* of resources and left them without recourse — the wage theft, the land encroachment, the debt arrangements that turned vulnerability into permanent servitude. The law of Moses was attempting to build a society structurally unlike Egypt. The prophets are pointing to the places where Israel has, over centuries, rebuilt the Egyptian arrangements under a different name.

The God of the Old Testament is consistently described as the protector of those who have no human protector. Psalm 146:9 is a brief but total statement: "The Lord watches over the foreigner and sustains the fatherless and the widow, but he frustrates the ways of the wicked." God is positioned at the gate, on behalf of those who have no one else there. To be the community of God, in this account, is to share that positioning — to place yourself at the gate in behalf of the one who cannot place themselves there.

## The New Testament Extension

The New Testament does not retire this obligation. Jesus's parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37) takes the boundaries of the *ger* obligation and pushes them past any kinship or ethnic category entirely: the neighbor is not defined by who they are but by who is in front of you in need. And Revelation's vision of the renewed Jerusalem includes "every nation, tribe, people and language" gathered before the throne (Revelation 7:9). The stranger at the gate of the earthly community is, in the eschatological vision, invited to the center of the heavenly one.

Matthew 25 brings this full circle: "I was a stranger and you invited me in" (Matthew 25:35). Not "you contributed to a relief fund that served strangers." Not "you voted for policies that technically improved conditions for strangers." You invited me in. The personal, particular, costly hospitality that the Levitical law envisioned — where the stranger is your native-born, where you love them as yourself — is the thing Jesus names as the criterion of judgment. The encounter with the stranger is the encounter with Christ. The welcome or refusal is rendered, in this account, to him.

## The Steel-Man Position

The serious objection deserves engagement: national sovereignty requires the ability to regulate borders, and the biblical category of the *ger* does not map cleanly onto contemporary immigration debates that involve complicated questions of legal process, national security, and economic impact. Reasonable people who take Scripture seriously disagree about the specific policies that should govern immigration. The text does not resolve those debates automatically.

This is true. The Levitical law was written for a covenant community, not for a modern nation-state, and direct application requires careful work.

But the objection is used, most commonly, to do something the text does not permit: to move from the complexity of policy to silence about the person. Whatever the correct immigration policy is, the person who has arrived — the *ger* who is present in the community, working, living, raising children — is protected by this sustained biblical concern. The law's specific provisions are translatable even when its specific mechanisms are not. The *ger* must be treated as your native-born. The foreigner must not be mistreated. You must love them as yourself.

The complexity of immigration policy does not release the church from the obligation to the immigrant in front of it. The complexity is real. The obligation is also real. The church that uses the one to avoid the other has made a choice that the text will not ratify.

## What the Church Has Done

I have watched the church in America decide, in recent decades, that its response to the question of strangers and immigrants is primarily a political position rather than a biblical one. I have made that calculation myself — weighing the political complexity against the textual clarity and concluding that the complexity was reason enough to stay quiet. That is not neutrality. It is a choice, and it has a cost that I have not always been willing to name.

The churches in my denomination have people in their pews who hold strong opinions about immigration enforcement. They have people whose families include undocumented members. They have people who came here as strangers and built lives. They have people who are afraid of the stranger and people who are the stranger. Navigating that pastoral reality is genuinely hard, and I do not want to pretend it is easy.

But the text is not hard to read. Leviticus 19:33-34 is not ambiguous. Deuteronomy 24:19 is not ambiguous. Ezekiel 22:7 is not ambiguous. The theological rationale — you were foreigners in Egypt — is not a complex hermeneutical puzzle. It is a direct appeal to a founding memory that the church has largely allowed to be overwritten by political identity.

The memory that Israel was commanded to maintain — that they had been the *ger*, that they knew the vulnerability from the inside, that God had been their advocate when they had no other — is available to the church in a different form. The church exists because God chose the stranger. Abraham left his country and kindred and went to a land he did not know (Genesis 12:1). Israel spent four hundred years as the stranger in a foreign land. Jesus was a refugee in Egypt (Matthew 2:13-14). The disciples scattered after the resurrection and took the gospel to nations that had not asked for it, making themselves strangers in service of the God who had welcomed them.

The church is not a settled community that receives strangers as a courtesy. It is, in its deepest identity, a community of strangers — people who confess that they have "no permanent city" (Hebrews 13:14) and who look for the city that is to come. The welcome of the stranger is not a program the church considers alongside other options. It is a recognition of what the church itself is.

The stranger is at the gate. The text is clear about what is required. The question is whether the community that claims to live by that text will remember what it was when it had no one at the gate to advocate for it.

The obligation has not changed. The gate is still the same gate.
