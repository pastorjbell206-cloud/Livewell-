A pastor asked me recently whether it was wrong to use artificial intelligence to write his sermons. I did not answer immediately. Not because the question was complicated technically, but because I was trying to locate what it was actually asking underneath the surface — and what I found there was older than any algorithm.

The question was really this: what does it mean to be called to preach?

---

The ministry of the word has always rested on a double claim. First, that the Scripture is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, able to divide and discern in ways that no technique can replicate (Hebrews 4:12). Second, that the preacher who stands before a congregation to open that Scripture has been formed — by study, by suffering, by encounter with the text over years — in ways that make his voice particular and irreplaceable. The congregation does not gather on Sunday to receive a competent communication of correct theological content. They gather to hear a human being, wounded and restored like them, who has wrestled with this text in the dark and come back to tell them what he found.

This is not a romantic notion. It is an ecclesiological claim rooted in the specific way the New Testament describes the task of proclamation. When Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 4:7 about "treasure in jars of clay," he is making a deliberate point about the medium through which the gospel travels: the weakness of the vessel is not a problem to be engineered away but the very condition under which the power of God becomes visible. The preacher who has been through the valley of the shadow of death and who speaks about Psalm 23 from inside that experience communicates something that no algorithmically optimized content can carry. The weight is in the clay, not the gold.

Artificial intelligence can produce content that is theologically accurate. It can organize an argument, identify relevant texts, and construct prose that reads smoothly. A capable model can produce, in minutes, a sermon outline that covers the exegetical bases, names the right commentators, and moves through application with the formal logic that homiletics textbooks describe. It can write better, in the narrow technical sense of grammatical correctness and logical coherence, than many preachers who have been in the pulpit for thirty years.

What it cannot do is spend Wednesday afternoon with a dying deacon and then bring the weight of that room into Sunday's exposition of John 11. What it cannot do is carry the specific grief and specific hope of a specific community into the particular encounter with Scripture that preaching, at its best, is. The machine has no Wednesday afternoon. It has no dying deacon. It has never stood at a graveside in February in Michigan and tried to say something true.

---

I am not arguing that pastors should not use these tools at all. I have used them. I have asked AI tools to summarize a commentary, to suggest parallel passages, to help me organize a complicated theological argument, to identify where a line of reasoning might be challenged. These are research tasks. They are not preaching. The distinction is analogous to the difference between using a concordance and having the concordance write your sermon. The concordance is a tool for finding what exists. The sermon is what you do with what you found, after you have lived with it.

The distinction matters because what erodes in AI-assisted preaching is not immediately obvious. The pastor who begins outsourcing the generative labor of sermon preparation — the sustained wrestle with the text, the search for the right word, the willingness to sit with what the passage demands before rushing to what it offers — does not suddenly become a fraud. He becomes, over time, something subtler and more dangerous: a communicator who no longer has anything of his own to say. The congregation eventually feels this, even if they cannot name it. They receive information. They do not receive witness.

It is worth asking why the outsourcing is tempting, because the temptation has a specific shape for pastors. The pastor is producing a significant piece of public communication every week, under the constraint of a demanding schedule, with no co-author and no editorial process, for an audience that has every reason to expect consistent quality. That constraint is real. The week the board meeting runs long, the family crisis lands on Thursday, the pastoral care situation that was supposed to be straightforward turns complicated by Wednesday evening — these are not hypothetical. They are the actual texture of pastoral ministry. The AI that can produce a serviceable sermon outline in forty-five minutes is genuinely attractive when the alternative is Friday morning with nothing on the page and Sunday coming.

But that attractiveness is itself diagnostic. The week that the sermon has to be outsourced because there was no time to prepare it is a week in which the pastor was not able to do the central work of his calling. The problem is not solved by outsourcing. The problem is a schedule that has crowded out the most important thing with things that seemed more urgent.

---

This is not new technology producing a new problem. The problem is as old as the difference between a scribe and a prophet. The scribe copies what has been written. The prophet has stood in the council of God and speaks from that encounter. Jeremiah did not dictate from a manual. He wept in chapter 20 because the word was in him like a fire he could not hold back, and the speaking cost him everything. That fire is not generated. It is given — given through the kind of slow, costly, personal formation that cannot be automated.

Bonhoeffer, in his lectures on homiletics at the Finkenwalde seminary in 1935 and 1936 [cite — confirm], insisted that the preacher's primary qualification was not rhetorical skill or theological knowledge but the willingness to speak as one who had heard. The sermon that the congregation needed was not a tour through biblical scholarship but a word — a specific word, for this community, at this moment, addressed by a person who had stood before God on behalf of the people and returned with something to say. That account of preaching is not compatible with a process in which the first draft comes from a language model.

---

There is a counter-argument here that deserves engagement, because it comes from a place of genuine pastoral concern rather than intellectual laziness. The counter-argument goes: "Many pastors, particularly bi-vocational pastors and pastors of small congregations with very limited time, are using AI to help them produce sermons that are better — more theologically careful, more structurally clear — than they could produce with the time available to them. Is it not better to give the congregation a good sermon than a poorly prepared one?" 

The argument has force. And I want to engage it honestly rather than dismiss it.

The answer turns on what we mean by "better." A sermon that is more theologically polished but less personally witnessed is not obviously better. It may be, in a narrow technical sense, more correct. It will not necessarily be more alive. The congregation that needs to hear about the death and resurrection of hope does not primarily need theological accuracy about eschatology. It needs a person who has lost hope and found it again to stand up and say so. That kind of sermon is not improved by artificial intelligence. It is undermined by it, because the personal witness is the whole point.

The bi-vocational pastor working sixty hours a week at his day job and trying to pastor a congregation on the side is not helped by better AI output. He is helped by a theological recalibration that tells him: you are allowed to preach a shorter, less polished, more honest sermon. The congregation that gets a fifteen-minute word from a man who actually lives among them and has thought about this text for thirty concentrated minutes will receive something more valuable than a forty-minute sermon optimized by a language model that has never met any of them.

---

The pastoral question AI raises is not primarily "how do we use this tool responsibly?" That is a legitimate question with practical answers: use AI for research, not proclamation; use it for background work, not for the word itself; maintain the discipline of original engagement with the text before turning to any tool.

The deeper question is: am I still doing the things that form a preacher, or have I begun substituting efficient output for the irreplaceable labor of actual encounter with God and with the people I serve?

That question does not have an AI answer. It has a Thursday afternoon answer — the hours given to the text before the sermon, the prayer before the study, the deacon's bedside before the pulpit. The formation that makes preaching something other than content delivery is not a product of any technology. It is a product of the slow, mostly invisible work of a life genuinely given to the calling.

The congregation that receives a sermon a human being actually wrestled through — even if it is rougher, even if the transitions are less polished, even if it leaves a few exegetical questions unresolved — receives something the most sophisticated language model cannot produce: a witness. A person who went somewhere hard and came back changed.

The tools are new. The calling is not.

And the calling, finally, cannot be outsourced without ceasing to be what it is.
