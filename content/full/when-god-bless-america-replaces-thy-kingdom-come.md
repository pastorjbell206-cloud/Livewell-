Patriotism is not a sin. Let that be said first, clearly, and without apology. The love of a particular place, a particular people, a particular inheritance — this is not idolatry. It is not wrong to weep at a flag, to feel the weight of what your nation's better moments have cost. The love of the proximate — the people placed near you by birth and history — is a kind of love the tradition has long recognized as ordered and right.

But there is a point where patriotism stops being love of country and starts being faith in country, and that crossing is one of the defining spiritual catastrophes of American church history. We have been crossing it for more than two centuries without noticing what we were doing, because the crossing is gradual, and gradual crossings are the hardest kind to name.

The Kingdom of God is the organizing center of Jesus's entire proclamation. Not America, not democracy, not Western civilization — the Kingdom. The Sermon on the Mount is not a speech about good governance. The Lord's Prayer is not a civic liturgy. When Jesus says "Thy kingdom come," he is not describing a project that requires congressional approval or a movement that belongs to one nation's story. He is announcing a sovereignty that stands over all other sovereignties, judges all other sovereignties, and will, in the end, outlast all other sovereignties. That is the thing we have quietly, incrementally, comfortably replaced with "God Bless America."

The replacement did not begin with malice. It began with gratitude — genuine, understandable, earned gratitude for a nation that provided unusual freedoms and unusual stability, that allowed the church to build and preach and organize without the kind of state persecution that had characterized Christian life in most of Christian history. That gratitude is appropriate. But gratitude, when it is not held under something larger than itself, curdles into entitlement, and entitlement hardens into theology. By the early twentieth century, the equation was already structural: many American pulpits preached a gospel in which the blessings of democracy and the blessings of Christ were assumed to be the same blessings, aimed at the same people, advancing the same project.

Robert Bellah wrote in 1967 about what he called American civil religion — the set of beliefs, symbols, and rituals that function as a national faith, drawing on but not identical to Christianity, in which America plays a special role in God's purposes, its founding documents treated as quasi-sacred texts, its wars interpreted as providential, its prosperity as divine favor. [cite — confirm Bellah's 1967 essay "Civil Religion in America"] That analysis was correct then. It has deepened since. The civil religion and the church have become harder to distinguish in large sectors of American Christianity, not because Christians are hypocrites but because the merger was slow enough that no single generation had to make a conscious choice for it.

I swam in that water. I preached in it, with more confidence than I had earned, telling the story in which the American experiment was the continuation of God's purposes rather than one more chapter in the long, unfinished history of nations rising and falling under his gaze. I was wrong. The wrongness was comfortable enough that I had little incentive to notice it. When the comfort of a theological position and the comfort of your social location align, you do not feel the weight of the error. You feel at home.

Here is what the replacement has cost the church, concretely.

When the Kingdom of God is the organizing principle of Christian life, the Sermon on the Mount operates as public ethics. The poor have a claim on you that your voting bloc does not. The stranger matters as much as the citizen — because the Kingdom makes no distinction between them, and Jesus's own account of judgment in Matthew 25 describes a God who identifies himself with the hungry, the stranger, the naked, the sick, the imprisoned. Not the powerful. Not the comfortable. The ones at the bottom of every social hierarchy. That is where Jesus locates himself in the final accounting.

But when patriotism becomes the practical savior — when the survival of American freedom becomes the thing we are actually working for, the prize the church is positioned to protect — the Sermon on the Mount becomes inconvenient. You spiritualize it. You defer it to the afterlife or the individual interior. You give it a private domain that does not touch the public square where the real decisions are made. The enemy's dignity cannot be honored if the enemy is what we are defending ourselves against. The poor have no special claim if the whole framework is about protecting what you have built.

The prophets of Israel never made that move. Amos did not tell the wealthy of Samaria that their economic predations were a private matter between themselves and God, outside the scope of the nation's public life. Isaiah did not suggest that the oppression of the poor was a question of personal morality that had nothing to do with the structure of the kingdom. Micah did not separate mishpat — the public establishment of right order — from the daily life of the nation. The prophets assumed that God had opinions about how the community organized itself, and that those opinions had consequences, and that the consequences were not requests. The whole prophetic tradition is premised on the claim that the God of Israel is sovereign over the nation, judges the nation, and is not obligated to protect the nation if the nation violates the covenant.

The American church, when it chose nationalism as its organizing principle, lost access to the prophets. Not formally. We still read them. But we read them as poetry about ancient Israel, sealed off from any analogical claim on our own national life. We have domesticated the fire.

The strongest counter-argument is worth honoring. Ordered love, as Augustine argued, begins with what is nearest — family, community, nation — and works outward. [cite — confirm Augustine on ordered love] The claim that Christians should have no particular attachment to their nation confuses universal love with abstract indifference and ends up serving neither God nor neighbor. And the specific goods of the liberal democratic order — freedom of speech, freedom of worship, procedural protections against tyranny — are genuine goods that Christians have benefited from and that deserve gratitude and some degree of defense. These are not nothing.

They are real goods. They are not ultimate goods. And the moment we treat proximate goods as ultimate goods, we commit the oldest error in the human story — idolatry. The error is not loving your country. The error is loving your country with the kind of love that belongs only to God, trusting your country with a trust that belongs only to God, and organizing your public life around the survival of your country in a way that crowds out the organizing claim of the Kingdom. When the flag is in the sanctuary, when the worship service includes pledges to the republic, when the church's political witness is indistinguishable from the platform of one of its parties, something has gone profoundly wrong at the level of what the church actually believes.

Lesslie Newbigin, writing after decades as a missionary in India and watching the European church from outside, identified the fundamental crisis of Western Christianity as the privatization of faith — the removal of the gospel from its claim on public life and public order, which he traced in part to the church's accommodation to whatever order it happened to inhabit. [cite — confirm Newbigin's argument in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society*] The American version of that accommodation is nationalist Christianity — the church that has identified its survival with the nation's survival and its health with the nation's health, and that therefore cannot speak a prophetic word to the nation without undermining its own institutional position.

That is not the church Jesus prayed for in John 17. That is not the community that Acts 2 describes — a community that cut across every national and ethnic line, that had no nation to protect because its Lord had already been executed by the most powerful nation in the world, and whose hope was not the preservation of an existing order but the coming of a new one.

I came to faith from atheism. I had no inherited loyalty to any version of Christian nationalism; I came in as an outsider, and I can still see the shape of the thing when I stand back from it. What I see is a church that has let the flag and the cross become confused — not through bad intention, but through the long, slow substitution of a proximate good for the ultimate one.

The Kingdom of God does not need America. America, like every nation, needs the Kingdom. Not to be blessed by it in the sense of preserved and prospered — but to be judged by it, corrected by it, and called, at least in its best moments, toward the justice and mercy that are not American values first and Christian values second but the character of the God who made every nation and will outlast them all.

The Lord's Prayer is not addressed to the republic. It is addressed to a Father whose name is to be made holy, whose Kingdom is to come, whose will is to be done — on earth as in heaven. That prayer is either the organizing center of everything we do, or it is a liturgical habit that doesn't cost us anything.

Which one it is, is showing.
