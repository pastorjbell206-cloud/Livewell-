The congregation learns what faith looks like from the pulpit. This is not an exaggeration — it is simply how communities form. What gets preached, repeatedly, shapes what the people believe is possible, what they think God is like, and what they understand Christianity to actually require of them. Which means that the patterns a preacher falls into — over months, over years — do more formative damage than any single bad sermon ever could.

I have preached these patterns. I am naming them from inside, not from above.

Before the habits themselves: the conditions that produce them. The preacher is not lazy who falls into any of these patterns. He is, in most cases, someone who learned his craft under particular pressures — the pressure to keep people engaged, to avoid Sunday-morning conflict, to be liked enough to be trusted, to serve a congregation that responds well to certain things and noticeably less well to others. Over time, those pressures reshape what preaching becomes. What began as genuine pastoral care calcifies into something safer, shorter, and smaller. The damage accumulates in the congregation's bones before anyone notices it in the sermon.

Knowing the historical weight behind the pulpit helps name what is at stake. Augustine's *De Doctrina Christiana* — written in the late fourth and early fifth centuries — understood preaching as requiring three tasks: to teach, to delight, and to move [cite — confirm]. Delight is not the enemy of substance. But when delight becomes the primary purpose, teaching and movement atrophy. The congregation is pleased. It is not being formed. There is a very specific kind of congregational health that registers as contentment on Sunday and shows up as fragility on Tuesday. The patterns below are what produce it.

**The first is the clean application turn.** The text does its work — raises the question, names the tension, maybe even lets the darkness gather — and then the sermon pivots too quickly to the resolution. Three steps. A practical response. The road home clearly marked. What this communicates to the congregation, over time, is that the Bible is primarily a problem-solving document, that faith is primarily about technique, and that anyone still living in the unreceived part of the promise is probably doing something wrong. Paul wrote Philippians from a Roman prison cell (Philippians 1:13), and the letter does not resolve his situation. It holds it. Paul can write "I have learned, in whatever state I am, to be content" (Philippians 4:11) not because the prison opened but because the prison did not have the last word. Preaching that always resolves teaches people that unresolved lives are off the map.

The church has been down this road before. The prosperity gospel is the extreme form of the clean application turn — if you pray and believe correctly, the situation will resolve. But the milder version is everywhere, and it does the same theological damage at a lower dosage. Lamentations exists. Job exists. Psalm 88 ends with darkness, the last word unredeemed. The canon insists on the territory that quick resolutions foreclose. So must the pulpit.

**The second is therapeutic substitution.** The language of psychology is not wrong. But when the sermon consistently translates Scripture into psychological terms — your worth, your healing, your growth process — without also holding the demands of the text, it has shifted the organizing category from the kingdom of God to the self. The congregation begins to read the Bible primarily as a mirror rather than as a window onto something larger than themselves.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer, writing from Germany in the 1930s, named the danger of a Christianity so accommodated to the surrounding culture that it could no longer speak a word the culture had not already authorized [cite — confirm]. Therapeutic preaching is not the German church of the 1930s. But the structural problem is the same: when the sermon is shaped by what the surrounding therapeutic culture has already made acceptable, it cannot speak the word that that culture cannot hear — the word about repentance, about the demands of the kingdom, about the cross as something other than a metaphor for your healing arc. You cannot be formed by a mirror. Formation requires encounter with what is genuinely other.

**The third is intellectual avoidance.** The text has a hard edge — a demand, a claim, a tension with the congregation's received politics or economics or family arrangements. And the sermon slides around it. It is not dishonest, exactly. It simply never arrives at the place where the text becomes genuinely costly. Over time, the congregation learns that the Bible is safe. That it will not ask anything they are not already prepared to give.

The prophets were not expelled from the sanctuary for being wrong. Amos 7:10–13 records Amaziah, the priest of Bethel, telling Amos to go preach somewhere else — not because Amos was theologically incoherent but because what he was saying was inconvenient to the existing arrangement. The preacher who slides around the inconvenient text is not being kind. He is being Amaziah. He is protecting a comfort that the text was sent to disturb. A faith that never costs anything eventually means nothing. The congregation did not come to hear what they already believe confirmed. They came, whether they know it or not, to hear the word that is alive and active and sharper than a two-edged sword (Hebrews 4:12).

**The fourth is the celebrity proof-text.** The sermon is built not from the biblical argument but from the accumulated authority of popular Christian voices — this well-known pastor said, that beloved author wrote. The congregation's faith becomes anchored to a personality rather than to the text itself. When that personality eventually falls — and some percentage of them always do — the faith built on their words falls with them.

The Bereans in Acts 17:11 are commended specifically because they tested Paul's preaching against the Scriptures themselves. Not because they distrusted Paul — they received the word eagerly. But they received it and then examined the Scriptures to see whether what Paul said was true. The congregation trained to defer to authority rather than to examine the text has been given a weakened faith. The current generation of young adults who have watched celebrity Christian culture implode — scandals, cover-ups, collapsed movements [cite — confirm] — can tell the difference between a sermon anchored in Scripture and a sermon anchored in a personality. The former will outlast the latter. The pastoral work is to build the faith that lasts.

**The fifth is systematic avoidance of lament.** The psalms of lament — and there are roughly sixty of them, constituting the largest single category in the Psalter [cite — confirm] — represent a theological territory that the American pulpit has almost entirely abandoned. Psalm 88 gives a full chapter of unanswered complaint, ending without resolution: *darkness is my closest friend.* Not as a setup for triumph. Simply as the psalmist's condition, named honestly before God.

The preacher who only celebrates, who frames every darkness as a setup for triumph, who cannot sit with genuine suffering and call it by name, creates a congregation ill-equipped to process their own lives when those lives refuse to resolve. Grief is not a phase. Doubt is not a failure. Anger at God has a scriptural tradition, and Job is not corrected by God for his complaints — he is honored for having spoken what is true in contrast to the friends whose theological tidy explanations were wrong (Job 42:7–8). The preacher who cannot give language to grief and doubt is leaving his people without a map for roughly half of what they will experience. When their lives enter the territory the sermons never described, they will conclude either that their faith is broken or that God has abandoned them. Both conclusions were produced by the pulpit's refusal to go where the canon actually goes.

None of these habits are chosen deliberately. They accumulate under pressure, in the direction of least resistance, shaped by what the congregation appears to respond to and what keeps Sunday morning from feeling like a confrontation. I understand the gravity of that. The pulpit is not a laboratory for the preacher's intellectual interests. It is a place of care.

But care that shrinks the faith is not care. It is management.

The congregation is trying to live inside a world that is genuinely hard. A marriage that will not stabilize. A diagnosis that will not improve. A faith that once felt solid and now feels like it is held together only by habit. They brought all of that into the room this morning. And the question the sermon must answer is not whether they will feel better when they leave but whether they will have been given something true and large enough to carry that weight through the week.

That requires something of the preacher that is not primarily technical. The five habits above can be named, analyzed, and corrected at the level of craft. But the deeper correction is theological: the preacher who has himself lived in the unreceived promise, who has himself named grief honestly before God, who has himself found that the text cost him something — that preacher has been somewhere the congregation needs to go. He can lead them there because he has been there.

Eugene Peterson spent decades arguing that pastoral work is fundamentally a practice of attention — to the text, to the congregation, to the ways the text speaks into actual lives [cite — confirm]. The preacher who has cultivated that attention will not fall into these habits, not because he is more disciplined but because he is more present — present to what the text actually says, to what the congregation actually carries, and to the enormous distance between those two realities that only genuine preaching can close.

The sermon that takes that distance seriously will not be comfortable. It will not resolve everything. It will not make everyone happy. But it will be alive — sharp enough to divide, honest enough to stay in the darkness when the darkness is where the text goes, clear enough to say what it costs and what it promises.

That is what the congregation came for. Most of them know it, even if they would not say so. The preacher's job is to give it to them, even when — especially when — the easier thing would be to stay on the surface and leave everyone undisturbed.

Care that shrinks the faith is not care. It is management. The congregation you are trying to protect from difficulty will not thank you when difficulty arrives and they have nothing to hold. Give them something that will hold.
