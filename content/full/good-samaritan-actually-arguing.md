We have turned the parable of the Good Samaritan into a moral lesson about being helpful. This is one of the more impressive acts of domestication in Christian history.

The parable has been used to name hospitals, homeless shelters, and good deeds of all kinds — a word for the person who stops to help when others walk by. There is nothing wrong with that application except that it misses almost everything the parable is actually doing. Jesus is not teaching a lesson about helpfulness. He is answering a question with a story that was designed to be offensive — designed to expose the way his questioner's entire framework for organizing the world was wrong.

---

The setup requires attention. A lawyer — an expert in the Torah — asks Jesus what he must do to inherit eternal life (Luke 10:25). Jesus asks him what the law says. The man answers correctly: love God with everything you have, and love your neighbor as yourself. Jesus tells him he has answered well.

But then comes the move that tells you everything about the man's actual concern: "And who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29). Luke adds the interpretive gloss that he said this "to justify himself." He is not asking in intellectual curiosity. He is asking because the commandment to love your neighbor has already landed, and he wants to know where the boundary is — how far out the obligation extends before he is free of it. He is asking for the line.

This is one of the oldest moves in the human moral playbook. We acknowledge the obligation in principle and then proceed to define the category of those to whom it applies as narrowly as possible. The question "who is my neighbor?" is the question of a man looking for the edge of the map. If he can locate it, he can love his neighbors and be righteous while remaining untroubled by everyone else.

Jesus tells a story in response. A man is beaten and left dying on the road from Jerusalem to Jericho. A priest passes. A Levite passes. Both see the man. Both go to the other side. Then a Samaritan comes.

---

To hear this as Jesus's audience would have heard it requires understanding who Samaritans were to first-century Jews.

The relationship between Jews and Samaritans was ancient and bitter. It originated in the aftermath of the Assyrian conquest of the northern kingdom in the eighth century BCE, when the Assyrian policy of population transfer brought foreign settlers into the land who intermarried with the remaining Israelites and developed a mixed religious practice centered on Mount Gerizim rather than Jerusalem. By the first century, the animosity was deep and mutual. Jews traveling between Galilee and Judea would often take longer routes to avoid Samaria. The Samaritan woman in John 4 expresses the basic social reality: "Jews do not associate with Samaritans" (John 4:9).

This is not simply a story about crossing a cultural boundary to be kind. In the cultural imagination of Jesus's audience, the Samaritan is the enemy. Not just the stranger, not just the foreigner — the person from whom your community is separated by centuries of mutual contempt and competing religious claims. Choosing him as the hero of the story is not a gentle challenge to cultural preferences. It is a provocation.

---

The priest and the Levite are not villains for the sake of contrast. They are figures whose religious function made them plausible candidates to obey the law of neighbor-love. If the story is about obeying the commandment to love your neighbor, these are the people who should be doing it. Their failure raises a specific question: what kind of religion produces the capacity to walk past a dying man?

The answer embedded in the story is that a religion organized around boundary maintenance — around the careful demarcation of clean and unclean, obligation and non-obligation, neighbor and non-neighbor — produces exactly this capacity. The priest may have had legitimate ritual concerns about contact with a body that might be dead. The Levite may have had similar reasoning. Their religion gave them a framework for not stopping, and they used it.

This is the sharpest edge of the parable: it does not only challenge the lawyer's question about where the boundary is. It suggests that the very desire to locate the boundary — the instinct to ask "who is my neighbor?" as a limiting question rather than an opening one — is the symptom of a religious formation that has gone wrong somewhere fundamental.

---

When Jesus concludes the story, he reverses the lawyer's question. The lawyer asked: "Who is my neighbor?" Jesus asks: "Which of these three do you think was a neighbor to the man who fell into the hands of robbers?" (Luke 10:36). The shift is significant. The question "who is my neighbor?" locates the moral agent at the center and asks which people in the world qualify for his attention. The question "who was a neighbor?" locates the suffering person at the center and asks which person responded to the obligation that the suffering created.

The first question allows you to define the circle. The second question does not. If the obligation is generated by the encounter with suffering, then the only answer to "who is my neighbor?" is: whoever you can help.

Kenneth Bailey, in his careful analysis of Middle Eastern cultural context in the parables, notes that the lawyer was forced into an extraordinary moment: he could not bring himself to say "the Samaritan" and instead answered "the one who showed him mercy" (Luke 10:37). [cite — confirm] Even in defeat, the boundary held in the language. Jesus tells him: go and do likewise. The "likewise" is not a commendation of charity. It is the command to cross the boundary the man's entire world was organized around maintaining.

---

The parable is a justice argument, not a charity argument.

The distinction matters. Charity keeps the charitable person at the center and the suffering person as the recipient. It is a transaction in which the giver maintains control — the ability to define who qualifies, to determine the form and duration of the help, to feel good about the giving without being changed by it. The Samaritan in the parable does not exercise that control. He gets off his donkey. He touches the man. He puts him on his own animal. He takes him to an inn. He pays. He comes back. He absorbs the cost of someone else's suffering into the structure of his own life. That is not charity in the limited sense. That is a restructuring of existence around someone else's need.

The New Testament word used for the Samaritan's response in verse 33 is σπλαγχνίζομαι — the same word used throughout the Gospels for Jesus's own response to crowds, to lepers, to the sick and possessed. It means something visceral and prior to decision — a gut-level response to suffering that precedes the calculation of cost. This is not the compassion of careful moral reasoning. It is the compassion that has been so formed by attention to human need that the sight of it produces immediate physical response.

The parable is arguing that this kind of formation — a formation that crosses ethnic, religious, and social boundaries in response to suffering — is what it means to love your neighbor. Not the careful management of an obligation extended to a carefully defined circle. Not the charity that maintains the giver's control. The willingness to let someone else's emergency become your own.

---

The church has used this parable for two thousand years to justify programs of charitable service. That is not wrong. But it is incomplete.

The parable also asks the question that the lawyer was trying to escape: what is the system that put a man on the road to Jericho, and who profits from the fact that it keeps producing victims? The Samaritan addressed the immediate crisis. That was essential. But the prophetic tradition — the tradition the parable draws on — did not stop at the immediate crisis. Amos, Micah, Isaiah: they asked about the gates where the cheating happened, the courts where the poor had no advocate, the systems that made robbery profitable and victims routine.

The full extension of the parable's argument is not simply: be a Samaritan. It is: ask why the road from Jerusalem to Jericho requires a Samaritan.

That question does not reduce the obligation to stop and help. It extends it. The Samaritan still gets off his donkey. But the community that takes the parable seriously also asks why the road is designed the way it is, who benefits from the design, and what faithfulness requires in the space between the victims.

Jesus told the lawyer to go and do likewise. He was talking about more than first aid.

The lawyer came with a boundary question. He left with a commission. The boundary he wanted to locate is the boundary Jesus refused to draw. The obligation, it turns out, extends to wherever your path intersects with suffering — and the only question left is what you will do when it does.
