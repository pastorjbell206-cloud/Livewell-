The word arrives with immediate weight. In most evangelical conversations I have witnessed, reparations is treated as a political position — one side of a culture war argument — before it is treated as anything theological. This is itself a diagnosis. That the first response of the church to a question this theologically freighted is to reach for political category rather than biblical category tells us something about which text we have been formed by.

The Bible has a great deal to say about restitution. More, in fact, than most American Christians realize. And the biblical account of restitution is not vague. It is specific, structural, and demanding.

---

Begin with Leviticus 6. When someone defrauds another — withholds wages, takes by force, deceives in a transaction — the restoration required is full restitution of the principal plus twenty percent (Leviticus 6:5). Not apology. Not acknowledgment. Twenty percent more than what was taken, paid back to the person from whom it was taken. The law assumes that the harm done by economic exploitation is real and measurable and that the spiritual relationship between the wrongdoer and the wronged cannot be restored without material restoration. "He must make restitution in full, add a fifth of the value to it and give it all to the owner on the day he presents his guilt offering" (Leviticus 6:5). The moral accounting and the material accounting are inseparable.

Zacchaeus, in one of the most structurally significant conversion accounts in the New Testament, demonstrates exactly this understanding. When he encounters Jesus, the first words out of his mouth after announcing his change of heart are material: "Look, Lord! Here and now I give half of my possessions to the poor, and if I have cheated anybody out of anything, I will pay back four times the amount" (Luke 19:8). This exceeds the Levitical requirement. And Jesus's response — "Today salvation has come to this house" — connects the salvation announcement directly to the restitution pledge. The two are not separable in the narrative. The salvation is manifested in the economic turning.

The church that preaches salvation while remaining indifferent to restitution has separated what the New Testament holds together.

---

The specific historical question of African American reparations has a concrete American beginning. In January 1865, General William T. Sherman issued Special Field Order No. 15 [cite — confirm], distributing approximately 400,000 acres of coastal land in South Carolina and Georgia to formerly enslaved people — roughly forty acres per family, the origin of the "forty acres and a mule" promise. President Lincoln endorsed the order. By June 1865, approximately 40,000 Black families had been settled on this land [cite — confirm]. Within months of Lincoln's assassination, President Andrew Johnson reversed the order, returned the land to the former Confederate owners, and displaced the Black families who had begun to farm it.

This is not ancient history approached through a haze of moral uncertainty. This is a documented federal promise made to people who had been subjected to 250 years of chattel slavery — forced, unpaid, brutal labor that produced enormous wealth for white landowners and for the American economy more broadly — followed by a documented federal revocation of that promise within the year. The theological question of restitution does not require speculative reconstruction. It requires reading the historical record.

The economist Thomas Piketty [cite — confirm] and others have documented how intergenerational wealth transfer functions — how the family that had land in 1865 used it to build equity, which funded education, which opened economic opportunity, which became assets transferred to the next generation. The family from whom the forty acres were rescinded entered the period of Reconstruction without land, without legal protection adequate to their situation, into a sharecropping system that was in many cases designed to recreate the economic conditions of slavery without its formal legal status. The compounding effect of that dispossession across generations is measurable and has been measured [cite — confirm].

---

The counter-position is important to take seriously, because it is held by thoughtful people who are not motivated by racial hostility.

The argument runs something like this: collective guilt is theologically incoherent. The biblical tradition holds individuals accountable for their own sins, not for the sins of their ancestors. The person alive today did not own slaves and should not be held financially liable for what others did. The practical mechanics of reparations — who qualifies, how much, administered by whom — are unworkable. And the deeper problem is that reparations treats race as the primary category of human identity, which is itself a form of racial essentialism that the church should resist.

These are real concerns. The theological point about collective guilt deserves careful engagement, because it is not nothing.

But notice what the argument quietly assumes: it frames reparations as punishment of the innocent for sins they did not commit. This is the wrong frame. The biblical model of restitution is not about punishment. It is about the correction of an ongoing condition. If I steal your land and then die, my children who inherited the land have not committed the original theft. But they are in possession of something that does not belong to them. The question of what they owe is not a question about their guilt for their father's crime. It is a question about their possession of property that was wrongly acquired.

The land in coastal Georgia that was redistributed to Confederate landowners after 1865 was, and remained, property that had a prior claim on it. The families who cleared it and would have farmed it had been promised it by the federal government as partial compensation for 250 years of uncompensated labor. That promise was broken. The breaking of it produced conditions that compounded economically across generations. The theological question is not whether descendants should be punished for their ancestors. The theological question is whether the ongoing possession and benefit of wrongly distributed wealth creates an obligation of restitution — and the biblical record, from Leviticus to Zacchaeus, says yes.

---

The Hebrew concept of *teshuvah* — turning, return — describes the full movement of genuine repentance: not only interior contrition but exterior action, not only acknowledgment but the material correction of what was wrong. The rabbis taught that teshuvah was incomplete without repair — without actually fixing, to the extent possible, the damage done. This is not a fringe position in the tradition. It is the tradition. Repentance that remains only interior is, in the rabbinic understanding, not yet complete repentance.

The American church's engagement with its racial history has been heavy on acknowledgment and light on teshuvah in the full sense. Denominational resolutions. Formal apologies. Lament services. Statements. These are not nothing. But they do not, by themselves, constitute the turning that includes material repair. And the communities most affected know the difference between being lamented and being compensated. They have been lamented before.

Desmond Tutu [cite — confirm] observed that when a man has stolen your land and built his house on it, his offer to pray with you about the situation does not constitute justice. Justice requires addressing the land. The prayer, disconnected from the land, is not a spiritual supplement to justice. It is a substitute for it.

---

What would a theology of reparations actually look like in practice for the church, rather than for the federal government?

It begins with honest historical reckoning. The Southern Baptist Convention was founded in 1845 in defense of slaveholding [cite — confirm]. The Presbyterian Church's southern branches were explicit defenders of slavery as a divinely sanctioned institution. Many of the most prominent seminaries of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries were built on land worked by enslaved people and funded by slaveowners' donations. These are facts, not interpretations. A church that does not know its own history cannot repent of it.

It continues with material commitment — not as a symbolic gesture, but as a structural redirection. What percentage of the church's endowment and annual giving is directed toward historically Black institutions, toward communities that bore the weight of the economic extraction that built the church's own wealth? Not as charity. As restitution. The category matters. Charity is voluntary, surplus-dispensing, and morally praiseworthy. Restitution is obligatory, debt-paying, and morally required.

It includes advocacy for public policy that addresses the material conditions created by centuries of documented theft. The pastor who preaches Zacchaeus without asking what federal policy today creates the conditions for restitution or prevents it has stopped the sermon a verse too soon.

---

I am inside this indictment. My tradition benefited from the same historical arrangements I am describing. I have preached repentance and been slower to ask about repair. I have been moved by lament services and returned to the same structures unchanged.

The theological case for reparations is not a political position borrowed from the left. It is an extension of what the Bible actually says about what justice requires when something has been taken. Leviticus 6 knew it. Zacchaeus knew it. The prophets knew it. The question is whether the twenty-first-century American church, which inherited institutions built on the labor of enslaved people, will finally know it too.

The debt is real. The history is documented. The theological framework is available.

What is missing is not the argument. It is the will.

That is a pastoral problem. Which means it is also a preaching problem. Which means it lands, finally, here — at the pulpit, where the text says what it says, and the preacher decides whether to let it land.
