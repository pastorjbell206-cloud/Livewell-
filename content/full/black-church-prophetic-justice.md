When white evangelicalism talks about rediscovering the prophetic tradition, about learning to connect justice and the gospel, about the church's responsibility to the poor and the marginalized — when it talks about these things as if they are new discoveries — the Black church in America has been practicing them for four hundred years.

This is not a minor omission. It is a form of historical amnesia with theological consequences.

The Black church emerged under slavery. Its first theologians were people who could not legally own a Bible, who worshiped in secret under penalty of violence, who developed a theology of liberation not as an academic exercise but as a survival technology. The spirituals were not simply religious music. They were a theology of protest and hope — a refusal to accept the slaveholder's gospel, which taught that God had ordained the hierarchy of race and that submission to the master was submission to God. The enslaved communities knew better. They sang about a God who hears the cry of the oppressed, who parts the waters for those who have no other escape, who brings down the powerful and exalts the humble.

They were reading the same Bible the slaveholders were reading. They were drawing very different conclusions. And their conclusions were correct.

## Two Christianities

Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery in Maryland in 1838 [cite — confirm], was one of the most penetrating theological minds of the nineteenth century, though he is not usually described that way. In his 1845 *Narrative* and more extensively in his 1855 *My Bondage and My Freedom* [cite — confirm], he wrote with surgical precision about the distinction between what he called the Christianity of Christ and the slaveholding religion of the South. He had experienced both: the Christianity that baptized the whip and invoked the authority of Paul's instructions to slaves to justify the cruelest violence, and the Christianity that sustained the faith of those being whipped — the Christianity of the hush arbors, the invisible church, the community that prayed in secret because the prayers that kept them alive were prayers the master could not be permitted to hear.

Douglass was not making a sociological observation. He was making a theological one. Two communities were reading the same Scripture and arriving at opposing conclusions about what God required, and one of those communities was reading in conditions of terror that clarified certain things the other community could afford to leave ambiguous. The enslaved communities had no patience for an abstract God. They needed a God who acts. And the God they found in the Exodus, in the Psalms of lament, in Isaiah's servant who was oppressed and afflicted, in the Jesus who was himself executed by an occupying imperial power — that God was available to them in ways that the comfortable church's God was not.

## The Institution of Liberation

By the time of Reconstruction, the Black church had become one of the most important institutions in American life, and its importance was inseparable from its prophetic character. The church was the school, the political assembly, the community bank, the social welfare system, and the center of cultural life for Black communities that had been systematically excluded from every other institution [cite — confirm]. The pastor was simultaneously a preacher, an educator, a civic leader, and a political strategist. The relationship between the sacred and the civic was not a confusion of categories. It was a theological necessity in a context where the categories were not permitted to stay separate.

This is the tradition from which the civil rights movement of the twentieth century emerged, and the white church's tendency to treat that movement as primarily a political phenomenon rather than a theological one reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of what was happening. Martin Luther King Jr.'s prophetic ministry was not political speeches occasionally decorated with biblical language. It was theology practiced in public — theology that drew on the full weight of the prophetic tradition that the Black church had preserved and practiced since before the republic existed. His sermons at Dexter Avenue Baptist Church in Montgomery and later at Ebenezer Baptist Church in Atlanta [cite — confirm] were structured as sermons, argued as sermons, rooted in the same liturgical tradition that had sustained Black faith through slavery and Reconstruction and Jim Crow.

The Birmingham campaign of 1963 [cite — confirm], the Selma to Montgomery marches of 1965 [cite — confirm] — these were not political events that borrowed religious imagery. They were acts of prophetic witness in the fullest sense of the tradition: the willingness to put your body in the gap between the world as it is and the world as God requires it to be. The people who marched across the Edmund Pettus Bridge and were beaten by state troopers were doing what Amos and Isaiah and Jeremiah had done: speaking the word of the Lord against the arrangements of power that crushed the vulnerable, and paying the cost.

## Howard Thurman and the Disinherited

Howard Thurman's *Jesus and the Disinherited*, published in 1949 [cite — confirm], articulated the connection between the gospel and liberation with a precision that most white evangelical theology had not approached and has not yet matched. His central argument was simple and devastating: Jesus was a poor Jew under Roman occupation, and his message was addressed primarily to people in the same condition — "those who stand with their backs against the wall." The meaning of the gospel is not separable from that condition of address. A Christianity that has been domesticated by power, that has become the religion of the comfortable, has cut itself off from the context in which the gospel made its original claim.

Thurman was writing for the grandmother he described in the opening of the book — the woman who had been enslaved, who was not permitted to learn to read, who had been read to from the Bible on special occasions and had always asked for the same texts: not Paul's instructions about slavery, but the passages that spoke about God's love and human dignity [cite — confirm]. She had arrived at her theology through the filter of suffering, and that filter had clarified things that no academic curriculum had produced in the white theology of his day.

I came to faith from atheism. My early formation was in predominantly white evangelical contexts that said very little about the Black church's theological contribution. When I encountered this tradition later — through reading, through relationships, through the corrective of people who had grown up in it — I recognized that I had been given a partial Christianity. Not false. Partial. A Christianity that had inherited the prophetic language while losing, somewhere in the centuries of comfort and cultural power, the prophetic practice.

## What the White Church Has Not Learned

The white evangelical church's engagement with the Black church's tradition has tended to follow a predictable pattern. In moments of social crisis, it discovers the prophetic tradition and describes it as newly relevant. It cites King. It references the spirituals. It gestures toward the freedom movement as evidence that the church can be prophetic when it needs to be. And then, when the crisis subsides, the prophetic tradition returns to its status as a special-case exception — interesting history, occasionally inspiring, not the center of the church's self-understanding.

This pattern is itself a theological problem. The Black church's prophetic witness was not a response to exceptional circumstances. It was the normal condition of faith practiced under duress, the baseline expression of a community that could not afford the luxury of separating the gospel from its social implications. To treat it as exceptional is to misunderstand what it is.

The credibility of prophetic witness is inseparable from the willingness to bear its cost. The Black church's voice on justice has weight because it was forged under pressure — not in academic conferences, not in safe ecclesiastical settings, but in communities that bore the consequences of speaking and the consequences of silence. The witness of the enslaved, the witness of those who faced the clubs and the fire hoses in Birmingham, the witness of communities that preached resurrection hope while burying their sons — that witness is not a program to be adopted. It is the product of centuries of practiced faith under conditions that the comfortable church cannot replicate.

What the white church can do is listen. It can stop describing the prophetic tradition as something it is recovering, as if the tradition disappeared between the writing of the prophets and the present moment. It survived. It was carried by communities the white church mostly ignored, occasionally actively suppressed, and is only now, in its own crisis of credibility, beginning to acknowledge.

## The Inheritance Available

There is something here that is available as inheritance, if the white church is willing to receive it on the terms it is offered — not as a resource to mine for the white church's self-renewal, but as a tradition in its own right, with its own teachers and texts and practices, that the white church has much to learn from and little to offer in exchange that has not already been learned.

The PCN pastors I have worked with who have the most durable, most effective prophetic ministries — who are most capable of connecting the gospel to the weight of what their communities are actually carrying — are, more often than not, pastors with direct roots in the Black church tradition or pastors who have sat long enough at those tables to have been genuinely changed by them. Not because race is a proxy for prophetic faithfulness, but because the tradition itself is richer where it has been practiced under pressure.

The prophetic tradition is not an innovation. It is a recovery. And the people who have been carrying it while others forgot deserve more than gratitude. They deserve to be heard, which is a different thing — harder, more disorienting, and ultimately more useful.

The Black church has been doing this work for centuries. The question is not whether the prophetic tradition is available. The question is whether we are willing to learn it from those who kept it alive.

That is not a rhetorical question. It is the one we will be judged on.
