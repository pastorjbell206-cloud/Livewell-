The color-blindness argument has a sound theological instinct at its center: that every human being bears the image of God and that racial categories are not ultimate. In the Kingdom of God, Paul announces, there is neither Jew nor Greek (Galatians 3:28). The eschatological vision of Revelation 7 shows the redeemed gathered from every tribe and language and people and nation. The church that knows this theology should not treat people differently on the basis of race.

This is true. It does not mean color-blindness is a virtue.

The error is confusing the eschatological reality with the present one. Galatians 3:28 is a declaration about what is true in Christ — about the identity that baptism confers and the standing that grace creates. It is not a sociological description of the present world. In the present world, racial categories are real because their effects are real. A theology that claims not to see race does not thereby undo the effects of seeing it for four centuries.

Consider what color-blindness actually accomplishes in practice. It enables the person who claims it to skip the question of what historical racial harm has produced in the present. If I do not see race, I do not see the gap in wealth, the gap in health outcomes, the gap in educational opportunity, the gap in incarceration rates that runs along racial lines in every major American city [cite — confirm]. I do not see these things not because they are invisible but because I have adopted an ideology that tells me not to look.

To understand what this costs, you need the history. The legal apparatus of American racial hierarchy — chattel slavery, the Dred Scott decision, the Black Codes, Jim Crow, redlining — did not end in a moment. Redlining, the federal practice of denying mortgage loans and insurance to residents of predominantly Black neighborhoods, was codified by the Home Owners' Loan Corporation beginning in the 1930s and enforced by policy and practice for decades [cite — confirm]. The effect was to systematically exclude Black families from the primary wealth-building mechanism available to postwar American households: homeownership. That wealth gap has compounded across generations. The color-blind ideology that says "I don't see race" does not undo a single generation of compounded exclusion. It merely refuses to see the evidence.

The prophets did not avert their eyes from the specific. Amos named the specific transaction in which the poor were sold: a pair of sandals (Amos 2:6). Isaiah named the specific people being crushed: the widows, the orphans, the ones without advocates in the gate (Isaiah 1:17). Jeremiah named the specific institution that was failing: the temple establishment that cried "peace, peace, when there is no peace" (Jeremiah 6:14). Prophetic vision is always particular. It sees the thing that is happening, names it, and refuses the comfortable abstraction that makes it invisible.

Color-blindness is a comfortable abstraction. It offers the emotional benefit of being a person who transcends racial division without requiring any engagement with the actual conditions that racial division has created. It is the theological equivalent of saying "I don't see poverty" as a way of not seeing the poor.

I grew up in contexts where color-blindness was considered the Christian position — where saying "I judge people by the content of their character, not the color of their skin" (invoking King's phrase, always out of context) was treated as the fullness of racial ethics. Martin Luther King Jr. was speaking in that sentence about a future hope, not a present description. He was naming a destination, not claiming to have arrived. To invoke that phrase as a reason not to examine the present is to conscript King into the defense of an arrangement he spent his life opposing. I held that misreading long past the point where I should have known better. It was easier. It did not require me to look at specific things. It gave me a clear conscience without giving me an examined one.

The counter-position deserves to be stated honestly: those who embrace color-blindness often argue that *naming* race perpetuates racial thinking — that the solution to a history of racial categorization is to stop categorizing racially, and that those who insist on naming race are themselves prolonging the problem. There is something in this. A politics of identity can, in certain forms, deepen division by making every interaction a performance of group allegiance. The concern is not invented.

But the response is not to move from racial obsession to racial blindness. Both positions have in common the refusal to look clearly. Racial obsession sees only race; racial blindness sees nothing but pretends that is virtue. What the prophetic tradition asks for is something different: the willingness to see what is actually there, with precision, without the distortion of either extreme. To see that a person is a full human being made in the image of God — and also to see that this particular person has been marked by a specific history, has inherited specific conditions, has experienced specific treatment because of a characteristic that society has invested with enormous and often destructive power. Both truths at once. Neither canceling the other.

The Revelation 7 vision of every tribe and nation and tongue worshiping together is not a vision of a world where difference has been erased. It is a vision of a world where difference has been honored within unity — where the specific history and culture and language of each people is present and celebrated in the worship of a God who made all of them and redeemed all of them. The nations do not disappear. They are brought in, each bearing their distinctiveness into the presence of God. John Stott observed that the preserved diversity of the nations in Revelation is not an accident of the text but a theological statement: the redeemed community is not homogeneous [cite — confirm]. God does not eliminate what he has made. He redeems it.

Taking the Revelation vision seriously means caring about the specific history that has shaped each particular people — including the history of how some peoples have treated others, and the damage that history has left. You cannot celebrate the healing without naming the wound. You cannot celebrate the gathering of the nations without accounting for how some nations have been treated by others.

The practical consequence for the local church is this: a congregation that claims color-blindness but whose leadership, programming, liturgy, and institutional culture reflect only one racial heritage is not actually blind. It is ignoring what everyone can see. And the people whose heritage is not represented — whose hymnody is absent, whose theological tradition is not engaged, whose particular history of suffering is not acknowledged — are not deceived by the language. They know what they are not seeing. The color-blind declaration lands among them as a request to be invisible.

James K.A. Smith argues, following Augustine, that the practices of a community shape what its members love before they know they love it — that liturgy and habit form the affections long before the intellect catches up [cite — confirm]. The implication for racial formation in the church is uncomfortable: a congregation that has never sung a song from the Black church tradition, that has never heard the theological account that emerged from under the weight of the Middle Passage, that has never sat under the pastoral witness of someone who carries that inheritance — that congregation has been formed in a particular direction whether it acknowledges it or not. Its color-blindness is not neutral. It has a shape.

The church that claims to see no color is not practicing the eschatological vision. It is practicing avoidance. The eschatological vision requires seeing clearly enough to name the gap between where we are and where God is taking us — and then doing the actual work of closing that gap, which requires seeing exactly what color-blindness refuses to see.

Here is what seeing requires in practical terms. It requires learning the history you were not taught — not as guilt-acquisition but as the same kind of intellectual honesty you would bring to any other subject where your initial formation was incomplete. The history of the Black church in America, for instance, is one of the most theologically rich traditions in the Christian story: a community that read the Exodus with their own bodies, that sang of freedom in coded language under a system that forbade literacy, that produced theologians and preachers of extraordinary depth precisely because their faith had to bear weight that the comfortable church never encountered. Howard Thurman's *Jesus and the Disinherited* (1949) was written for people who lived "with their backs against the wall" — people for whom the gospel had to mean something practical or it meant nothing [cite — confirm]. The white church that has never engaged that tradition has a gap in its theological education, not just its social awareness.

It also requires a different kind of listening than most predominantly white congregations have practiced. Not the listening that receives another community's experience as evidence to be weighed before reaching your own conclusions, but the listening that holds the speaker's testimony as primary data — that says: you have access to your experience in a way that I do not, and I need to hear it before I can respond faithfully. Miroslav Volf's work on embrace and exclusion argues that genuine reconciliation requires "the will to give space in oneself to the other" — to allow the other's reality to enter and reshape your perception before moving to response [cite — confirm]. This is not comfortable. It was not designed to be comfortable.

To see clearly is not the same as to treat people unjustly on the basis of race. Seeing clearly is the precondition of justice, not its enemy.

The first act of repair is always the same. You stop pretending you cannot see. You look at what is actually there. You let what you see make its demands on you.

That sequence is not the full work. But nothing else begins without it.
