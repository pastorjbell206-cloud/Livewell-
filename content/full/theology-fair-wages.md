There is a command in Deuteronomy that the American church has treated as an artifact of ancient Near Eastern labor regulations — an interesting historical detail with no particular claim on contemporary economic practice. It reads: "You shall not oppress a hired worker who is poor and needy, whether he is one of your brothers or one of the sojourners who are in your land within your towns. You shall give him his wages on the same day, before the sun sets (for he is poor and counts on it), lest he cry out against you to the Lord, and you be guilty of sin" (Deuteronomy 24:14-15).

The specificity of this text is worth dwelling on. Not a general principle about being fair to workers. A specific command about daily wage payment, grounded in a specific explanation — "he is poor and counts on it" — and attached to a specific consequence: his cry reaches God, and you bear guilt for it. The legislator of Deuteronomy is describing an economy in which day laborers had no cushion, no savings, no capacity to wait until Friday for money they needed to feed their households on Tuesday. The wage on the same day, before the sun sets, was not a courtesy. It was the margin between eating and not eating.

That economy is not as distant from our own as we have preferred to believe.

The theology of fair wages is not a minor theme in Scripture. It runs through the law, the prophets, the wisdom literature, the epistles, and the recorded words of Jesus with a consistency that should be embarrassing to any tradition that claims Scripture as its final authority and has nonetheless found a way to treat wage justice as a political topic rather than a theological one.

Start in the law. Leviticus 19:13 prohibits both robbery and the withholding of wages — placing them in the same sentence, as equivalent violations. The connection is not rhetorical. The law understands that withholding earned wages is a form of taking what belongs to someone else. It differs from theft only in its legal form, not in its moral substance. The worker who has performed the labor and not received the agreed payment has been robbed — the verb in the Hebrew, `ashaq`, carries the sense of extortion, of taking by power and position what belongs to another. This is not a matter of business negotiation. It is a matter of theft with a different name.

The prophets intensified this. Malachi, in a passage the church reads primarily as a promise about tithing, includes among those who will face divine judgment "those who oppress the hired worker in his wages" (Malachi 3:5). Malachi places wage theft in the same list as sorcerers, adulterers, and those who swear falsely. The company is instructive. These are not the gray areas of ancient ethics. These are the violations that drew the sharp edge of prophetic condemnation. And wage theft — the oppression of the hired worker in his wages — is among them without qualification.

Jeremiah, in one of the most pointed indictments in the prophetic corpus, turns his attention to Jehoiakim, king of Judah, who was building himself a magnificent palace: "Woe to him who builds his house by unrighteousness, and his upper rooms by injustice, who makes his neighbor serve him for nothing and does not give him his wages" (Jeremiah 22:13). The word translated "for nothing" is `hinnam` — gratuitously, without payment, without cause. Jeremiah is naming something specific: the king who extracts labor without compensation to fund his own grandeur. The grandeur does not impress Jeremiah. The extraction damns him.

James 5 carries this into the apostolic tradition with a force that should have been difficult to misread: "Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts" (James 5:4). The language reaches back to Deuteronomy 24 — the cry that reaches God. The wages kept by fraud cry out. The God who hears is named with a specific title: "Lord of hosts," the God of armies, the God of power. James is not making a pious wish that things were different. He is announcing that the God of the universe has specific interest in whether people receive what they have earned.

The church has read these texts for centuries. It has not, in the main, understood them as applying to the structure of wages in a modern economy.

The reasons for this are complex, but one of the most important is a theological partition that the Reformation helped produce and that American Christianity has deepened: the partition between the spiritual and the material, between "religious" questions (salvation, prayer, worship, personal ethics) and "secular" questions (economics, wages, the organization of labor). On this partition, the Deuteronomy texts about wages become ancient Near Eastern labor codes that have been superseded; the James 5 text becomes a warning about individual greed rather than a word about systemic wage structures; and the prophets become spiritual advisors whose economic critiques were addressed to specific historical situations with no contemporary applicability.

This partition is theologically incoherent, but it has been remarkably durable. It has allowed the American church to develop a robust theology of individual sin (the man who cheats his neighbor) while maintaining a nearly complete silence on the structural arrangements that determine how millions of workers are compensated for their labor.

Those arrangements deserve naming. The federal minimum wage in the United States has not been raised since 2009 [cite — confirm]. In many states and industries, workers earn wages that make it functionally impossible to afford housing, food, and healthcare simultaneously, regardless of how many hours they work. Wage theft — defined as the failure of employers to pay legally required wages, overtime, or benefits — costs American workers tens of billions of dollars annually [cite — confirm]. These are not abstract economic statistics. They are the specific contemporary form of what Deuteronomy 24 addresses: workers who are poor and count on their wages, and do not receive them.

The counter-argument from the church's comfortable wing runs predictably: "The Bible is not an economics textbook. It doesn't specify a minimum wage. Free markets determine wages more efficiently than biblical commands can. We should address the spiritual roots of greed rather than legislate outcomes." This argument has the form of theological seriousness. It does not have the substance.

It does not have the substance because it treats the scriptural witness as a source of spiritual principles rather than specific demands. Deuteronomy 24 is specific. It names day laborers. It names the sun going down. It names the cry that reaches God. That specificity was not accidental — the law was addressing specific people in specific situations of vulnerability, because law that addresses only spiritual principles and never specific situations protects no one.

And it does not have the substance because the free-market account of wages depends on assumptions — perfect information, equal bargaining power, genuine mobility of labor — that the people who mow the fields and work the warehouses and staff the call centers simply do not possess. The bargaining power between an employer who can afford to wait and a worker who cannot afford not to work is not a negotiation between equals. This is not a radical economic claim. It is an observation about power that Deuteronomy and Jeremiah and James made before any formal economics existed.

The church that is serious about the theology of fair wages will need to think concretely. That thinking might include paying everyone employed by the church at a wage that allows dignified living in the local economy — which more churches do not do than do [cite — confirm]. It might include speaking from the pulpit about the specific conditions of wage-earning in the community the church serves — acknowledging that some families in the congregation work full-time and cannot make rent, and that this is a theological problem, not a personal failure. It might include joining coalitions of workers and advocates working for wage standards that would bring local employment practices into alignment with what Deuteronomy 24 requires.

None of this is comfortable. The theology of fair wages will create friction in congregations that include business owners and employers — people who are not necessarily acting in bad faith but who have absorbed, along with their business education, a set of assumptions about wage determination that has very little overlap with what James 5 describes. The pastor who addresses this directly will receive pushback. Some of it will be sophisticated pushback. It will deserve honest engagement.

But the pastor who avoids the topic because the topic creates friction has made a choice: the comfort of the employed is worth more than the cry of the laborer that reaches the ears of the Lord of hosts. That is not a neutral pastoral decision. That is a theological position, whether or not it is acknowledged as one.

The worker counts on his wages. Before the sun sets. The specificity is not incidental. It is the whole point.

The God who wrote the specificity into the law of Israel has not since revised his interest in what happens to the person who cannot afford to wait.
