You cannot make a compelling case for the value of a human life and then decline to fund what that life requires to survive.

This is not a rhetorical point. It is a logical one. If the argument for protecting the unborn is grounded in the Imago Dei — in the conviction that every human being bears the image of God and therefore possesses intrinsic worth — then that argument does not expire at delivery. The image of God is not a prenatal condition. It is a permanent one.

The church has sometimes spoken as though the protection of life before birth and the support of life after birth are separate concerns, pursued by different kinds of people with different political orientations. That separation is not biblical. It is a product of American political sorting, and the church has been far too willing to accept it.

## What the Scriptures Say About Life After Birth

Deuteronomy 15:11 is not a suggestion: "There will always be poor people in the land. Therefore I command you to be openhanded toward your fellow Israelites who are poor and needy." The openhanded command is not qualified by whether the poor person made good choices or by whether their poverty was deserved. It is absolute, and it is addressed to the whole community as a community obligation, not only as an individual aspiration.

Proverbs 19:17 makes the theological stakes explicit: "Whoever is kind to the poor lends to the Lord." The language is astonishing. To care for the poor person in front of you is to be in a transaction with God — not a metaphorical one, but a real one in which God is the debtor. The Proverbs do not offer this as an exaggeration. It is a claim about the identification between God and the person in need.

Matthew 25:40 extends this identification into the New Testament: "Whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me." The "least of these" in Matthew 25 are not abstract. They are hungry, thirsty, naked, sick, imprisoned. They are specific human beings in specific circumstances, and the test Jesus describes is not ideological affiliation or theological correctness. It is material response to material need.

The child who is born into poverty is hungry, thirsty, and in need of the community's material response. The infant whose mother cannot afford prenatal care, whose family has no stable housing, whose neighborhood has no adequate healthcare — that child is in exactly the category Matthew 25 is describing. The church that was passionate about that child's right to be born and then withdraws from the community's responsibility to support the child's life has not been pro-life. It has been pre-natal.

## The Imago Dei Does Not Expire

The theological argument for the sanctity of unborn life is the same argument that demands material support for the lives already born. If the ground of the argument is that every human person, regardless of their size, their developmental stage, their economic contribution, their cognitive capacity, bears the image of God — then the argument that covers the embryo also covers the infant, the toddler, the child in the underfunded school, the teenager in the juvenile justice system, the adult in prison.

The image of God does not come with conditions. It is not contingent on productivity or viability or social utility. It is a permanent feature of what it means to be human. And the church that uses the Imago Dei to defend the unborn but then treats the born poor as a separate category — as a political issue rather than a theological one — has not actually applied the argument it claims. It has applied it selectively, which is to say it has not applied it at all.

This is an indictment that lands on me before it lands anywhere else. I have been quicker to preach the sanctity of the unborn than to examine what my congregation's budget says about its actual commitment to the lives of those who have been born into hardship in our community. The gap between the conviction I articulate and the resources I deploy is a gap that requires honest examination.

## What the Credibility Gap Costs

The credibility of the pro-life argument depends, in no small part, on whether the people who make it are willing to pay for it. This is not cynicism. It is the ordinary epistemology of moral argument: the person who calls you to a sacrifice they are not willing to make themselves has a credibility problem that is not rhetorical. It is substantive.

The watching world — including the people most likely to be persuaded by a pro-life argument — has noticed the gap. The political scientist who observes that the states with the strongest abortion restrictions also have the weakest social safety nets is making a sociological observation [cite — confirm]. But it lands as a moral one. The church that is loudest about protecting the unborn in the womb and quietest about funding what those children need after birth has demonstrated something about what it actually believes. Not what it says it believes. What it believes.

The pro-choice argument gains its most powerful traction from exactly this gap. When the argument is made that pro-life legislation forces women to carry pregnancies to term without adequate support for what comes after — and when the churches that advocate for that legislation have not demonstrated a credible commitment to filling that support gap — the argument is not easily answered. The church's response cannot be purely theological. It must be material.

## What the Counter-Argument Gets Right

There is an honest counter-position: the church is not the government, and the demand that congregations solve systemic poverty before they earn the right to advocate against abortion is a form of rhetorical hostage-taking designed to produce paralysis rather than action. No other advocacy position is held to this standard. Anti-trafficking organizations are not required to solve poverty before they can oppose trafficking. Civil rights organizations were not required to solve structural inequality before they could oppose segregation.

This argument is partially correct. The demand for perfect consistency before any advocacy is permitted is unreasonable and is often deployed strategically to silence positions that the person making the demand opposes. The church should not accept the premise that its pro-life advocacy is invalid until it has solved poverty.

But the counter-argument does not discharge the obligation. It establishes that the obligation does not need to be fully met before advocacy is permitted. It does not establish that the obligation does not exist. The church can advocate for the unborn and simultaneously — not sequentially, but simultaneously — advocate for and fund the material support that makes life after birth possible. The failure to do the second does not invalidate the first. But it does narrow the argument's moral reach in ways the church should care about.

## What Material Support for Life After Birth Actually Looks Like

The obligation is specific. It looks like adoption and foster care ministries that are as organized, funded, and publicly celebrated as the political advocacy for the unborn. It looks like material support — diapers, food, rent assistance, childcare — for the women in the congregation who chose life without a support system. It looks like advocacy for the healthcare and childcare policies that determine whether a single mother can survive with a newborn. It looks like congregations that track the outcomes for the children they helped bring into the world, and remain present for those outcomes.

It looks like a church willing to ask the uncomfortable question: of the women in our congregation who have given birth in the last five years, how many of them had genuine material support from this community beyond a baby shower? How many of them know they can call someone here at midnight when the situation is impossible? How many of them are still connected to this congregation, or did the community's attention evaporate when the urgency did?

Those questions are harder than a political position. They require more than a vote. They require the kind of long, slow, material investment that does not generate a testimony at the denominational gathering.

They are also the questions that determine whether the pro-life conviction is real or performed. And the answer to those questions in a given congregation is not found in its doctrinal statement or its political affiliations. It is found in the budget, in the volunteer hours, in the phone contact list of the woman who is now alone with a one-year-old and needs someone who will pick up when she calls.

The image of God does not expire at birth. The church's obligation does not expire there either. Abstract conviction is cheap. The version that costs something — the version that shows up after the delivery, after the cameras are gone, after the celebration has moved on, for as long as the need persists — is the version that means anything.

That version requires something of the church that no political position can substitute for. It requires presence. And the absence of that presence is itself a theological statement.
