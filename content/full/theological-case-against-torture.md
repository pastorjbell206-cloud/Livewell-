The question should not require a theological case. That it does — that, in 2026, the church finds itself in a position where making a theological argument against the deliberate infliction of pain on human beings to extract information is necessary — is itself a symptom worth diagnosing. The symptom is not primarily political. It is a failure of theological formation so thoroughgoing that the most basic implications of Christian doctrine have become contested terrain.

Let me begin where Christian theology must always begin on questions of human treatment: imago Dei.

Genesis 1:26-27 establishes something that the rest of Scripture never revises: human beings, every human being, bear the image of God. This is not a merit-based designation. It is not restricted to the innocent, the cooperative, the friendly. It is a statement about the ontological status of human beings as such — not what they have done or failed to do, not what they know or refuse to reveal, not what nation they belong to or what cause they serve. Every person who has ever been strapped to a chair or submerged in water or subjected to sleep deprivation or exposed to extremes of temperature or had their body broken by another person — every one of them bore the image of God when it happened.

Torture, defined precisely, is the deliberate infliction of severe suffering — physical, psychological, or both — upon a person in order to coerce a response. The coercion may target information, a confession, compliance, or simply the capitulation of the person's will. In every case, the torturer is using the image of God as an instrument. He is taking the God-bearing creature and treating her as a thing — a system to be broken, a source to be mined, a problem to be solved by the application of sufficient pain. The imago Dei is not destroyed by this; the theologians are right that it is indelible. But it is treated as if it does not exist. And the theological verdict on that treatment is not difficult to locate.

The second pillar of the theological case is the Incarnation. In becoming human, God entered the condition that makes torture possible. Jesus of Nazareth had a body that could be struck, pierced, exhausted, broken. The accounts of his crucifixion in the Gospels describe not a dignified execution but a process of systematic state torture — the flogging before crucifixion, the crown of thorns, the cross itself as an instrument designed to maximize suffering and extend dying. When the church says "we believe in the crucifixion," it is confessing, among other things, that God has occupied the position of the torture victim. He has been there. He is there still — Matthew 25:31-46 makes explicit the logic: what is done to the least of these is done to him. The person undergoing torture in a black site or a detention facility — whatever she has done, whatever she knows, whatever threat she represents — she is, in the calculus of Matthew 25, the one to whom the crucified God is present.

The theological implications of this for the church's stance on torture are not ambiguous. The church that is silent about torture, or that hedges its position with calculations about national security, has not merely failed a political test. It has failed to acknowledge the location of its Lord.

The counter-argument that any honest treatment of this subject must engage is the argument from extreme necessity. It runs as follows: "Suppose you have captured a person who has information about an imminent attack that will kill thousands of innocent people. Are you really arguing that no amount of suffering inflicted on this one person is permissible to prevent that mass killing?" This is the "ticking bomb" scenario, and it has been deployed with considerable effectiveness in post-September 11 debates about enhanced interrogation [cite — confirm]. It deserves a direct answer, not dismissal.

The answer has two parts. The first part is empirical: the ticking bomb scenario has almost never accurately described real interrogation situations [cite — confirm]. Professional interrogators with decades of experience in intelligence gathering have consistently reported that coercive interrogation produces unreliable information — that the person being tortured will say whatever he calculates will stop the pain, which is not reliably the truth, and that rapport-based interrogation techniques produce better information in real situations [cite — confirm]. The scenario that makes torture seem obvious is a fiction of television dramas, not an accurate description of the actual conditions under which interrogation occurs. Real intelligence work is slow, uncertain, and fundamentally incompatible with the logic of emergency that the ticking bomb scenario depends on.

The second part is theological: moral principles that apply "except in extreme circumstances" are not principles. They are preferences. The absolute prohibition on torture in the Christian tradition — articulated in the Geneva Conventions, in Catholic social teaching, in the statements of mainstream Protestant denominations — is not an absolute because the tradition failed to think about hard cases. It is an absolute because the tradition understood that the moment you build in the exception, the exception becomes the rule. Every torturing regime in modern history has operated under the conviction that its particular circumstances were exceptional enough to justify what it was doing. The Nazis had their exceptional circumstances. The Khmer Rouge had theirs. The logic of emergency exceptionalism, once admitted, has no natural stopping point.

Augustine, in the early fifth century, wrote about the judge who tortures the accused to discover whether he is guilty — and noted the bitter irony: the judge inflicts certain suffering on the accused in order to prevent uncertain suffering, and if the man is innocent, the judge cannot give him back what the torture took [cite — confirm]. Augustine was not opposed to capital punishment. He was not what we would now call a pacifist. But he saw clearly that torture corrupts the administration of justice at its foundations, because it treats the presumption of guilt as a license for what amounts to punishment before judgment.

That corruption is not merely institutional. It is personal and pastoral.

The person who tortures — the one whose hands do it, who gives the order, who signs off on the policy — does something to themselves in the doing. The church has always understood that sin distorts the sinner, not merely the sinned-against. The soldier who learns to inflict systematic suffering on another human being, who trains himself to be unmoved by screaming and weeping and the breaking of a body, has not merely performed a function. He has formed himself. He has become something. The pastoral literature emerging from those who have worked with veterans of enhanced interrogation describes the moral injury — a concept the field of trauma psychology has developed to describe the damage done to the self by participation in acts that violate one's own moral understanding — as serious and lasting [cite — confirm]. These are not people whose consciences were unaffected by what they did. They are people who discovered, too late, that the act requires the actor to cross a threshold that cannot be uncrossed.

The church's silence on this is not political caution. It is pastoral negligence. The same community that has people in it who have been asked to torture, and who carry the weight of having done so, needs to have a theological vocabulary that takes seriously both the gravity of what was done and the possibility of the forgiveness and healing that the gospel announces. That vocabulary requires being clear about whether torture is something the church believes God condemns. If the church cannot say that clearly, it has nothing to offer the man in the back pew who cannot sleep.

I came to faith from atheism, and one of the things that drew me toward Christian theology was its refusal to treat the question of what we do to bodies as merely political. The Christian tradition insists that bodies matter — that the resurrection of the body is not a metaphor, that what happens to a body in history has eschatological weight, that the God who made a body his own is not indifferent to what bodies undergo. A faith built on those convictions cannot be neutral about the deliberate destruction of a body's integrity in order to extract its secrets.

The silence of the American church on torture — a practice that was officially authorized and documented in the recent history of this country — is a theological failure of the first order. It is not a gray area. The image of God, the Incarnation, and the Matthew 25 logic together produce a case that does not require a close reading of policy documents. It requires the church to say, clearly and without qualification, that the deliberate infliction of severe suffering on a human being made in the image of God is something that the God of Scripture hates.

He hates it. The church should say so.

And then it should ask — as I ask of myself — where its silence has made it complicit in what it refused to name.
