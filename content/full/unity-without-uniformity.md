The night before he died, Jesus prayed for unity.

Not for agreement on every doctrinal question. Not for a single worship style or a unified political position or a cultural consensus that would make the church legible to the empire. He prayed that his followers would be one "as you, Father, are in me, and I in you" — and the purpose he named was not institutional efficiency but witness: "that the world may believe that you have sent me" (John 17:21). The unity is not an organizational achievement. It is an argument. It is the thing that makes the claim credible in a world that has every reason to be skeptical of grand theological assertions.

We have, on the whole, not produced that thing. We have produced a different thing — a tendency to mistake sameness for unity, to create communities held together by shared cultural markers, shared political assumptions, shared worship aesthetics, shared levels of education or income — and to call the result the body of Christ. What we have built is a club. Clubs are comfortable. They are not arguments. They confirm what their members already believe about the world, and the world watches them and learns nothing.

The New Testament church was not a club. Acts 2 describes a community that would have been sociologically implausible in first-century Jerusalem — Parthians and Medes and Elamites, residents of Mesopotamia, Judea and Cappadocia, Pontus and Asia, Phrygia and Pamphylia, Egypt and parts of Libya, visitors from Rome, Cretans and Arabs (Acts 2:9–11). This was not diversity as a program or as an institutional commitment. These groups did not naturally gather. They had histories of competition, mutual suspicion, and ethnic distance that went back generations. What gathered them was not shared background or shared social location. It was a shared encounter with a risen Lord, and the claim that encounter made on every other organizing principle of their lives.

Including the organizing principles they most valued.

Paul's letter to the Galatians was written to a community that was fracturing along exactly the lines that come most naturally to human beings — ethnic and religious identity. Some in the Galatian church were insisting that Gentile believers adopt the covenant markers of Jewish identity, particularly circumcision. This was not a fringe position; it came from people who believed, sincerely, that they were protecting the integrity of the gospel. And Paul said — with a force that shocks the reader who encounters it without warning — that they were not protecting the gospel. They were destroying it. "There is neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28). Not a sentimental aspiration. A description of what baptism does to the social order and to the identity categories the social order depends on.

The passage does not mean that the differences cease to exist. Paul knew perfectly well that Gentiles remained Gentiles and Jews remained Jews after baptism. The differences remained. What changed was their organizing power — their capacity to function as the primary categories by which people were included or excluded, valued or dismissed. In Christ, those categories are relativized. Not erased. Relativized — placed under something larger than themselves, something that determines their ultimate weight and reach.

This is a costly claim. It was costly in the first century, when it produced the scandal of Jew and Gentile eating at the same table and wealthy and poor being addressed without distinction. It is costly now, when it asks a congregation to remain in relationship with the member whose conclusions they find difficult — to share the table with someone whose politics or aesthetics or secondary theology produces friction, because the ground on which we stand together is more fundamental than the things we dispute.

I have attended churches that achieved unity by achieving uniformity. Everyone agreed on the contested questions, the worship had one sound, the congregation occupied a tight band of the cultural spectrum. And in every case — without exception — the uniformity had been purchased at the price of someone's absence. The people who complicated the picture had left, or had learned to keep their complications private. The community was unified because it was not actually diverse, and the appearance of unity was maintained by the quiet removal of everyone who would have made it harder.

This is not witness. This is performance. The world does not look at a room of people who all think the same things and feel the same things and come from the same places and conclude that the resurrection must be true. There is nothing there that cannot be explained by sociology. A community that holds together across genuine difference — that stays in the room with the person it disagrees with, that works out the tension rather than eliminating the one who produces it — that is harder to explain. That is the thing that requires an account.

The honest counter-argument holds that doctrinal clarity requires some exclusion, that a community with no real center cannot hold, that the language of unity-without-uniformity can become a way of refusing to name error. This is not wrong. The unity I am describing is not theological indifferentism. Not the claim that all positions are equivalent or that the center of the faith is too thin to generate any real conviction. The gospel is a specific thing: the death and resurrection of Jesus, the forgiveness of sins, the reconciliation of human beings to God, the coming of the Kingdom that is already and not yet. That center is not negotiable, and the church that trades its specificity for a vague inclusiveness has abandoned the thing that makes it capable of being any particular community at all.

The question is which things belong to the center and which things belong to the secondary ring where genuine Christians have disagreed across centuries without destroying each other. The church's history of fracture is in large part a history of treating the secondary ring as though it were the center — of splitting over baptismal mode and church governance and worship style and political alignment, as though the truth about these things were as critical as the truth about the resurrection. Every split has come with theological justification. Almost none of them have been worth what they cost.

James K.A. Smith argues that what forms communities is not primarily shared beliefs but shared practices — the liturgies of daily life that shape desire and identity before explicit theology gets to them. [cite — confirm Smith's argument in *Desiring the Kingdom*] If that is right, then the question of unity-without-uniformity is not primarily a question of how to manage doctrinal disagreement. It is a question of whether the community's shared practices — worship, communion, service, prayer — are thick enough to hold people across the differences they bring to those practices. The practice of sitting at the same table, over time, does things to the people sitting there that argument alone cannot do.

The Lord's Supper was, in the New Testament context, a shared meal that included people from every level of the social hierarchy — and Paul's fierce rebuke in 1 Corinthians 11 is precisely about the failure to eat together across those lines. The wealthy were eating their own food and getting drunk while the poor went hungry, and Paul said this practice made the Lord's Supper not the Lord's Supper at all. The practice was invalidated by the exclusion. The unity the practice was meant to enact required actual sharing across actual difference — bodies at the same table, eating from the same loaf.

Genuine unity costs more than uniformity. It requires staying in the room with the person you would prefer to have leave. It requires the theological confidence to say: the ground on which we stand is larger than the questions we disagree about, and I am not going anywhere. It requires the willingness to be changed by the encounter — to discover that the person you disagreed with has something that you need, that your picture of the faith is smaller than the faith itself, that the community you have built is impoverished by the absence of the people who would have complicated it.

The church that can produce that kind of unity is the church that has something to say to a fractured world. Not because it has solved the problem of difference — no institution has — but because it is visibly trying to hold together what everything else is tearing apart, and the effort itself is an argument about what is ultimately real.

The prayer of John 17 is not answered once and then preserved. It is answered continuously, by people who have decided that the prayer matters more than their comfort with the people who share it.

That work is the witness. The world is watching, and it has been watching for a long time, waiting to see whether the church means it.
