The homeless man sleeping under the interstate overpass three blocks from my church does not come to our Sunday services. This is not because he has not been invited. It is because the social distance between where he is and where we gather on Sunday morning is too great to be bridged by an invitation.

I have known this for years. I have mostly not said it from the pulpit.

The people Jesus spent the most time with — the tax collectors, the prostitutes, the lepers, the demoniacs, the Samaritan woman at the well, the man in the tombs among the Gerasenes who cut himself with stones — were not the kind of people who showed up at synagogue. They were excluded from the formal religious community. Some of them were legally impure and could not enter. Some of them were socially marked in ways that made their presence unwelcome. And Jesus went to them. He did not wait for them to figure out how to cross the threshold into the respectable gathering. He crossed the threshold into their world.

This is not a minor strategic difference. It is a theological one. The God who became flesh in a feeding trough in an occupied province, who was born to refugees who fled across a border into Egypt, who grew up in Galilee of the Gentiles — this God did not arrive through official channels. He arrived among the people the official channels had already abandoned.

The church has built many programs to reach vulnerable people. Food banks, clothes closets, holiday toy drives, addiction recovery groups — these are genuine goods, and I am not dismissing them. But they are structured around an assumption: the vulnerable need to come to where the church is. They need to make the journey to our building, to enter our space, to participate on our terms. The model is attractional, even when the programs are labeled outreach.

Jesus did not operate on an attractional model. The Sermon on the Mount was delivered to a crowd that had followed him into a field because there was something in him that drew people who had nothing else to try. His ministry was characterized by going — into the synagogues, yes, but also into the countryside, into the cemetery, into the home of a tax collector, into the social space of a Samaritan village where no self-respecting rabbi would have stopped. He went where the need was, and the need was not primarily inside the religious gathering.

## The Architecture of Absence

Why are the most vulnerable people in your city not in your pews? Several reasons, and most of them are design choices.

The building is inaccessible — architecturally, socially, culturally. The gathered congregation is, in most cases, economically and culturally homogeneous in ways that signal to the poor and marginalized that this is not a room built for them. The service is structured around the assumptions and capacities of middle-class participants. The social rituals of hospitality — the coffee hour, the greeting, the small talk — require a kind of social fluency that people in genuine crisis do not have at nine on a Sunday morning.

These are design choices. Every design choice is a theological statement about who the church is for.

When Nehemiah surveyed the broken walls of Jerusalem in the fifth century B.C., he began not with a capital campaign but with a census — a careful accounting of who was in the city and who was absent (Nehemiah 7). The gaps in the wall corresponded to the gaps in the community. You could read the social fractures of the city in the architecture of its neglected defenses. Every church building tells the same story. Where are the gaps? Who is missing, and why? That question is not administrative. It is prophetic.

The prison population in the United States exceeds two million people [cite — confirm]. The vast majority of those two million have families, most of them poor, living in the communities around your church. They are one of the largest unreached populations in the country, and they are geographically proximate to every congregation in America. Most churches have no sustained engagement with them — not because the need is invisible, but because meeting the need requires going somewhere that makes middle-class Christians uncomfortable. It requires walking through a metal detector, sitting in a room with people who did things we would rather not think about, and offering the gospel in a context where it cannot be packaged with comfortable programming.

The same is true of the shelter network, the addiction recovery houses, the transitional living facilities for people exiting incarceration, the apartment complexes where the working poor live three families to a unit. These are not distant mission fields. They are within walking distance of most American churches. The distance is not geographical. It is social. And social distance can be crossed.

## The Shape of Jesus's Ministry

Luke's Gospel is the one that preserves most clearly the pattern of Jesus's ministry in relation to the excluded. In Luke 4:18, quoting Isaiah 61, Jesus describes his mission: good news to the poor, release to the captives, sight to the blind, freedom for the oppressed. These are not metaphorical categories. They are social descriptions of people at the bottom of every hierarchy in the ancient world.

Luke 7:34 records that Jesus's enemies accused him of being "a friend of tax collectors and sinners." This was not a compliment. Tax collectors were collaborators with Roman occupation, people who had bought the right to extract money from their own neighbors and were despised accordingly. "Sinners" was a broad category that included people in occupations considered ritually polluting — tanners, who worked with dead animals; shepherds, whose work made Sabbath observance nearly impossible; women whose sexual history placed them outside the boundaries of respectable community. Jesus ate with these people. In the ancient world, table fellowship was an act of social identification. To eat with someone was to say: I am willing to be associated with you in public. Jesus made that statement repeatedly, and the religious establishment found it scandalous.

We should find our own version of it equally scandalous. Not the fact of association, but its absence.

Luke 14 describes a dinner where the host, after the invited guests make excuses, sends his servant to bring in "the poor, the crippled, the blind and the lame" from the streets and alleys (Luke 14:21). When there is still room, he sends the servant out again to the roads and country lanes to compel people to come in, "so that my house will be full." This is not a church growth strategy. It is a description of what the kingdom feast looks like — and it requires the host to go where the guests are, rather than waiting for the guests to arrive.

## The Counter-Argument

The honest objection deserves to be named. A congregation that sets out to serve the most vulnerable will face real challenges: safety concerns, resource constraints, the difficulty of building a sustainable community across vast social distances, the risk of creating a congregation that is a social services provider rather than a worshiping body. Some of these concerns are legitimate.

But they tend to be invoked selectively. The same congregations that cite safety concerns when asked about engaging the homeless are, in many cases, running elaborate and expensive ministries that serve people who are already comfortable. The safety concern is real; the question is whether it is being applied consistently or whether it functions as a socially acceptable way of protecting the congregation's demographic homogeneity.

The deeper concern — that engagement with the most vulnerable will distort the church's primary calling to worship and formation — is also worth addressing honestly. The church is not a social service agency. Its primary gathering is for worship, proclamation, and the formation of people in the gospel. But the gospel being proclaimed shapes who shows up to hear it. A gospel that announces good news to the poor will draw the poor. A gospel that doesn't mention them will draw people who are comfortable without it.

The New Testament church in Acts 2 is remarkable not primarily because of the spectacular events of Pentecost but because of what followed: a community that shared possessions, sold property, and distributed to anyone in need (Acts 2:44–45). The early church was multiclass in composition and radically redistributive in practice. Not because redistribution was the point. Because when the poor and the wealthy heard the same gospel and gathered at the same table, the practical logic of their belonging required it.

## What It Would Require

Going to the most vulnerable in your city — not merely offering programs for them to come to — requires sustained commitment of the kind that doesn't produce visible metrics on Sunday morning. It requires volunteers who will show up at the shelter on Tuesday. It requires pastoral staff whose job descriptions include the county jail. It requires a budget allocation that reflects the belief that the people outside the building matter as much as the people inside it.

It requires the courage to let the congregation's identity be shaped by its engagement with the excluded rather than by its reputation in the suburbs.

The early Methodists in eighteenth-century England went into the coal mines and the mills and the slums of industrializing Britain precisely because John Wesley understood that the people who most needed the gospel were not the people who had the leisure and the social confidence to enter a church building [cite — confirm]. He took the gospel to the spaces where the poor already were — and the result was a revival that transformed not just individual lives but the social fabric of a nation.

I am not arguing for a program. I am arguing for a posture. The posture that asks, on Sunday morning: who is not here? Why are they not here? What would it cost us to go to them? And then, having asked, to be willing to hear an answer that requires something.

The most vulnerable people in your city are on the streets and in the shelters and in the county jail and in the apartments where the heat has been cut off. The church that takes Luke 14 seriously knows where to find them.

The question is whether it will go.

That question does not resolve easily. It sits there every Sunday morning, in the gap between who is present and who is not, waiting for someone to take it seriously enough to act.
