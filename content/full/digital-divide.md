There is a pattern in how injustice works that the church should recognize by now, because the prophetic tradition has been naming it for three thousand years. The resources that enable human flourishing — land, water, shelter, education — are not distributed randomly. They concentrate. They follow the lines of existing wealth and power. And the communities with the least access to those resources are systematically the communities that already had the least.

The digital divide is that pattern applied to the infrastructure of the twenty-first century.

Access to reliable high-speed internet and the devices that use it is no longer a luxury for the technologically enthusiastic. It is the basic requirement for education, employment, healthcare access, civic participation, and increasingly the ability to manage the administrative demands of everyday life. The family without it is not disconnected from convenience. It is disconnected from the systems through which society now distributes its resources and opportunities.

---

The geography of internet access in the United States follows the geography of poverty and race with a fidelity that should stop us.

Rural communities — disproportionately poor, disproportionately communities of color — have the lowest rates of broadband access. Urban low-income neighborhoods have access to the infrastructure but often cannot afford the monthly cost, which for the family already under financial pressure is not a trivial calculation. The Federal Communications Commission has documented the access gap; independent researchers have consistently found the gap is larger than official data captures. [cite — confirm] During the period when schools moved to remote learning in 2020-2021, the disparity became unmistakable: the children who already had the least advantage were conducting school from parking lots trying to access a library's wireless signal, while children from more advantaged households attended class from dedicated home offices.

That is not an accident. That is a system producing exactly the outcome it was built to produce — not through malice in most cases, but through the accumulated effect of infrastructure investment decisions that have consistently favored markets with existing capacity to pay. Private broadband providers build where the return on investment is clearest. That means wealthy suburbs get fiber and exurban rural counties get nothing. The theological category for this is not a complicated one. It is the opposite of the gleaning laws. Instead of ensuring that the margin of the harvest reaches the poor, it ensures that the margin of the infrastructure reaches the wealthy.

---

The argument against treating this as a theological issue rather than a technical one goes like this: internet access is a product of private markets, not a fundamental human right or a matter of biblical concern. The prophets did not say anything about broadband, and importing policy preferences into the pulpit under prophetic cover is a category error.

There is something to this. The specific policy levers — municipal broadband, universal service requirements, subsidized device programs, public-private partnerships — are genuinely contested, and the church does not have technical expertise in the comparative effectiveness of these approaches. The church should be cautious about treating any particular policy solution as the biblical answer.

But the objection does not touch the underlying moral claim. The prophetic tradition has consistently named access to the basic resources required for human flourishing as a justice issue — not because these resources were mentioned by name in Leviticus, but because the principle is stated repeatedly in terms that translate across changing material conditions. "You shall not strip your vineyard bare, neither shall you gather the fallen grapes of your vineyard. You shall leave them for the poor and for the sojourner" (Leviticus 19:10). The gleaning laws did not catalog every possible form of resource; they established the principle that the productive capacity of the community carried an obligation to those who had been excluded from its full benefits.

The vineyard has changed form. The obligation has not.

---

The practical consequences of the divide land most heavily on children.

Education in America has been restructured around the assumption of digital access. Research projects, homework assignments, college application processes, standardized testing registration, financial aid applications — these are not optional components of the educational system. They are the system. The child who does not have reliable home internet access and a functioning device is not working at a disadvantage in some competitions; she is excluded from the basic infrastructure through which educational opportunity is currently distributed.

The long-term consequences compound. The young adult who entered the digital economy without adequate preparation — who did not have the access that made skill development possible, who could not participate in the learning and credential-building that now largely happens online — faces an economic environment that has pre-selected against her. The gap that began with a missed homework assignment becomes a gap in employability, a gap in earning potential, a gap in the ability to build the stability that makes every other dimension of flourishing more possible.

James 2:14-17 asks a question that applies directly: "What good is it, my brothers, if someone says he has faith but does not have works? Can that faith save him? If a brother or sister is poorly clothed and lacking in daily food, and one of you says to them, 'Go in peace, be warmed and filled,' without giving them the things needed for the body, what good is that?" James is not describing an exceptional case. He is describing the gap between what is spoken and what is done — the gap between acknowledging a need and addressing it.

The church that acknowledges the digital divide as a real problem and does nothing about it has produced exactly this: words without works. Faith without the things needed for the body.

---

The pastoral dimension is particular to this issue in a way that requires naming.

The digital divide is experienced as shame. The child who cannot submit the assignment. The adult who cannot complete the application. The senior who cannot access the telehealth appointment. Each of these moments carries the weight of technological inadequacy — the sense of being left behind by a system that the rest of the world is navigating smoothly. The shame is not warranted. The failure is systemic, not personal. But shame does not care about the origin of the failure. It attaches to the person who experiences it.

The church that sees this shame, names its systemic origin, and works to address it is doing pastoral work and prophetic work simultaneously. It is saying: your inability to get online is not evidence of your inadequacy. It is evidence of a system that has failed to extend its benefits equitably. And we are going to say so publicly and work on it practically.

Congregations in many cities have done exactly this: providing devices, partnering with programs that offer subsidized internet, advocating for municipal broadband, creating digital literacy training for seniors who were never integrated into the technological transition the rest of society went through. [cite — confirm] These are not grand gestures. They are the gleaning law applied to the harvest of the twenty-first century.

---

The question is not whether the church has the technical expertise to build the infrastructure. It does not.

The question is whether the church has the prophetic clarity to name whose children are being left behind and why, the pastoral presence to sit with the shame that the divide produces, and the practical commitment to use its organizational capacity to narrow a gap that is not accidental.

We have known for most of human history that access to resources concentrates. We have known what happens to the communities that end up on the wrong side of that concentration. The prophets knew. The law knew. Jesus knew, in the most specific terms, who the vulnerable were in his world and what the failure to reach them indicated about the heart of the community that called itself faithful.

The mechanisms change. The pattern does not. The obligation does not.

We know who is on the other side of this particular gap. The question is what we intend to do with that knowledge.

The prophetic tradition in Israel was not abstract about these things. It named the mechanisms — the specific ways that power and resources and access concentrated in the hands of those who already had them, at the direct cost of those who did not. It named the people who benefited from those arrangements and the people who were harmed by them. And it called the community that claimed to follow the God of the Exodus to the same standard God applied to Egypt: the measure of a community's faithfulness is not how well it treats those who have everything, but how it treats those who have nothing.

Access to education, to economic opportunity, to the systems through which contemporary society distributes its goods — this is the gleaning question of our moment. The vineyard has changed. The people left at the margins have not changed. The God who required the margin to be left for them has not changed.

The church that sees the gap and names it, that refuses to accept its children's exclusion from the infrastructure of opportunity as someone else's concern, is not engaging in politics. It is doing what faithfulness has always required: standing where the vulnerable stand, refusing to look away, and putting the weight of its organizational presence on the side of access rather than the side of comfortable silence.
