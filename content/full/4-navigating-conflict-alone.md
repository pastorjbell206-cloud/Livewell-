Every church has conflict. Pastors who pretend otherwise are either new or dishonest. The mistake most new pastors make is trying to handle it in isolation — without a mentor, a peer who can offer outside perspective, or a coach who has been through similar terrain.

Conflict handled in isolation tends to escalate or calcify. Bring it to someone you trust outside your congregation. Their perspective will almost always reveal angles you cannot see from inside the situation.

That piece of advice is correct and almost never followed. The reasons why it is not followed are worth examining, because they are not trivial. They have to do with shame, with the pastoral role's peculiar loneliness, and with a misunderstanding of what it means to lead well.

## Why Pastors Go It Alone

The loneliness of pastoral leadership is structurally produced. The congregation needs the pastor to be the strong one, the settled presence in the storm. That role creates a relentless pull toward the management of appearances — toward performing stability even when the interior is not stable, toward projecting competence even when the situation is beyond what any one person can manage alone. And conflict, more than almost anything else in ministry, exposes the gap between the managed appearance and the actual reality.

When a deacon challenges the pastor's direction in a board meeting. When a long-tenured family begins moving people to their side. When the associate pastor you hired and mentored begins conducting his own shadow conversations. These situations produce a kind of interior weather that the pastoral role was never designed to house alone — anxiety, anger, second-guessing, the specific grief of being opposed by people you love and have served at cost. The pastor who tries to carry that weather without outside help is not being strong. He is being isolated, and isolation does not produce good decisions.

I have tried to handle it alone. I have sat with a conflict situation for weeks, turning it over in my mind, running the same mental loops, arriving at the same confused conclusions, without once calling someone outside the congregation to say: *I am in a difficult situation and I do not know what to do.* What I told myself was that I was being discreet, protecting people's privacy, keeping the conflict from spreading. What I was actually doing was protecting myself from the humility required to admit I was stuck.

## What Isolation Does to a Conflict

Conflict handled in isolation does one of two things. It escalates — because the pastor, without any external check on his own anxiety or his own reactive instincts, handles the situation from a place of internal pressure that the situation does not deserve. Or it calcifies — because the pastor, afraid of making the wrong move, makes no move at all, and the unaddressed conflict hardens into permanent relational damage.

Neither outcome is what the congregation needs. And neither outcome is the mark of a leader. Real leadership in conflict requires something that solo processing cannot provide: the perspective of someone who is not inside the emotional field of the situation.

When you are inside a church conflict, everything is too close. The anxious elder seems like a threat to the entire ministry. The dissenting voice in the board meeting sounds like the voice of a faction trying to undermine everything. The family that is unhappy seems like the leading edge of a exodus. The proximity distorts. It makes small things large and large things larger. You need someone outside who can say: this is a difficult situation, and it is also manageable, and here is what people in your position have done that worked.

That external voice is not a luxury. It is a leadership requirement.

## The Biblical Shape of Counsel

The Book of Proverbs is direct about this: "Where there is no guidance, a people falls, but in an abundance of counselors there is safety" (Proverbs 11:14). The word translated "guidance" in that text is from a Hebrew root meaning the art of steering a ship [cite — confirm]. The image is navigation — not in the abstract, but in the practical sense of getting a vessel through difficult water without wreck. The proverb is saying that the person who tries to steer through dangerous conditions without counsel is not brave. He is reckless.

Jethro gives Moses the same advice in Exodus 18. Moses is trying to adjudicate every dispute in Israel by himself, and his father-in-law watches and says, plainly: "What you are doing is not good. You and the people with you will certainly wear yourselves out, for the thing is too heavy for you. You are not able to do it alone" (Exodus 18:17-18). Jethro is not criticizing Moses's capability. He is naming a structural impossibility. The load is not designed to be carried by one person, and the one person who keeps trying to carry it will break before the load does.

The advice Moses receives is about distributing judicial responsibility, but the principle reaches further. The pastor in conflict is in the same position — carrying something that is structurally too heavy for one person to carry well — and the Jethro principle applies. Get help. Not because you cannot lead, but because leadership requires it.

## What Outside Perspective Actually Provides

The mentor, peer, or coach you bring in outside your congregation provides several things that you cannot provide for yourself.

First, they provide emotional regulation. The person outside your congregation is not afraid of your conflict the way you are. It is not their ministry, their livelihood, their identity. That distance allows them to look at the situation without the distortion that proximity creates, and their relative calm can be transferred to you through the conversation. You arrive at the call tightly wound, and you leave it with a more accurate read of the actual scale of the problem.

Second, they provide pattern recognition. Someone who has been in ministry for twenty years has seen this particular conflict in its various forms before. The deacon who challenges the pastor's direction. The dissatisfied family. The associate who is growing his own following. These are not unique situations. They are patterns, and the person who has walked through the pattern before knows what the end of the road looks like — knows which moves tend to help and which tend to harm, which confrontations are necessary and which create more problems than they solve.

Third, they provide accountability. The pastor who processes a conflict alone processes it with his own instincts, his own reactivity, his own fear or anger driving the conclusions. The pastor who processes it with a trusted outside voice has to articulate the situation to someone else, and articulation requires clarity that internal rumination does not. You cannot give someone a muddled account without noticing that it is muddled. The discipline of explaining the situation is already a step toward understanding it.

## The Counter-Argument

The counter-position is real and worth taking seriously: there are situations where involving outside people in a church conflict makes things worse. Conflict spreads. Involving a coach or mentor sometimes means the situation becomes more people's knowledge than it needs to be. Some conflicts are genuinely private, and privacy protects the people in them.

All of that is true, and none of it is an argument for navigating conflict alone. It is an argument for choosing the outside voice carefully. The mentor in another city who has no connection to your congregation is not a vector for the conflict to spread — he is a sealed container in which you can process the weight without it leaking. The peer supervisor whose confidentiality you trust is not adding to the conflict. He is helping you survive it.

The pastor who uses the risk of spreading conflict as a reason to avoid all outside counsel is not being careful. He is being afraid. Fear dressed as wisdom is still fear.

## Building the Structure Before You Need It

The best time to identify the outside voice you will call in conflict is before the conflict arrives. Not when the elder is already hostile. Not when the faction has already formed. Before.

This means building peer relationships with pastors in other churches, at least one of whom is senior enough in ministry to have seen what you haven't. It means having a mentor relationship that is genuine — not the kind where you present the polished version of your ministry, but the kind where the other person actually knows you. It means being in a context — a cohort, a denomination's peer group, a coaching relationship — where you are known well enough that calling them in a crisis does not require starting from scratch.

The pastor who builds those relationships in the ordinary seasons of ministry will find, when the conflict comes, that he has something to call on. The pastor who only reaches out when things are already bad will find that a cold call to someone who barely knows him produces a generic and limited response.

This is not complicated. But it requires acknowledging, before the conflict, that the conflict will come. Every church has it. Pastors who pretend otherwise are either new or dishonest.

You are not the exception. You will need someone outside your congregation to help you see clearly. Build the relationship now. The conflict is already on its way, and you will be grateful you did.
