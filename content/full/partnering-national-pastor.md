The mission trip model that most American churches inherited was built on a set of assumptions so deep they were almost invisible: that the primary resource in the mission equation flows from here to there, that the expertise originates in the West and is received abroad, that the goal is to plant something that looks, when the team flies home, like a smaller version of what they left. Underneath all of it was a single organizing premise: we have what they need.

That premise is not evil. It is not even entirely wrong. American churches have resources — financial, educational, infrastructural — that many churches in the Global South still lack. The problem is not that the resources flow. The problem is the posture that comes with them. And that posture, when it goes unexamined, produces exactly the opposite of partnership.

Genuine partnership with a national pastor looks different from what most American churches practice. Understanding how different requires a brief honest account of the history that shaped the current arrangement.

The modern Western missionary movement — from William Carey's arrival in India in 1793 through the great expansion of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries [cite — confirm] — carried the gospel with genuine faithfulness to places where it had not arrived before. It also carried the assumptions of empire. The missionary was usually white, educated, and situated within colonial administrative structures that his very presence reinforced. He built institutions — schools, hospitals, churches — modeled on their Western equivalents. He trained indigenous leaders in Western modes of thought and organization. In many cases, he functioned as an agent of cultural disruption that went far beyond the theological content of what he proclaimed. The gospel and the colonizer traveled on the same ships. The church that exists today in much of Africa, Asia, and Latin America carries both inheritances.

This is not a reason to regret the missionary movement entire. The global church that now constitutes the majority of Christianity worldwide [cite — confirm] exists partly because of that movement. But it is a reason to ask, with genuine humility, what assumptions the American church still carries into its partnership arrangements — and what those assumptions cost the people on the other side of them.

The national pastor I am thinking of — and I am thinking of a real relationship, though I will not name him — leads a congregation in a context I do not understand from the inside. He speaks languages I do not speak. He navigates political pressures I have never faced. He shepherds people through losses that my suburban congregation in Michigan will, in most cases, not experience. He understands the theological tradition his community emerged from — the ways the gospel was preached to his people, the ways it was used against them, the ways they have reshaped it to speak into their own life — with a depth and nuance I cannot access from the outside.

When I arrived in his world and began talking about what we could do together, the first thing he did — graciously, without embarrassment — was explain to me that I had misunderstood what his community needed. The project I had imagined would not serve his congregation. The structure I had proposed replicated a pattern he had spent years trying to move away from. He was not ungrateful. He was honest. And in his honesty was the beginning of what actual partnership requires.

Actual partnership begins with listening rather than proposing. It requires a willingness to spend a significant amount of time understanding the context — the history, the cultural dynamics, the theological tradition, the specific challenges the pastor is navigating — before offering anything. Not because Western resources and perspectives have nothing to contribute. Because the pattern in which Western resources arrive with Western agendas and produce Western-shaped outcomes, regardless of what the receiving community actually needs, has been documented too thoroughly to ignore [cite — confirm].

The listening is not simply strategic — a smarter approach to accomplishing the same goal. It is theological. The body of Christ is not a body in which one part provides and the other parts receive. Paul's description in 1 Corinthians 12 is of mutual dependence, in which the parts that appear less honorable are actually indispensable (1 Corinthians 12:22–23). The American church that arrives in a Global South context with the posture of a provider has already decided that it is the stronger member — has already arranged the relationship in a way that contradicts the ecclesiology it claims to hold.

What does genuine partnership require in practice?

It requires a long relationship before significant resource transfer. The short-term mission trip model — two weeks in country, a project completed, a check written, and home — is almost structurally incapable of producing genuine partnership. Partnership requires the kind of knowledge that only develops over time: knowing the pastor's theological commitments, his community's history, what has been tried before and failed, what would be received as support and what would be received as intrusion. A single visit does not produce this knowledge. A decade of correspondence and mutual accountability might begin to.

It requires accountability structures that flow in both directions. Most Western partnership arrangements involve accountability from the national pastor to the Western donor — reports submitted, photos provided, outcomes documented. Genuine partnership inverts and equalizes this: the Western church is also accountable to the national pastor, who has the standing to challenge the assumptions the Western partner brings. This is uncomfortable for institutions accustomed to holding the financial power. It is theologically necessary.

It requires genuine deference on questions of contextualization. The national pastor knows how the gospel should be proclaimed and embodied in his context with a depth of knowledge the Western partner cannot match. When those two perspectives conflict — when the Western partner thinks the church should look or sound different than it does — the burden of proof is on the outside perspective, not the inside one. The Incarnation argues for this: God did not bring a universal model into the world. God entered a particular language, a particular culture, a particular body. Contextual faithfulness is not a compromise of the gospel. It is the mode in which the gospel has always arrived.

There is a real counter-concern here. The argument for genuine cross-cultural accountability includes the observation that theological error can be embedded in contextual practice in ways that are difficult to see from inside the context. Some partnerships have sent resources to national pastors who held theological positions the Western church would have challenged if it had known about them. Deference is not the same as abdication. The partnership involves genuine mutual theological accountability — and that requires the Western partner to have done enough theological work to have a real position, held with real conviction, not just the posture of endless openness.

But the imbalance in most existing partnerships runs heavily in the direction of Western assumption rather than Western deference. The correction needed is not more Western oversight. It is more genuine listening — and the discipline of letting the national pastor's knowledge of his context override the Western partner's categories for understanding it.

The PCN network has given me access to pastors around the world who are leading faithfully in contexts I would not understand without them. The most important thing those relationships have given me is not the satisfaction of having contributed something. It is the correction of my assumptions about what the global church looks like and what it needs. I arrived thinking I was bringing something. I received something more.

That is what genuine partnership produces. Not a better-run mission trip. A rearranged understanding of who is rich and who is poor in the economy of the kingdom — which turns out to run differently than the economy I was formed in.

The Macedonian churches in 2 Corinthians 8 gave "beyond their ability" (v. 3) to support the Jerusalem collection — churches that were themselves in "extreme poverty" (v. 2). Paul describes their giving not as charity toward the worse-off but as a participation in grace. The categories of who gives and who receives are scrambled in the kingdom economy. The American church that arrives in the Global South assuming it knows which category it occupies has not yet understood this.

The national pastor who told me I had misunderstood what his community needed was not refusing partnership. He was inviting me into one. The real kind — the kind in which I came home changed, not just the kind in which the project got done.

Those are not the same thing. Partnership worth the name produces the first. The second is easier to report and impossible to sustain.
