There was a time — not that long ago in historical terms — when the average congregation was not a cross-section of the political spectrum in quite the way that most congregations are today. Political diversity within a congregation existed, but political identity was not the kind of defining framework that it has become — was not the thing that people felt most keenly when they sat next to someone who voted differently, attended the same church as someone who held views they found incomprehensible, and tried to take communion alongside someone who they suspected did not share their sense of what was at stake in the world.

Today it is. The pastor who pretends otherwise — who acts as though the political diversity in the pew is not there, or not charged, or manageable through carefully crafted neutrality — is pastoring an imaginary congregation rather than the actual one in front of them.

## What Is Actually at Stake

The challenge of pastoring across political division is not primarily a political challenge. It is a theological one. The question is not how to manage different political opinions in the same room — that is a management challenge. The question is whether the gospel is genuinely bigger than the political identities that have come to define so much of American life, and whether the church is capable of being that embodied demonstration.

Paul's argument in Ephesians 2 is that the cross does not just reconcile humans to God — it reconciles humans to each other, creating a new community from people who were genuinely, historically divided. The Jew and the Gentile. The citizen and the foreigner. The free person and the slave. These were not minor differences in perspective. They were the defining fault lines of the ancient world, the divisions that organized social reality and determined status, safety, and belonging. And Paul's claim is that the church — specifically the church — is the community in which those divisions are being overcome.

The political divisions of twenty-first-century America are serious. They are not, by any historical measure, as serious as the Jew-Gentile divide that Paul is addressing. That was a chasm forged over centuries of mutual hostility, religious boundary-keeping, and social apartheid. And Paul's word to the Ephesians is that the cross has made "one new humanity in place of the two" (Ephesians 2:15). If the cross could accomplish that, the premise that it cannot hold a congregation divided over tax policy or immigration enforcement is a failure of theological nerve.

## The Historical Weight of This Moment

The current political polarization in American life is not simply a disagreement about policy. Sociologist Robert Bellah identified decades ago that America has always carried within it the tension between its civil religion — its sense of special national destiny — and the prophetic tradition that calls all national stories to account before the God of Scripture [cite — confirm]. What has happened in the twenty-first century is that the civil religion has fractured into competing tribal versions, each claiming the authority of ultimate conviction, each demanding the kind of loyalty that properly belongs to God alone.

James K.A. Smith's analysis of how our loves are formed by cultural liturgies — the rituals and practices and narratives that shape what we desire before we reason about it — helps explain why this is so intractable [cite — confirm]. People do not simply hold political opinions the way they hold opinions about restaurants. Their political identity has been formed through a long process of liturgical formation: the news they consume daily, the community that affirms their view of reality, the enemy-narrative that gives shape and meaning to their sense of the world. Changing that formation requires the same kind of patient, long-term, countercultural liturgical work that genuine discipleship has always required.

The pastor who faces a politically divided congregation is not simply managing a disagreement. They are standing at the intersection of two competing liturgical formations, each of which believes it is not merely political but moral — perhaps even sacred. The pastoral work required is not conflict management. It is counter-formation.

## What the Pastor Can and Cannot Do

The pastor can model. The pastor can preach a gospel that is genuinely larger than any political framework and embody, in their own relationships across political lines, what reconciliation looks like. The pastor can refuse to allow the church's gatherings to become partisan rallies in either direction. The pastor can speak the prophetic word that no political identity fully accommodates — challenging both sides with what the gospel actually demands.

What the pastor cannot do is eliminate the tension. The tension is real. The differences are real. The pastor's job is not to pretend they do not exist but to insist, from the authority of the gospel, that they are not ultimate — that the thing that unites the congregation in Christ is more fundamental than the things that divide them in the public square. Making that case compellingly, consistently, and with enough pastoral care for the real people on each side to feel genuinely received — that is the work.

## The Counter-Position, Honestly Held

There is a serious counterargument that must be named and answered. It goes like this: the church's claim to transcend politics is itself a political act — an exercise of power that privileges those who can afford to depoliticize their faith, who have never needed the church to be a site of resistance to injustice, who find the status quo comfortable enough that "rising above politics" means leaving it unchallenged. The person who has been harmed by specific policies, who has watched specific political decisions affect specific people they love, cannot simply set that down at the door and call it discipleship.

This is not a cynical argument. It is a serious one. And the response cannot be to dismiss it. The response is this: the prophetic tradition within Scripture — Amos, Micah, Isaiah, Jesus himself — was deeply political in its content while refusing to be domesticated by any political party. The prophets were not neutral. They named injustice with precision. But they named it from within the covenant framework, from within the claims of God on the whole life of the community, in ways that condemned both the false comfort of the powerful and the false despair of the oppressed. The church that refuses to name injustice in the name of political neutrality has abdicated the prophetic function. The church that names it only through the frame of one political party has surrendered it. The pastor's work is to recover the prophetic imagination that is genuinely independent — beholden not to a platform but to the kingdom.

## A Pastoral Scene

I have sat across from two members of my own congregation — people I love, who love Jesus, who would both die for the other if the need arose — and watched them look at each other with the specific pain of people who suspect they are enemies. One had attended a school board meeting and come away shaking with anger about what was being taught to children. The other had watched the same footage and come away shaking with anger about who was being targeted. They were responding to the same event with the same emotional intensity and the same conviction that something sacred was at stake.

I did not have a clever word to resolve it. I still do not. What I had was the Communion table, and the stubborn conviction that both of them belonged at it. What I had was the reminder that the same Paul who wrote Ephesians 2 wrote Romans 14 — that the community of faith has always contained people whose disagreements ran deep, and that the call is not to agree but to receive one another as Christ received us (Romans 15:7). That is not neutrality. It is a harder and higher demand than agreement.

## Practical Pastoral Strategies

Several specific practices help. First: preach through passages that address political realities — justice, power, care for the vulnerable, the sovereignty of God over human governments — and engage them honestly, without partisan framing, letting Scripture make its own claims. Romans 13, Revelation 13, Micah 6:8, Luke 4:18 — these texts have political freight. Preach them straight. Trust the congregation to receive them.

Second: create intentional space for cross-partisan relationship within the congregation — small groups that are not organized by affinity, service opportunities that bring different kinds of people together around shared mission, social gatherings that cross the informal social boundaries that parallel political ones.

Third: be willing to name the idolatry in both directions. The person who has made their political identity the organizing center of their life — who cannot imagine being genuinely committed to someone who votes differently — has placed something in the seat that belongs to Christ. Augustine's definition of idolatry as disordered love — loving something that is good with the kind of love that belongs only to God — applies here with precision [cite — confirm]. Politics is not nothing. But it is not ultimate. The pastor who cannot name that, from the pulpit, with equal charity toward both sides, has abdicated the prophetic function of the office.

Fourth: model cross-partisan friendship publicly. The pastor whose personal relationships with leaders in the community visibly cross political lines demonstrates, in action, what they are asking the congregation to practice. Hypocrisy in either direction — the pastor who preaches unity but privately dismisses one side — will be noticed by the people who trust that pastor most.

## The Steel Test

The congregation that has been genuinely formed by the gospel should be unable to be fully claimed by any political party. Both parties, in any healthy pluralist democracy, contain positions that a serious reading of Scripture affirms and positions that it challenges. The congregation that is a reliable voting bloc for one party — regardless of which party — has already been shaped by something other than the gospel as its primary loyalty.

I have said this from my own pulpit. I have watched the discomfort it produces on both sides of the aisle. That discomfort is not a pastoral failure. It is, I think, the gospel doing its work — unsettling the idolatries that political tribes require, insisting on an allegiance that precedes and exceeds every human political project.

Not every political opinion is equally valid. Prophetic preaching names injustice by name. But the community that can only hear the indictment of the other side's sins has ceased to be a congregation and become a caucus.

## The Weight at the End

The Communion table is the answer the church has been given, and it is an inadequate answer in the sense that it does not resolve the disagreements, and it is the only answer in the sense that nothing else can hold the room. This is the body of Christ given for you. Not for your tribe. For you. And for the person beside you who voted the way you did not, and whose fear for the country is as real as yours, and who belongs here as fully as you do.

The pastor's job is not to fix the polarization of American political life. It is to maintain, Sunday after Sunday, the stubbornly inconvenient claim that there is a unity deeper than politics and a loyalty that precedes every party platform. Most weeks, that claim will feel inadequate to the moment.

It is the only thing we have. It turns out to be enough.
