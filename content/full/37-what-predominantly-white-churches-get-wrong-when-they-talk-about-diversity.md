Conversations about racial diversity in predominantly white churches have been happening with increasing frequency for a decade or more, and the quality of those conversations varies enormously — from genuine engagement with Scripture and history and relationship that actually changes communities, to well-intentioned but ultimately shallow performances of commitment that change very little and leave the people they claim to be reaching for unconvinced and sometimes wounded.

This article is not an attack on the impulse. The impulse toward multiethnic, multicultural community in the church is a genuinely biblical one — the vision of Revelation 7, the breaking down of ethnic and social walls in Ephesians 2, the surprising inclusivity of Jesus throughout the Gospels, the radical social implications of the church in Acts where Jew and Gentile ate at the same table. The desire to embody that vision is right.

The mistakes that derail it, however, are common enough and specific enough to be worth naming directly.

## The Historical Weight of the Separation

American Christianity did not arrive at its racial homogeneity accidentally. The segregation of American churches is one of the most thoroughly documented features of American religious history, and its roots are not aesthetic or organic — they are structural and theological, the product of deliberate choices by the white church that bear examination before any conversation about diversity can be honest.

The denominational splits of the 1840s over slavery — which produced separate Baptist, Methodist, and Presbyterian denominations along sectional and racial lines — established institutional structures for racial separation that outlasted slavery by a century [cite — confirm]. The theological defense of segregation offered by Southern white churches from Reconstruction through the Civil Rights era was not a fringe position. It was a mainstream position, articulated by theologians and pastors who cited Scripture in its support. The white churches that were silent during the Civil Rights era were not passive observers — their silence was a form of complicity that allowed the system to function without the moral opposition that the church, of all institutions, was positioned to provide.

Martin Luther King Jr.'s "Letter from Birmingham Jail," written in April 1963 [cite — confirm], was addressed not to avowed segregationists but to moderate white clergy who counseled patience. Their moderation was, in his analysis, a greater obstacle to justice than the opposition of declared opponents. The church that is now, sixty years later, having earnest conversations about diversity is having them in the shadow of that history, whether it acknowledges it or not.

Acknowledging it is the beginning of having the conversation honestly. The predominantly white church that begins its diversity conversation without this historical context is trying to build on a foundation it has not yet examined.

## Mistake 1: Treating Diversity as a Goal Rather Than a Fruit

Diversity in a congregation is not something you produce through programming and intentional recruitment. It is something that grows from genuine relationships of mutual respect and care across racial and cultural lines. The predominantly white church that sets a demographic target — "we want to be X percent non-white by Y year" — has mistaken the fruit for the goal and is likely to pursue it in ways that feel extractive to the people they are recruiting.

The question is not "how do we get more diverse people in our church?" The question is "are we genuinely committed to the wellbeing and belonging of every person who walks through our door, and to the relationships that would produce a community where different kinds of people would genuinely want to stay?" The second question is harder, slower, and more likely to produce the fruit the first question is chasing.

The distinction matters because the predominantly white church that pursues demographic diversity as a goal tends to interpret the presence of people of color as evidence that the goal is being achieved, regardless of whether those people are genuinely belonging, genuinely influencing, and genuinely at home. A Black family sitting in your congregation on a Sunday morning is not evidence of a healthy multiethnic community if that family is invisible to the culture of the church, if their presence is noted and celebrated but their perspective is never consulted, if they experience the church as a place where they are welcome to attend but not to fully belong.

## Mistake 2: Making People of Color the Teachers Without Making Them the Leaders

A common pattern in predominantly white churches that are genuinely trying to grow in racial awareness is to invite people of color to speak, to share their experience, to educate the congregation about the realities of racism — while the actual leadership of the church remains predominantly white. This is not integration. It is the use of people of color as educational resources for the benefit of white congregants, without the commensurate power-sharing that genuine partnership requires.

Jemar Tisby, in *The Color of Compromise*, argues that this dynamic is itself a form of racial accommodation — the white church finding a way to benefit from the presence and perspective of Black believers without being fundamentally changed by it [cite — confirm]. The church that hosts a racial reconciliation Sunday and invites a Black pastor to preach has not changed its culture. It has added an event.

If the church's commitment to diversity is genuine, it will show up in leadership — in who is on the elder board, in who occupies staff positions, in whose voice shapes the culture of the community. The church that hires a "diversity pastor" while the senior leadership team remains unchanged has added a role without changing the culture. It has found a way to address the appearance of the problem while leaving its structure intact.

This is not an accusation. It is a description of a pattern that I have observed in churches where the desire for change is genuine but the willingness to share power has not yet caught up with the desire. Shared leadership is not just an organizational adjustment — it is a theological statement about whose wisdom the church trusts.

## Mistake 3: Asking People of Color to Come to You Rather Than Going to Them

The final and perhaps most fundamental mistake is the assumption that a diverse church is built by convincing people of color to attend a white church. This assumption contains, often unexamined, a hierarchy: the white church is the center, and the people being invited are being asked to accommodate its culture, its worship idiom, its relational patterns, its unspoken norms.

The more genuinely biblical vision is for predominantly white churches to pursue genuine relationship with predominantly minority churches — to be genuinely in each other's spaces, to collaborate on shared mission, to let the relationship reshape both communities rather than asking one community to accommodate the culture of the other.

This requires white pastors to be genuinely willing to receive from, be shaped by, and follow the leadership of their brothers and sisters of color. It requires sitting in their churches and noticing that the worship is different and that different is not deficient. It requires the humility to acknowledge that the white church's cultural practices are not the universal standard against which other Christian traditions are measured.

Ephesians 2:14-16 describes Christ as having "made the two groups one and has destroyed the barrier, the dividing wall of hostility." That wall was a specific, structural, theological barrier — not a feeling, not a preference, but a dividing structure that organized the community of God's people by separating them. The churches in your city that are predominantly white and predominantly non-white are separated by something structural, not something natural. The work of breaking down that structure cannot be done by asking one side to move toward the other. It requires both communities moving together.

## The Counter-Position Answered

The strongest objection to emphasizing racial diversity in the church is that it imports the categories of secular identity politics into the community of God's people, where the only relevant category is "neither Jew nor Gentile, slave nor free" (Galatians 3:28), and that the church's excessive attention to racial identity reflects a capitulation to cultural pressure rather than fidelity to the gospel.

This objection has genuine theological content that deserves a real answer rather than a dismissal.

The answer is this: Galatians 3:28 is not a statement that racial and ethnic difference ceases to exist in Christ. It is a statement that racial and ethnic difference ceases to determine access, status, or belonging in Christ. The Jewish identity of the apostles did not disappear at Pentecost — it was sanctified and brought into the service of a mission that transcended it. The argument that the church should be racially and ethnically indifferent does not reflect the vision of Revelation 7, where the nations are gathered in their particularity before the throne — not erased into uniformity, but united in the worship of the one who made them all.

The church that is not attending to the racial dynamics of its community is not being racially neutral. It is allowing the default patterns of racial separation in American life to organize the body of Christ without examination. That is not faithfulness. It is passivity dressed as theology.

## What Honest Progress Looks Like

A pastor I respect leads a predominantly white church in a mid-sized Midwestern city. Three years ago, he began a formal partnership with a predominantly Black church eight blocks away — not a diversity program, but a genuine pastoral friendship between the two senior pastors that expanded into shared worship, shared mission in their common neighborhood, and eventually shared governance of several joint initiatives.

He told me that the hardest part was not the logistics. It was the moment he realized that the partnership was changing him — that the worship idiom of his partner church was awakening something in his own congregation's worship that a decade of inside-the-tradition renewal had not produced. He was not absorbing the Black church's culture. He was encountering it, and the encounter was doing something in him that no program had managed.

That is what the fruit of genuine partnership looks like. Not a demographic target met. Not a hiring milestone achieved. A community that looks more like what God intended it to be, because the people in it allowed themselves to be genuinely shaped by people unlike them.

It is slower. It requires more of everyone. It produces something more real than a diversity initiative has ever produced.

The wall does not come down by talking about it. It comes down when the people on both sides of it decide that what is on the other side is worth the cost of the crossing.
