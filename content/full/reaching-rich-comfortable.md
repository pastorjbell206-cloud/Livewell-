The rich young ruler walked away sad (Mark 10:22). Jesus let him go.

This moment tends to make us uncomfortable, so we soften it. We note that Jesus loved him (Mark 10:21), which is true. We point to the broader context about things being possible with God, which is also true. What we do less readily is sit with the plain sequence of events: a wealthy, morally serious, spiritually seeking young man asked Jesus what was required, Jesus told him to sell what he had and give it to the poor, and the man left. And Jesus did not call after him with a more accommodating answer. He watched him go and then told his disciples: it is very hard for the wealthy to enter the kingdom of God.

Very hard. Not impossible, but the disciples' astonishment — "Then who can be saved?" — tells us they heard this as a radical narrowing of expectation. Jesus had just made a theological statement about the relationship between accumulated wealth and the kingdom, and the statement was not comfortable.

The American church has worked very hard to make it comfortable again.

Not explicitly. No one gives a sermon titled "the rich young ruler was wrong to leave." But the theology gets reconstructed through emphasis — through what gets said and what does not, through whose stories are told from the stage, through whose comfort level shapes the ministry culture. A congregation that measures its success by the quality of its facilities and the income level of its zip code has made choices about which demographic it is optimized to reach. Those choices are theological, even when they are not named as such.

## The Principled Defense

There are articulate defenses of ministry that disproportionately engages the wealthy. Wealthy people have resources that multiply ministry impact. A congregation with financial stability can fund missionaries, support benevolence, sustain programs that serve the poor. The wealthy need the gospel too, and it would be reverse snobbery to treat their souls as less valuable. To deliberately avoid reaching successful people out of some romantic preference for the poor would be its own error. These arguments are not frivolous.

But they have a tendency to become cover for something less defensible: the decision to build ministry cultures that are comfortable for people with disposable income, located in areas accessible to the affluent, structured around programming sensibilities that reflect middle and upper-class assumptions, staffed by leaders whose personal financial situation insulates them from the daily precarity that shapes the lives of the poor. None of this is consciously chosen as anti-poor policy. It is the accumulated effect of a thousand small decisions, each of which felt pragmatic rather than theological.

The road paved with pragmatism arrives, eventually, at a destination that is not the one the gospel describes.

## The Shape of Jesus's Ministry

Jesus's ministry did not look like what the comfortable church has built. He was based in Galilee, not Jerusalem — a provincial region associated with economic marginality and limited cultural prestige [cite — confirm]. His primary audience was peasant farmers, fishermen, people living at subsistence level under both Roman taxation and the temple tax system. The twelve he chose were not drawn from the educated class. When he described the scope of his mission in Luke 4:18, borrowing from Isaiah 61, the list was specific: the poor, the captive, the blind, the oppressed.

These are not metaphorical categories. They are social descriptions of people at the bottom of every hierarchy in the ancient world. Jesus did not come primarily to people who had other good options and were wondering which way to optimize their lives. He came to people for whom the good news was genuinely, materially news — who had nothing else to try, nowhere else to go.

Luke's Gospel preserves the starkest account of this orientation. In Luke 6:20–26, Matthew's "poor in spirit" becomes simply "poor" — the literal poor, those who literally hunger, those who literally weep. And the woes are addressed to the literally rich, the literally full, the literally comfortable. Luke is not spiritualizing. He is quoting a Jesus who said things that made the established order uncomfortable, and the reason this Jesus gets softened in so many sermons is precisely because his comfort level with the poor makes us uneasy about our own.

Paul makes an observation about the Corinthian church that reads as almost sociological: "Not many of you were wise according to worldly standards, not many were powerful, not many were of noble birth" (1 Corinthians 1:26). The church at Corinth was disproportionately poor, low-status, marginalized. Paul does not treat this as a problem to solve. He treats it as a revelation of God's logic — that God chose the foolish to shame the wise, the weak to shame the strong, the low and despised to bring to nothing the things that are (1 Corinthians 1:27–28). This is not accidental. It is the sign of what kind of movement this is and who it was built around.

## What the Historical Record Shows

The reforming movements in church history that most faithfully embodied the gospel's priorities have consistently been movements that moved toward the poor rather than the wealthy. The early Methodist movement in eighteenth-century England deliberately engaged the coal miners, mill workers, and urban poor of an industrializing society that the established church had largely abandoned [cite — confirm]. William Booth founded the Salvation Army in the slums of East London in 1865 [cite — confirm], not in the drawing rooms of the middle class. Dorothy Day and the Catholic Worker movement planted their houses in the poorest neighborhoods of American cities, not in the suburbs where the giving was reliable [cite — confirm].

These movements did not avoid the wealthy. They did not treat the wealthy as beyond reach. But their gravitational center was at the bottom of the social order, not the top, and that center shaped everything — their vocabulary, their priorities, their institutional culture, their willingness to bear costs that a prosperity-oriented church would never accept.

The revivalism that shaped American evangelicalism moved in a different direction. Charles Finney's anxious bench was pitched to farmers and merchants in upstate New York [cite — confirm], not to the urban poor. Dwight Moody's revivals were sustained by the donations of wealthy industrialists like Cyrus McCormick and Jay Cooke [cite — confirm]. The arrangement was not corrupt. But it created dependencies that shaped what could and could not be said, and what populations were and were not centered in the ministry's imagination.

By the twentieth century, the megachurch movement that came to define evangelical success had largely completed the orientation toward the suburban middle class. The mega-facilities that became the flagship of American Christian witness were built in the fastest-growing, wealthiest suburbs — not by accident, but by deliberate demographic targeting. The prosperity gospel that emerged from this environment was not an aberration. It was the logical conclusion of a trajectory that had been building for a century.

## The Cost to Witness

I have made decisions about where to focus ministry energy that followed the logic of resource efficiency rather than the logic of the cross. I have considered which neighborhoods offered the best growth potential, which demographics were most likely to give, which partnerships would enhance the church's reputation. That is not entirely wrong — stewardship requires thinking about resources. But when it becomes the primary logic, it has displaced something essential.

The cross is not efficient. It reaches down rather than up. It spends toward the lost rather than the found, the marginal rather than the central, the ones who cannot reciprocate rather than the ones who can write a check.

The practical consequence of the church's orientation toward the comfortable is a credibility problem among the poor. The people most likely to have no other advocate — the incarcerated, the undocumented, the homeless, the addicted — tend to have the most precisely calibrated sense of whether an institution is actually on their side or is performing concern while protecting its donors. They have long memories for the times the church showed up and the times it didn't. They notice the difference between the church that comes to their neighborhood when there is a photo opportunity and the church that is there every Tuesday regardless.

That credibility is not recovered easily. It is built, or not built, in the thousand small decisions about where to locate, who to staff, what to fund, whose voices to center in the church's public witness. The rich young ruler walked away, and Jesus let him go. But Jesus stayed — in Galilee, among the poor, with the people the religious establishment had already written off.

I have not consistently done the same. I have made the easier calls more often than I want to admit, and the accumulated result is a ministry that reflects my comfort more than his priorities.

The hard thing about the rich young ruler story is not what Jesus said. It is what it reveals about the cost of saying nothing. The ruler walked away sad. Jesus watched him go. And then, in the silence after the departure, he turned to the disciples and told them the truth about money and the kingdom.

We have been qualifying that truth ever since. The qualifications are understandable. They are also the reason the poor continue to find the church — with its enormous resources and its enormous silence — more comfort to the comfortable than to them.
