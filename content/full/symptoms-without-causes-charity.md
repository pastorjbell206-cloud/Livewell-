There is a story, attributed to various sources over the years [cite — confirm], about people standing on a riverbank pulling drowning bodies from the water. They work heroically, exhausting themselves, saving every person they can reach. Then someone turns and walks upstream. The others call out: where are you going? We need you here. And the one walking says: I'm going to find out who's throwing people in.

The story is not an argument against rescuing the drowning. It is an argument about the limits of rescue when throwing continues uninterrupted.

The church in America is very good at the riverbank. We run food pantries, emergency assistance funds, coats for kids drives, and Christmas toy collections. These things are not nothing. They are genuine expressions of love and genuine responses to genuine need. I have been part of them. I am grateful for them. The people who received a bag of groceries from our pantry were helped by that bag of groceries. The help was real.

But the bag of groceries did not change why they needed it. And if the same person returns to the pantry for the fourth consecutive month, we are managing a symptom. The cause — whatever arrangement of income and cost and lack of savings and medical debt and housing instability produced the need — is still operating. The throwing continues. We are on the riverbank.

---

This distinction has a theological lineage. It is not a recent political invention.

The Hebrew prophets were not opposed to generosity. They were opposed to generosity that functioned as a substitute for justice. Amos is the sharpest voice: "I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies. Even though you offer me your burnt offerings and grain offerings, I will not accept them" (Amos 5:21–22). The community was performing religious obligation while leaving the structural arrangements of economic exploitation untouched. God called their worship noise. Not because the offerings were insincere but because they were incomplete — they addressed the individual's religious duty without addressing the social conditions the same individuals were creating and benefiting from.

The eighth century BCE in Israel was a period of extraordinary prosperity for the wealthy and extraordinary precariousness for the poor. Trade routes through the northern kingdom were producing surplus wealth at the top. At the same time, subsistence farmers were losing their land through debt mechanisms, selling themselves and their families into debt servitude, finding the legal system captured by the wealthy so that appeals to justice were unavailing. [cite — confirm] The religious calendar continued. The temples were full. The offerings were generous. And God, through Amos, declared it all an abomination — not because worship was bad, but because worship performed while exploiting the poor was a theological contradiction of the most serious kind.

Israel could not simultaneously claim to worship a God who "executes justice for the oppressed, who gives food to the hungry, who sets prisoners free" (Psalm 146:7) and also construct economic and legal arrangements designed to produce oppression, hunger, and captivity. The worship was exposing the contradiction without resolving it. And the prophetic word was that God preferred the resolution to the worship.

---

The structural alternative to symptom treatment is not the absence of generosity. It is the disruption of the system that produces the symptoms alongside the relief of the people it harms.

Amos names both dimensions. The immediate requirement — "Seek good, not evil, that you may live" (Amos 5:14) — is inseparable from the structural requirement — "Let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24). The image is hydrological, not transactional. Water rolling down is not something you hand out. It is something that changes the shape of the land. The stream analogy describes not a one-time gift but an ongoing, self-sustaining current of right ordering. Not the distribution of charity to individuals. The reshaping of the conditions in which those individuals live.

What this looks like in practice depends on what the system producing the suffering actually is. Food insecurity requires understanding the arrangements of agricultural policy, food distribution infrastructure, wages, and housing costs that produce it — and then addressing those arrangements, not only their immediate human consequences. [cite — confirm] Housing instability requires understanding the mechanisms of the housing market, the relationship between zoning policy and affordability, the structures that produce displacement — and addressing those, not only providing emergency shelter to the people displaced. Poverty wages require understanding the economic and political dynamics that make poverty wages possible and profitable — and naming them, not only helping individuals survive the consequences.

---

I have been part of churches that had flourishing benevolence ministries and no public voice on the policies that produced the need those ministries were serving. We helped people survive the consequences of arrangements we never named. That is not full faithfulness to the prophetic tradition. It is the acceptable half — the half that does not cost relationships with wealthy donors or require public positions on contested policy questions or put the pastor on the wrong side of the congregation's political preferences.

This is not an accident. Charity is safe. Justice is costly. The food pantry improves the church's reputation in the community. The prophetic challenge to the economic arrangements that produce food insecurity costs relationships with the people who benefit from those arrangements. The benevolence ministry attracts donors who feel good about helping. The structural critique alienates donors who feel implicated by it.

The result, across decades of American evangelical practice, is a church that has developed extraordinary capacity for charitable distribution and almost no capacity for structural analysis or prophetic challenge. We know how to give. We do not know how to name what produces the need.

William Booth, who founded the Salvation Army in nineteenth-century London, was driven by this very tension. [cite — confirm] He watched the poor of the East End suffer from immediate deprivation and addressed it directly with soup and bread and shelter. But he also understood that the poverty was structural — that the industrial economy was producing it, that the political arrangements were sustaining it, that the church's charity was necessary but not sufficient. The tension he held between immediate relief and structural change is the tension the church has mostly resolved by choosing the comfortable side.

---

The objection deserves honest engagement: policy change is slow, contested, uncertain. Direct charity helps people now, in this week, in this particular emergency. You cannot feed a child with a policy paper. True.

The choice between charity and justice is not a real choice — both are necessary, and the church should be doing both. The problem is not the food pantry. The problem is the church that has chosen only charity and told itself this is equivalent to justice. It is not. It is the easier choice. It is the choice that keeps the uncomfortable questions unasked.

The harder questions are always: why? Why does this family need emergency food assistance three months in a row? Why does this neighborhood have no grocery store within two miles? Why does this child's school have outdated textbooks while the school across the district line has a new gymnasium? Why does this worker earn wages that require emergency assistance to supplement even when working full time? [cite — confirm] Those questions lead to systems, to policies, to arrangements that benefit some people at the expense of others. The people benefiting are often in our pews. The people paying the cost are often in our pantry lines.

---

Charity without justice is not neutral. It functions as a pressure valve — enough relief to prevent the suffering from becoming politically destabilizing, not enough change to alter the conditions that produce it. That is a service to the arrangement, not a challenge to it. The church that maintains the pressure valve while declining to address the pressure source has, in effect, chosen sides. It has chosen the side that benefits from the arrangement continuing.

That is a theological statement, whether or not it is intended as one.

The Jubilee legislation of Leviticus 25 makes this concrete in a way that charity never could. Every fifty years, land returned to its original family, debts were cancelled, people who had sold themselves into servitude were freed. The Jubilee was not charity. It was structural reset — the recognition that economic arrangements, left to themselves, produce concentration of wealth and displacement of the poor, and that justice requires periodic, deliberate interruption of those arrangements. Whether Israel ever fully practiced the Jubilee is historically debated, [cite — confirm] but the legislation's existence tells us something important: the biblical framework included not only generosity toward those harmed by unjust arrangements but the active dismantling of those arrangements at regular intervals.

The church has the Jubilee in its tradition. It has mostly preached the food pantry.

The prophetic tradition insists on going upstream. It insists on naming who is throwing and why, and demanding that the throwing stop, and working to dismantle the mechanisms that make throwing profitable and the consequences of throwing invisible. It does all of this while also pulling people from the river, because the drowning are drowning now, and the work of justice is long and the need is immediate.

Both. The river is not a metaphor for patience. It is a metaphor for the scope of the calling.

Pull people from the river. Walk upstream. Name what is happening. Do not call the first activity the whole of the second.

The church is called to both. It has mostly chosen one.

That is not faithfulness. It is comfort wearing faithfulness's clothes.
