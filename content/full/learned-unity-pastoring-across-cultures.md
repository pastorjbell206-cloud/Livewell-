Several years ago I was part of a ministry collaboration that put me in regular pastoral relationship with leaders whose cultural backgrounds were very different from mine — African American, Latino, first-generation immigrant from West Africa. We were trying to build something together. What we built instead, at least for the first eighteen months, was a very polite set of misunderstandings.

I thought we were unified. They were gracious enough not to tell me immediately that we were not.

What I understood about unity at that point was essentially: shared doctrine and shared purpose, expressed in a shared room. If we believed the same things and were working toward the same goals, we were unified. That definition is not wrong. It is severely insufficient. It describes the minimum conditions for unity the way describing a marriage as "two people who live in the same house" describes the minimum conditions for marriage. Technically accurate. Existentially empty.

---

What I learned — slowly, at cost, often from feedback I had to be pushed to hear — is that unity across cultural lines requires something that same-culture community never demands: the willingness to be wrong about your own assumptions.

The white pastor in a cross-cultural partnership brings a set of operational assumptions about time, authority, decision-making, communication, the role of community in individual decisions, and dozens of other categories, all of which feel to him like simply "how things work." They do not feel like cultural assumptions because, in his experience, no one has ever challenged them. They are the water he swims in.

The moment he is in genuine relationship with leaders who operate from different assumptions, the water becomes visible. And that visibility is uncomfortable in direct proportion to how invested he is in his own way of doing things being the right way of doing things.

I was very invested. I am constitutionally certain. I process quickly and speak before I should. I thought that made me a good leader. My brothers from other cultural contexts taught me that it made me someone who was very difficult to be in genuine partnership with, because the speed and certainty left no room for the slower, more communal discernment processes that their cultures understood as wisdom.

The process I was racing past was not inefficiency. It was how their communities had, across generations, tested decisions for wisdom before committing to them. The communal consultation was not a drag on the decision. It was the quality control. And my impatience with it was not a leadership strength. It was a cultural assumption dressed up as efficiency.

---

Paul writes in Romans 15:7, "Welcome one another as Christ has welcomed you, for the glory of God." The Greek word there — proslambanesthe — carries the sense of taking someone alongside you, drawing them into your company. It is not a word for tolerating someone's presence. It is a word for active inclusion, for making room, for genuinely receiving what the other person brings into the shared space.

That word welcome, in its full sense, does not mean making someone comfortable within your existing structures. It means changing your structures enough to actually include them. The church that welcomes people of different cultural backgrounds into a service whose aesthetic, liturgical structure, preaching style, community rhythms, and decision-making processes are entirely shaped by one cultural tradition has not welcomed them. It has extended a polite invitation to assimilate.

This is not unity. This is majority culture extending a conditional invitation.

The Ephesians passage is the most sustained biblical treatment of cross-cultural unity in the New Testament. Paul writes about the dividing wall of hostility between Jew and Gentile — not a metaphorical wall, but the literal stone barrier in the Jerusalem temple that marked where Gentiles could not pass, on penalty of death [cite — confirm]. Christ has demolished that wall (Ephesians 2:14). He has created "in himself one new man in place of the two" (Ephesians 2:15). The key phrase is "one new man" — not the Gentile being absorbed into the Jewish community, not the Jew being absorbed into the Gentile community, but something genuinely new, a third thing, a community that does not exist before the work of Christ creates it.

Most cross-cultural church conversations are not aiming at the third thing. They are aiming at one community graciously accommodating the presence of another. That is generosity. It is not the "one new man" that Paul describes.

---

The formation that my cross-cultural partnerships produced in me was real, and it ran in specific directions.

My understanding of grief changed. The West African tradition my colleagues carried included a way of communal grief — extended, public, physically present, sustained across weeks — that was almost entirely foreign to how I had been formed to grieve and to pastor grieving people. The "move through it and forward" framework I carried was not biblical. It was cultural. The psalms of lament last longer than a week. The book of Lamentations does not resolve. The early church's practice of extended mourning, preserved in Eastern Christian traditions, is closer to the African pattern than to the efficient, processed, ready-to-move-on approach I had been applying to the pastoral care of grieving people.

My understanding of authority changed. The cultures I was in relationship with carried a deep respect for elder wisdom — not deference to age as such, but reverence for the kind of authority that comes from having lived long enough to carry the community's memory and to have earned trust through sustained faithfulness. That authority was consulted before major decisions. It was not bypassed because the decision seemed obvious. The pastor who makes decisions without consulting the community's wisdom carriers has not streamlined the process. He has damaged a relationship and signaled that he does not actually need what they carry.

My preaching changed. Not my theology. But my understanding of which aspects of the text the people in front of me most needed to hear. I had been preaching from a position of cultural security — the assumption that my hearers' primary context was middle-class white evangelical American culture, with its specific anxieties and its specific assumptions about God and success and personal flourishing. My brothers from other cultural traditions read the same texts and heard different words landing — the words about God's presence in suffering, the words about the enemy's impotence against the one who walks with God, the words about the God who sees the oppressed and acts on their behalf. These were not different texts. They were the same texts read from different positions in the social order.

That reading is not less theological than mine. It is more textually complete. The prosperity of the majority culture reader has been editing out the passages that most directly address the experience of people for whom prosperity is not the presenting context.

---

There is a counter-position worth engaging. It says: the emphasis on cultural difference can itself become an obstacle to unity. The church is called to transcend cultural divisions, not to perform them. The preacher who is always contextualizing the text for specific cultural communities is fragmenting the congregation rather than unifying it. The gospel has a universal claim that transcends cultural particularity, and the church that makes too much of cultural difference risks being more sociological than theological in its self-understanding.

This is a genuine concern. The gospel is not a cultural artifact. Christ is not the property of any ethnicity. The New Testament's most dramatic claim about the community of faith is that it crosses lines that human beings treat as ultimate — there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female (Galatians 3:28). That is a real theological claim with real social consequences, and it must not be surrendered in the name of cultural sensitivity.

But the claim that the gospel transcends culture cannot become the claim that culture does not matter. The gospel has always been received in cultural contexts, and the way it is received reflects the assumptions of those contexts. The insistence that a particular cultural form of Christianity is simply Christianity, without the modifier, is not the affirmation of the gospel's universality. It is the particular culture's failure to recognize itself as a culture.

What Paul describes in Ephesians 2 is not the erasure of Jewish and Gentile particularity in the name of a new uniformity. It is the creation of something in which both particulars are held, transformed, and surpassed. The Jew does not stop being Jewish. The Gentile does not stop being Gentile. But neither one's cultural inheritance determines the community's life. Both are placed under the lordship of the one who has made peace between them.

---

That kind of unity formation cannot happen at a diversity conference. It cannot happen through reading. It happens in relationship sustained long enough to produce genuine trust and genuine friction — because trust without friction is politeness, and politeness is not unity.

What homogeneous churches miss is not the aesthetic variety of diverse worship. What they miss is the theological depth that comes from encountering the same God through genuinely different eyes.

I became a different pastor through those relationships. The certainty did not disappear — it never does, entirely, for people constituted the way I am. But it acquired a companion it had not had before: the genuine awareness that the person across the table from me might be right about something I am wrong about, and that the only way I am going to find out is by staying in the room long enough for the friction to happen, and by not leaving when it does.

That staying is the work. Not the conference attendance, not the curriculum, not the well-intentioned programs that perform diversity without producing it.

The work is the staying.

I needed to learn to stay.

I am still learning it.
