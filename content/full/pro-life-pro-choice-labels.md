The labels arrived before most of the conversations did. And now they do most of the work — sorting people into camps, signaling which team you're on, ending discussions that never actually began.

This is not an accident. Labels serve a political function. They compress complex positions into recognizable signals, make coalitions possible, and allow people to communicate their allegiances quickly in environments where careful argument is neither expected nor rewarded. The political economy of labels is real, and the people who deploy them are not being irrational.

What they are being is imprecise. And in a conversation where the precision matters — where real people and real lives and real theological convictions are at stake — imprecision carries a cost that the political efficiency cannot offset.

## What the Labels Hide

Here is what the labels hide: most people are not at either pole. Most people believe that human life in the womb has real moral weight. Most people also believe that the circumstances of a woman's life matter — that desperation, poverty, age, and coercion are real factors that simple prohibition does not adequately address. Most people hold both of those convictions simultaneously, imperfectly, with genuine tension between them.

The labels do not have room for that person. So she stays quiet, or she picks a side she does not entirely believe in, or she leaves the conversation altogether because it asks her to be simpler than she is. The cost of participation is the surrender of her actual position in favor of a position that fits the available categories.

This is how political sorting produces theological impoverishment. The categories that are useful for organizing coalitions are not the categories that are useful for doing theology. Theology requires the ability to hold complexity, to follow arguments past their comfortable conclusions, to be changed by the hardest version of an opposing position rather than by the caricature of it.

## The Proverbs 18 Principle

Proverbs 18:17 states it plainly: "In a lawsuit the first to speak seems right, until someone comes forward and cross-examines." The first position in the room always sounds more convincing than it will after cross-examination. The pro-life case, stated at its strongest, is serious and should be taken seriously. The pro-choice case, stated at its strongest, is also serious and should also be taken seriously — not as a rhetorical concession, but as an intellectual obligation.

A conviction that has never been cross-examined by the hardest version of the opposing argument is not a conviction. It is a preference that has not been tested yet. And a church that insulates its people from the strongest opposing arguments — because those arguments might be "confusing" or "dangerous" — has produced people whose convictions cannot withstand the first serious challenge from the actual world.

I have met people who left their faith because they encountered an argument they had never been equipped to answer — not because the argument was unanswerable, but because no one had thought it important enough to give them the tools. The church's habit of protecting people from hard questions is not a pastoral kindness. It is a pastoral failure that presents itself as one.

## What the Label "Pro-Life" Costs Its Holders

The pro-life label carries with it a set of implied associations that its holders have not always examined. In its current political form, it implies opposition to abortion at the legislative level, alignment with a particular political party, and a set of other policy positions that have become bundled with it in the coalition. A person who accepts the label accepts the bundle, whether or not the bundle accurately represents her convictions.

The person who believes that the unborn child has full moral standing from conception but also believes that the criminalization of abortion without the social infrastructure to support the women and children affected is a form of cruelty — that person finds herself unable to fully inhabit either camp. The pro-life camp expects policy support she cannot offer. The pro-choice camp hears her views on fetal personhood and closes the door.

She is not confused. She is consistent in a way that the available labels cannot accommodate. And the church — which should be the place where that kind of consistency is not only possible but expected — has often made the situation worse by aligning itself so fully with one of the labels that it has made the person who cannot adopt it feel unwelcome.

## What the Label "Pro-Choice" Costs Its Holders

The symmetrical problem exists on the other side. The pro-choice label, in its standard deployment, implies not just a legal position about who should make reproductive decisions but a set of philosophical commitments about the nature of personhood, the moral status of the fetus, and the primacy of individual autonomy that many people who favor legal access to abortion do not actually hold.

The person who supports legal abortion access in the first trimester, favors significant restrictions in the third, believes that abortion represents a genuine moral loss even when it is the right choice, and would like to see the conditions that make abortion feel necessary addressed at their root — that person is using the word "pro-choice" to describe a position that the label was not designed to contain.

She is making a morally serious argument. The label is not adequate to it. And the political environment, which has no use for positions that cannot be categorized, gives her nowhere to stand.

## What the Church Should Be

The church should be the place where the complicated conversation can happen. Not because the church has no convictions — it does, and they are not negotiable — but because those convictions need to be held by people who have genuinely reckoned with the hardest objections to them. A church that has only ever heard its own positions stated by people who agree with them has not done theology. It has done affirmation.

The biblical tradition is not afraid of hard argument. The book of Job is a sustained cross-examination of theodicy that does not end in a tidy answer. The Psalms of lament contain arguments addressed to God that would be removed from any polite conversation. The prophets did not simply announce positions; they argued them against the counterclaims of the culture and the competing interests of the people they were addressing.

The church that creates space for genuine engagement with the hardest version of the opposing position — not to capitulate to it, but to understand it — is the church that produces convictions worth holding.

## A Concrete Proposal

What would it look like for a congregation to create a space where a woman who has had an abortion could speak honestly, where a woman who chose life in desperate circumstances could tell her full story, where the people who argue over policy in the abstract had to sit with the people whose lives are the subject of the argument?

Not a debate format. Not a panel with positions already staked out. A genuine encounter between people and their actual experiences — the kind of encounter that is impossible to maintain at the level of the label because the label does not have a face.

The labels would dissolve. The actual human questions would remain. People would be forced to hold the complexity that the labels allow them to avoid — the moral weight of the unborn life and the moral weight of the woman's circumstances, simultaneously, without the relief of a category that makes one of them disappear.

That is exactly what makes it worth doing. And exactly what makes it difficult. And exactly the kind of difficulty that the church, of all places, should be willing to attempt.

The easy conversation is already happening everywhere. It produces heat and no light, conviction and no change, certainty and no wisdom. The church does not need to replicate it.

It needs to be the place where the harder conversation is possible. Where the person who is too complicated for the available labels can still find a home. Where the conviction that human life is sacred is held with the same seriousness as the conviction that the people whose lives are most affected by this debate deserve to be seen as more than their policy positions.

The labels stopped the conversation. The church can start a different one. That decision is not abstract. It requires a specific pastor in a specific congregation deciding to make the room large enough for the people whose actual convictions are more complicated than the available categories permit.

That pastor will be accused of hedging. Of refusing to take a clear position. Of making room where room should not be made. The accusation is the cost of the decision, and the decision is worth making anyway — because the people on the other side of the accusation, the ones whose actual lives do not fit the label assigned to them, are the people the church is most likely to lose if it stays comfortable with the categories it inherited.

The room can be larger than the labels. That decision is the weight this leaves with you.
