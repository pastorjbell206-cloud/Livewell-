One of the most formative relationships of my ministry has been with a pastor who is wrong about several things I consider important. I mean that warmly, and I suspect he would say the same about me. We disagree about baptism, about how the gifts of the Spirit function, about a few of the harder texts. We have not resolved it. We are not going to. And he has shaped me more than most of the men who agree with me ever have.

This will sound strange in a season that treats every disagreement as a threat and every difference as a line to be defended. We have built a Christian culture that sorts people quickly — our tribe and the other tribe, the sound and the suspect — and then keeps each tribe safely among its own. It feels like faithfulness. It is mostly fear, and it produces pastors who are confident, unchallenged, and slowly narrowing, surrounded only by the echo of their own conclusions.

The historical situation is worth naming here, because we did not arrive at this condition by accident. The fragmentation of Western Protestantism — by most estimates, tens of thousands of distinct denominations globally [cite — confirm] — is in part the accumulated scar tissue of every unresolved dispute about secondary matters treated as primary. The Reformation produced genuine and necessary fractures. But what followed the Reformation was not a settlement. It was a logic: when you disagree, separate. And that logic has run itself out to absurdity, producing communities so doctrinally tight that they can no longer hear a word from outside their own walls. The tribe becomes the whole horizon. The horizon shrinks to the size of the tribe.

I grew up in that world. My theological formation was thorough and narrow in equal measure. By the time I entered pastoral ministry, I had a complete account of the important questions and knew which voices were reliable and which were suspect. I had not been taught to examine my convictions — I had been taught to defend them. There is a difference that matters more than I understood at the time.

The pastor who changed that entered my life through a city-wide ministerial association — the kind of gathering I attended because I thought it was my duty and from which I expected to learn nothing useful. He was there from a tradition I had already categorized, which meant I had already decided what he believed and what I thought of it. He turned out to be a more serious reader of Scripture than I expected. He turned out to ask harder questions than I was accustomed to. He turned out to hold his own convictions with the kind of grip that comes from having examined them rather than merely inherited them.

Here is what the brother who disagrees with me did that the brothers who agree could not. He made me defend what I believe to someone who was not already persuaded — which is the only way you find out whether you hold a conviction or merely inherited a slogan. The doctrines I had only repeated, I now had to actually understand. Some of them got stronger under his pressure. A few of them, honestly, did not survive, and I am better for having lost them. You cannot get that from a room of people nodding. Agreement is comfortable and it is not formative; it leaves your weak places exactly as weak as it found them.

He also showed me the difference between the center and the edge. When you spend all your time among people who share your whole list, every item on the list starts to feel equally central — the cross and the calendar, the resurrection and the worship style, all defended with the same heat. A friendship across real difference forces the question the tribe never asks: *what here is actually essential, and what is just mine?* I came to hold the gospel more firmly and a dozen secondary things more loosely, and that is not a softening. It is the maturity Paul kept pleading for — unity in the essentials, freedom in the rest, charity in all of it.

The Pauline framework is worth pressing here. In Romans 14 and 15, Paul addresses a congregation divided over real matters — food sacrificed to idols, calendar observance, practices that carried genuine theological weight in their context. His instructions do not resolve the theological disputes. He tells the stronger and the weaker to receive one another, to welcome one another as Christ has welcomed them (Romans 15:7). The welcome comes before the agreement. The agreement may never come. The welcome stands regardless. This is not doctrinal indifference. Paul elsewhere guards the gospel with fierce precision. But his refusal to treat every secondary dispute as a fellowship-breaking matter reflects a pastoral wisdom that has largely left us.

James K.A. Smith has argued that what we believe is shaped as much by what we practice as by what we confess [cite — confirm]. The practice of theological isolation — staying only with the doctrinally similar — shapes a particular kind of believer: certain, defensive, unable to distinguish conviction from habit. The practice of genuine engagement across difference shapes a different kind: one who has had to ask, again and again, which hills to actually stand on, and why.

There is a counter-position worth hearing. The man who says that theological precision matters — that careless ecumenism has historically produced the diluted faith of the mainline denominations, that there are doctrines for which the church must hold the line — is not wrong. There are fellowship-breaking matters. The Reformation was not optional. Some disputes cannot be papered over without betraying the gospel itself. I know which disputes those are, and I do not treat them as secondary. The question is not whether to hold convictions. The question is whether every conviction receives the same grip.

The friend who disagrees with me and I have had that argument. We have drawn the lines in different places, and we have found, in the drawing, that we actually agree about where the center is. That discovery could not have happened at a distance. It required proximity, patience, the willingness to be in the same room for longer than comfort suggested.

Jesus prayed that we would be one so that the world would believe (John 17:21). He did not pray that we would be uniform. The unity he asked for is harder than uniformity and more credible than it, because uniformity only proves that similar people can get along, while unity across real difference proves that something larger is holding people who, by every natural measure, should have split. The watching world has seen plenty of Christians who agree with each other. It has rarely seen Christians who disagree and will not let go.

The watching world in our moment is not persuaded by our doctrinal consistency. It has watched the church fragment repeatedly over matters that turned out not to matter, and it has drawn the obvious conclusion: that we believe in our positions more than we believe in each other, more than we believe in the one who told us to be one. That is an indictment I cannot fully escape. I have been part of the fragmentation. I have drawn lines that kept people out. I have given doctrinal precision work that only love is supposed to do.

What changed was not that I decided doctrine was unimportant. What changed was that I spent enough time with a man who believed differently to understand that the Lord we share is larger than the differences between us — and that understanding that fact has made me more faithful, not less, to the things I actually hold with conviction.

So find the brother who is wrong, by your lights, about things that matter to you, and stay close enough to be changed. Let him press your convictions until you know which ones you actually hold. Let him show you where your certainty was tribe and not gospel. You will lose a few things you should lose, hold the central things more firmly, and gain something the agreeable rooms could never give you.

The man who only learns from people who already agree with him has not protected the truth. He has simply stopped finding out whether he has it.

There is a particular kind of pastoral poverty that comes from working in theological isolation — not just personally, but for the congregation. The people you lead take their cues about how to handle difference from how you handle it. The pastor who cannot sit across a table from a man who disagrees and remain in genuine relationship is teaching his congregation that disagreement ends relationships. In a world pulling itself apart over every conceivable division, that lesson is not just impoverishing. It is disqualifying.

The congregation that learns from its pastor how to hold both conviction and charity — how to disagree without severing — has been given something genuinely rare. They will know how to stay at tables where they are in the minority. They will know how to receive correction without losing their center. They will know the difference between a truth worth dying for and a preference worth holding loosely.

That is not a small formation. In the room I serve, on any given Sunday, there are people navigating marriages in which the two people believe differently, friendships in which theological distance has opened up over years, families in which the children have returned from university with questions the parents cannot answer. They need more than a pastor who can articulate the correct position. They need a pastor who can show them what it looks like to remain faithful while remaining in relationship with someone who sees it differently.

The brother I disagreed with taught me that. He remains the most useful theologian I have encountered, for reasons that had nothing to do with his conclusions. He made me think harder, hold tighter to the essentials, and carry the rest more loosely.

That is not a concession. It is growth. There is a difference, and it matters.
