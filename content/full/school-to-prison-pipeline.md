There is a system in this country that takes children who are poor, who are Black, who are Brown, who are disabled, who carry the weight of trauma in their bodies — and routes them, with bureaucratic efficiency, away from classrooms and toward cages. It does not announce itself as a pipeline. It presents as a disciplinary code. As zero tolerance. As resource officers and suspension policies and the quiet, cumulative mathematics of a child counted as a problem to be removed rather than a person to be formed.

The school-to-prison pipeline is not metaphor. It is mechanism. And the church that does not name it has made its peace with it.

---

To understand how we arrived here, you have to go back past the education reform debates of the 1990s, past the "superpredator" rhetoric that shaped the 1994 Crime Bill, all the way to a foundational decision in how this country imagined its poor. The history of compulsory public education in America was always entangled with social control. The earliest reformers — including Horace Mann in Massachusetts in the 1830s — envisioned public schools partly as institutions that would produce orderly, compliant workers and citizens. That vision was not neutral. It carried within it a question about whose children deserved formation and whose deserved management.

After the Civil War, the question became explicit. The schools built for formerly enslaved people in the Reconstruction South were systematically underfunded, then destroyed, then reconstructed as institutions designed to produce agricultural and domestic labor rather than educated citizens. W.E.B. Du Bois documented this in *The Souls of Black Folk* [cite — confirm] — the deliberate design of Black education to produce a permanent subordinate class, not participants in democracy. That architecture did not disappear when Jim Crow fell. It adapted.

The pipeline in its current recognizable form took shape in the 1990s. After a series of school shootings, the federal Gun-Free Schools Act of 1994 required schools to expel students who brought weapons to school. The logic was defensible. What happened next was not. Zero-tolerance policies spread far beyond weapons, and school resource officers — armed police stationed inside schools — became standard in districts serving poor and minority students. By the early 2000s, students were being arrested for behavior that previous generations handled in the principal's office: talking back, dress code violations, throwing food. The school had acquired the machinery of the criminal justice system and placed it in the hands of people trained to use it.

The data that emerged was devastating. Studies found [cite — confirm] that Black students are suspended and expelled at rates three to four times higher than white students for the same behaviors. Students with disabilities are disciplined at twice the rate of students without them. A student who is suspended is three times more likely to have contact with the criminal justice system within the following year than a student who is not. Every suspension is, statistically, a step toward a cell. The system was not broken. It was producing exactly what the incentive structures inside it demanded.

---

The prophet Amos understood this kind of architecture. He was not speaking to individual moral failures when he condemned the merchants who "trample the heads of the poor into the dust of the earth" (Amos 2:7). He was describing a system — interlocking economic and legal structures that took the most vulnerable members of society and ground them into poverty while the comfortable called it order. *They sell the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals* (Amos 2:6). The market in Amos's time turned human beings into inventory. The pipeline in ours turns human beings into criminal statistics.

The imago Dei — the insistence in Genesis 1 that every human being bears the image of God — is not an abstraction. It is a claim about that child in the third grade who cannot sit still because no one at home is safe, and who will be suspended for disruption, and who will eventually be counted as a number in a recidivism report. He bears the image of God. The system that removes him from the classroom rather than asking what is wrong in his life is treating that image as inconvenient.

Jesus was explicit about whose side the kingdom stands on in relation to children. *Whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin, it would be better for him to have a great millstone fastened around his neck and to be drowned in the depth of the sea* (Matthew 18:6). The word translated "sin" here — *skandalizo* — means to cause to stumble, to place an obstacle in the path. A system that removes a child from education at the age of nine, twelve, fourteen — that removes him from the room where his future might have been different — is placing a millstone around his neck and calling it policy.

---

The counter-position deserves honest engagement, because it comes from a real place. Advocates of strict discipline in schools argue that disruptive students harm the learning of the majority, that teachers in under-resourced schools need the ability to restore order, that the real problem is the home environment rather than the disciplinary system, and that holding schools responsible for what parents have failed to do is unjust to educators who are themselves underserved. These arguments are not wrong about everything. A classroom in which one student's persistent disruption destroys the learning of thirty others is a genuine injustice. No one benefits from the romanticism that refuses to see it.

But the argument collapses when it confronts the data on racial and disability disparities. If the pipeline were simply about removing genuinely dangerous students from classrooms, the rates would track behavior rather than race. They do not. The disparities are not explained by higher rates of actual dangerous behavior — they are explained by the differential application of subjective judgments to bodies that have been conditioned, in the imaginations of the institutions, to be read as threatening. The child who is poor and Black and carrying trauma is not more dangerous than his white suburban counterpart. He is read differently. And the reading has legal and physical consequences that follow him for decades.

The solution, therefore, is not softer discipline. It is different discipline — restorative rather than punitive, rooted in the understanding that behavior is almost always communication, that a child who is acting out in a classroom is telling you something about his life that the suspension does not answer, and that removing him from school simply removes him from the only institution that might have interrupted the trajectory his circumstances have put him on.

---

What does the church owe this conversation?

The church that sits in a city where children are being criminalized for being poor and young has a prophetic responsibility it cannot satisfy with food drives alone. Charity addresses the symptom — the child who is hungry, the family that is homeless. Justice addresses the system that is creating the symptoms. And the church that only does charity while the pipeline runs is choosing the comfortable form of faithfulness over the costly one.

Practically, this means a few things. It means that pastors and congregations need to understand the pipeline well enough to name it — to talk about it from the pulpit with the same moral seriousness they bring to the sins they find less politically complicated. It means advocating for restorative justice programs in local schools, which the research consistently shows [cite — confirm] reduce suspension rates and improve learning outcomes for all students, not just those disciplined. It means supporting the organizations in your city — and if none exist, starting them — that provide legal representation and advocacy for students facing long-term suspension or expulsion, because those are criminal-justice-adjacent proceedings and the families least equipped to handle them are the ones most in need.

It means, closer to home, asking whether your church has relationships with the schools in its neighborhood. Not just a back-to-school drive, but the kind of sustained, relational presence that makes you a known quantity to the principal, a trusted resource when a family is in crisis, and a voice in the community that has standing because it has shown up. The church that parachutes in at Christmas and disappears in February has not earned the right to speak prophetically about what the school district is doing to the children in that neighborhood.

And it means the harder reckoning. Many congregations sit in suburbs that were created, in part, by the white flight that stripped resources from urban schools in the first place. The neighborhoods where the pipeline runs hardest are not geographically or historically unrelated to the neighborhoods where the comfortable churches sit. The tax base that funds the suburban school and underfunds the urban one is a consequence of redlining, restrictive covenants, blockbusting, and the thousand other mechanisms by which wealth was sorted by race across the twentieth century. The church that benefits from that sorting and remains silent about its consequences is not neutral. It is complicit.

Walter Brueggemann wrote that the prophetic tradition is engaged in an act of counter-imagination — insisting that the world as it is is not the world as God intends it to be, and that the people of God are summoned to bear witness to the difference [cite — confirm]. The pipeline is not inevitable. It is a policy choice. It was built by human decisions and it can be dismantled by human decisions. The church that carries the prophetic tradition carries the responsibility to say so — loudly, specifically, at whatever cost that specificity requires.

The child in that third-grade classroom, the one who cannot sit still because no one at home is safe — he is not a disruption to be managed. He is the person the kingdom is organized around. *Let the little children come to me, and do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of heaven* (Matthew 19:14).

The pipeline is how we hinder them.

That is not an abstraction. It is a verdict.
