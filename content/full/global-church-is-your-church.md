The global church is not somewhere else. We tend to picture it that way — believers in distant countries, with unfamiliar languages and harder lives, the subject of a missions slideshow and a line in our giving. Real, but remote. Theirs, not ours. That picture is a quiet heresy, and it impoverishes us more than it impoverishes them.

---

The New Testament will not allow the distance. Paul tells the Corinthians that they are members of a single body, and that "if one member suffers, all suffer together; if one member is honored, all rejoice together" (1 Corinthians 12:26). He is not speaking only of the people in one building in one city in the ancient world. He is speaking of the body of Christ, which has no nationality, which spans every tribe and tongue and language, which is one organism with one Head. The believer worshipping in a house church in a hostile country is not a stranger you support from a distance. He is your brother, closer kin in the only family that finally matters than your neighbors who do not share the faith.

This reframes everything.

The persecuted church is not a charity. It is your family under fire. Hebrews tells you to "remember those who are in prison, as though in prison with them, and those who are mistreated, since you also are in the body" (Hebrews 13:3). As though with them. Not as distant beneficiaries of your concern, but as your own people, whose chains you are meant to feel in your own wrists. The command is not to send resources to people in difficult circumstances. It is to locate yourself inside their experience, because the body that is being imprisoned is the body you inhabit.

---

The history of how Western Christianity has understood the global church is worth honest examination, because the errors are still producing consequences.

The nineteenth-century missionary movement — for all its genuine faith and genuine cost — operated largely within a framework that placed the Western church at the center: as the sender, the teacher, the mature tradition, and the recipient communities as the mission field, the immature, the ones who needed what the West possessed. The framework was not entirely wrong. There was real gospel. There was real sacrifice. William Carey, Hudson Taylor, David Livingstone — these were not frauds, and dismissing the missionary movement as merely colonialism fails the actual complexity.

But the frame was distorted, and the distortion produced consequences that the global church is still living with. Indigenous leadership was often undermined rather than developed. Western theology was often delivered as the universal form of Christianity rather than as one culturally situated expression of it. The ecclesial traditions and spiritual gifts of the receiving communities — traditions that in some cases were richer and more ancient than anything the Western missionaries carried — were treated as raw material to be evangelized rather than as the spiritual inheritance of communities who already knew something about God.

Lamin Sanneh, the Gambian-born scholar of world Christianity who taught at Yale, argued that the translation of the gospel into local languages carried within it the seeds of the frame's own correction: when you put Scripture into someone's mother tongue, you have admitted that their language is adequate to carry the revelation, and once you have admitted that, the claim of Western cultural superiority becomes theologically untenable. ---

The numbers tell a story that the Western church has been slow to absorb.

The center of global Christianity is no longer the North Atlantic. It is sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and parts of Asia. Philip Jenkins, in his landmark work on the future of global Christianity, documented the massive shift in the demographic center of the church that occurred through the twentieth century. [cite — confirm] The church is growing fastest, and often suffering most, in the very places the missions movement once treated as the recipients. The Christianity being practiced in the Global South is frequently more theologically conservative, more pneumatically alive, and more directly engaged with the suffering of poverty than what most Western congregations experience on a Sunday morning. The vitality is not here. It is there.

This does not mean Western Christianity is without contribution. It means the frame has reversed. We imagined ourselves the teachers. We are increasingly the ones who need to sit down and learn.

I have carried the provincial picture. I have prayed for the global church the way you pray for a cause rather than a family — briefly, at a distance, without really believing that their joys were mine to share and their suffering mine to feel. The repentance required is not primarily organizational. It is imaginative. It is the willingness to let the theological reality become emotionally actual: that I am bound, by something thicker than blood, to people I will not meet until glory, and that their faithfulness is part of my inheritance and their suffering part of my own body's pain.

---

The theology Paul is working with in 1 Corinthians 12 is not metaphor. It is ontology. He is not saying the church is *like* a body. He is saying the church *is* the body of Christ — a real, organic unity in which the Spirit distributes gifts to each member for the good of the whole, in which the suffering of one part constitutes suffering of the whole organism, in which the provincial protection of any one part's interests at the expense of the whole is a self-wounding.

That means the Western church's insularity is not only a moral failure. It is a form of self-harm. The gifts that the global church carries — its theologies of suffering, its practices of communal prayer and fasting, its clarity about the cost of discipleship, its experience of the Spirit's movement in conditions of material poverty — these are gifts to the body. When we treat the global church as the recipient of our resources while remaining closed to what it has to offer, we impoverish ourselves. We amputate a portion of our own inheritance.

---

What does it change, practically?

It changes how you pray. Not the blanket intercession for "the church around the world," but specific, named, located prayer — for the congregation in northern Nigeria navigating the violence between farming and herding communities, for the house church in a Central Asian city where gathering involves real risk, for the pastor in Haiti rebuilding for the fourth time. Prayer that has geography and names and the specificity you would use for family.

It changes how you give — not as a patron funding a project, but as one part of a body sharing resources with another part, because the organism does not thrive when its resources are permanently concentrated in one location.

It changes how you hold your own comfort. The member of your body who considers a week of genuine risk to be an ordinary week — who leads a church where attendance itself is an act of courage — will recalibrate what counts as hard in your own ministry. Not with guilt. With proportion.

And it changes how you understand the church itself. Not as your congregation with some international affiliates. Not as the Western tradition with a mission arm. But as a single, global, blood-bought people, spread across every language and nation, of whom your church is one small, beloved, accountable part — accountable to the whole body for how it uses its resources, for whether it is praying with the suffering members, for whether it has let the distance become an excuse to live as though the body that suffers on another continent is not its own.

Revelation 7:9 describes the end: "a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb." That gathering is what the church already is, in Christ, right now. The gathering on Sunday morning in one building is not the church. It is the church assembling as itself in one place — one expression of a body that extends to every corner of the globe and every generation of the faithful dead.

The New Testament writers held this global scope as a non-negotiable feature of Christian identity. Paul's collection for the Jerusalem church — the project he described in Romans 15, 1 Corinthians 16, and 2 Corinthians 8-9 — was not a charitable program. It was a theological statement about the unity of the body. Gentile churches sending money to Jewish churches across the Mediterranean was Paul's evidence that the dividing wall had actually fallen, that the new humanity he described in Ephesians 2 was not a theological claim but a lived reality. The collection was the body behaving like a body.

What would it look like for a congregation to take that seriously today? Not as an annual missions offering, not as a line in the budget — but as a structural commitment to the truth that what happens to the church in one part of the world is happening to us? That the body in chains is our body? That the body rebuilding after the third earthquake is our family, not a worthy cause?

The rest of your church is already assembled. In prisons you are not in. In cemeteries you have not visited. In services conducted in languages you do not speak. In sanctuaries that do not have the heat on because the congregation cannot afford it, where the worship is more fully alive than anything you experienced last Sunday.

They are your family. They have been praying for you. The question is whether you will start praying as though you believed it was mutual.
