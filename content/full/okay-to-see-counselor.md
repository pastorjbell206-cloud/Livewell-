He will send you to a counselor. He knows the referral by heart — the gentle transition from pastoral conversation to clinical recommendation, the assurance that there is no shame in it, that professional help is wisdom not weakness, that the body of Christ uses every resource available to it. He has said these things to hundreds of people over the years, in dozens of different pastoral offices, in hospital rooms and kitchen tables and parking lots after Sunday services. He believes them.

He has not seen a counselor himself.

**The Shame Underneath**

The resistance most pastors carry toward professional counseling is not primarily theological, though theology is sometimes used to rationalize it. It is shame — the specific, deep shame of being a man trained to bear others' burdens who cannot admit that his own burden has become too heavy for his private resources. The pastor understands suffering. He walks into it, names it, prays over it, accompanies it across years. He is the one who helps people carry what they cannot carry alone. To sit in a counselor's chair feels like stepping out of role — like admitting a failure that will damage the authority he needs to lead, the credibility he has built over years of being the person who helps.

I have felt that shame. I have resisted help I needed because receiving it would have required acknowledging a need my self-understanding as a pastor had no room for. I did not say it that way at the time. I said I didn't have time. I said the right person wasn't available. I said I was working through it on my own. These were rationalizations dressed in practical language. Underneath them was the older thing: the conviction, never fully spoken, that needing help was a kind of pastoral failure.

The formation most male pastors received reinforced that conviction without ever stating it directly. Evangelical pastoral culture — particularly in its American, broadly Reformed, institutionally conservative expressions — has absorbed a set of assumptions about leadership that include something very close to stoic self-sufficiency. The leader who needs no help. The man whose faith is adequate to whatever the call requires. The pastor who can carry the congregation's grief through the week and preach confidently on Sunday and present an untroubled exterior through the entire sequence. This is not the biblical portrait of pastoral leadership. It is a mythology with significant cultural roots and significant pastoral costs.

**The Theological Confusion**

The version of this that gets dressed in theological language goes roughly like this: if I truly believed, I would not need this. Prayer should be sufficient. The Spirit is the Counselor (John 14:16), and to rely on a human counselor is to express some deficiency in my dependence on God. Professional mental health care is for people who have not learned to take their needs to the Lord.

This is a misreading of Scripture serious enough to be dangerous. The Holy Spirit is the Paraclete — the one who comes alongside, who advocates, who sustains. But the God who created the human mind with its complex architecture of emotion, memory, embodied experience, and relational formation did not design it to resolve its disorders through spiritual discipline alone. The same theological tradition that believes the Spirit heals also — consistently, across centuries — believes the Spirit works through created means. Through surgeons and medications and teachers and friends. The healing is from God; the means are creaturely.

The church has never argued otherwise when it comes to physical illness. A pastor with a broken leg does not refuse orthopedic care on the grounds that prayer should be sufficient. A pastor with Type 2 diabetes does not decline medication because the Holy Spirit is the Great Physician. The distinction the evangelical tradition makes for mental and emotional wounds — the insistence that spiritual intervention should be the primary or exclusive response to psychological suffering — is not theological. It is cultural. It reflects an inherited suspicion of psychology rooted in the culture wars of the late twentieth century, not a considered theological anthropology.

**The Biblical Pattern of Sought Help**

The biblical portrait of leadership is not the portrait of the self-sufficient man. It is consistently the portrait of the man who asks for help, who acknowledges limits, who receives support from others when the weight exceeds his own capacity.

Moses had Jethro. After the exodus, Moses was judging disputes from morning to evening — carrying the entire administrative and judicial weight of the community himself. His father-in-law looked at what Moses was doing and said plainly: "What you are doing is not good. You and these people who come to you will only wear yourselves out. The work is too heavy for you; you cannot handle it alone" (Exodus 18:17-18). Jethro was not criticizing Moses's faith. He was identifying a structural problem and offering a structural solution. Moses was wise enough to receive it.

Elijah, after the confrontation at Carmel, collapsed under a juniper tree and asked God to let him die. He was exhausted, isolated, and in despair — conditions that would meet any clinical definition of a mental health crisis. God's response was not a rebuke for insufficient faith. God sent an angel with food and water and told him to sleep. Twice. The divine response to Elijah's crisis was physical care, rest, and the provision of a companion for the next leg of the journey (1 Kings 19:4-21). God understood that the prophet's interior state required something beyond spiritual exhortation.

Paul wrote with explicit gratitude about the emotional support he received from communities. "God, who comforts the downcast, comforted us by the coming of Titus" (2 Corinthians 7:6). He was not embarrassed to name that he was downcast, or that human presence was the means of his comfort. The isolated leader who needs no one is not a New Testament figure. He is a modern mythology with no serious biblical support.

**What Counseling Actually Is**

Counseling, done well, is not a substitute for prayer, community, or Scripture. It is something else entirely: a structured and skilled engagement with the interior life of a person who has been formed, wounded, and confused by experiences that do not resolve themselves through good intentions or spiritual effort alone.

The pastor who has spent twenty years carrying congregational grief — who has absorbed trauma through proximity, who has maintained a public persona under relentless scrutiny, who has managed the emotional demands of a helping vocation on top of his own personal history — that man has specific interior work to do that requires a specific kind of help. Not because his faith is deficient, but because the work has shaped him in ways that he cannot fully observe from inside, and the therapeutic relationship provides a context outside the pastoral role in which someone with appropriate training can help him see himself clearly.

The process matters here. Trauma — including the secondary trauma of sustained pastoral exposure to others' suffering — creates patterns of response that operate below conscious awareness. The person who has absorbed years of grief and crisis learns to manage his reactions in ways that may have been adaptive in the short term and become problematic over longer periods. Anxiety, perfectionism, the compulsive overwork that looks like dedication, the emotional unavailability at home that looks like introversion, the crisis of meaning that emerges after fifteen years of preaching to people whose lives do not seem to be changing much — these are not failures of faith. They are human responses to the particular demands of a particular vocation, and they respond to skilled, sustained pastoral care.

That care includes good therapy.

**The Practical Objection**

The most honest version of pastoral resistance to counseling is not theological but practical: the concern about confidentiality. If the pastor in a small-to-medium-sized city sees a counselor and that counselor is somehow connected to the congregation — a member, a referral source, someone whose professional circles overlap with the church's pastoral network — the privacy of the session is genuinely compromised. The concern is real.

It is solvable. The PCN network exists partly for this reason: connecting pastors to resources in contexts outside their immediate community. Telehealth has made geographic distance no longer prohibitive. There are counselors who specialize in working with clergy and who understand the specific pressures of pastoral life, who are deliberately positioned outside congregational networks, who are equipped to maintain the confidentiality the situation requires.

The pastor who says "I can't find someone to talk to" is often the pastor who has not seriously looked. The looking requires admitting the need is real, which is the place where the shame lives.

**The Weight of Refusing**

The pastor who refuses to receive care for his own interior life will eventually pay a price, and so will the people he serves. The burnout that emerges from unaddressed accumulation of pastoral stress is not a personal matter. It is a congregational one. The man who runs the church out of emotional reserves he has not replenished will make increasingly poor decisions, will have diminishing capacity for genuine pastoral care, will drift toward the performance of ministry rather than its substance — and will often not recognize any of this until the crisis is already visible.

The congregation deserves a pastor who is alive inside the work he does — not managed and performing, but genuinely present. That presence requires interior health, and interior health requires the same care the pastor recommends to everyone else.

See the counselor. Not as an alternative to your faith. As an expression of it. As the application to your own life of the theological conviction you preach: that God provides means of grace, that receiving help is not weakness, that the community of Christ exists for the restoration of every person in it — including the one standing at the front.

The man who sends everyone else and goes himself nowhere is not practicing what he preaches.
