There is a confession I have to make before I make an argument.

For the better part of a decade in ministry, I preached sin as if it were always and only a personal transaction. A man lusts. A woman deceives. A person steals. The solution: conviction, repentance, grace, change. I was not wrong about any of that. I was incomplete. I was reading Amos as if Amos were simply a better version of a recovery group facilitator, addressing individual hearts when he was actually indicting structures — the grain merchants who shorted their measures, the judges who took bribes, the wealthy who built houses of hewn stone on the backs of the poor. Individual people were doing it. The system was designed to let them.

The church's current allergy to the language of systemic sin is not a theological conviction. It is a cultural reflex. In the decades since the civil rights movement was reframed as a political rather than a prophetic event, many white evangelicals came to treat any reference to systems or structures as a code word for a particular political program. That conflation is understandable as a historical accident. It is inexcusable as a permanent hermeneutic.

Scripture knows no such restriction.

---

Amos 5 does not only condemn the man who takes a bribe. It condemns the gate — the system of adjudication where bribery operated as standard procedure: "You hate the one who reproves in the gate, and you abhor the one who speaks the truth" (Amos 5:10). The gate was not a metaphor for the heart. It was the physical location where legal disputes were adjudicated in ancient Israel — the institutional structure through which justice was either delivered or perverted. When Amos condemned what happened at the gate, he was condemning an arrangement, not only the individuals who participated in it.

Isaiah 10 does not merely scold corrupt judges individually; it announces woe upon those who "make iniquitous decrees" — legislation, law, policy — "to turn aside the needy from justice and to rob the poor of my people of their right" (Isaiah 10:1–2). The prophetic tradition is not a collection of sermons about individual character improvement. It is a sustained indictment of the arrangement of power itself.

Paul understood this. His language about "principalities and powers" in Ephesians 6 has been domesticated into a vocabulary about personal spiritual warfare, but Walter Wink's work [cite — confirm] restores the social dimension: these powers are the spiritual reality behind institutional arrangements, the invisible architecture of systems that dehumanize and destroy. To fight them requires more than changed hearts, though changed hearts are essential. It requires confronting the structures that make certain sins profitable, certain injustices invisible, certain people disposable.

---

What does it mean for sin to calcify into a system?

It begins with individual wickedness. A merchant discovers that cheating the measure is profitable and unlikely to be detected. He does it once, then as a matter of course. He tells a colleague. The colleague also begins cheating the measure. The practice spreads across a market. Eventually, honest merchants are disadvantaged — the cheating merchants undercut them, the buyers prefer the lower prices, and the honest merchant must either cheat or fail. What began as individual wickedness has become structural: a market arranged so that cheating is rational and honesty is penalized.

This is not a hypothetical. Amos was describing something like this in eighth-century Israel. The grain merchants who shorted their measures and sold the chaff as wheat (Amos 8:5–6) were not only sinning individually. They were operating within and reinforcing a market structure that favored exploitation. The system and the individual sinner were in a relationship — each enabling the other, each making the other worse.

That relationship is what the prophetic tradition insists on naming. To address only the individual is to leave the system intact to produce the same sins in other individuals. To address only the system while ignoring individual repentance is to pretend that structural arrangements change without people changing. The prophets did neither. They named both — the individual and the arrangement, the sinner and the system — because they understood that sin operates at both levels simultaneously.

---

The standard objection deserves honest engagement. Systems do not sin, people do. No institution repents; only persons can turn. This is true.

But consider what follows from it. Individual repentance that produces no structural change is incomplete repentance. The slaveholder who has a genuine conversion experience but keeps his enslaved people in chains has not finished repenting. The banker who privately regrets predatory lending practices but continues them has not repented of them. The question "What must I do?" is always also the question "What must I stop participating in?" — and sometimes, "What must I work to dismantle?"

The story of Zacchaeus makes this concrete. His encounter with Jesus in Luke 19 produced not only interior transformation but a structural response: "Half of my possessions, Lord, I will give to the poor; and if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I will pay back four times as much" (Luke 19:8). The fourfold restitution is not charity. It is structural repair — the recognition that genuine turning means addressing the material consequences of the wrongs committed. Jesus called it salvation. The word he used was *sōtēria* — wholeness, restoration, the comprehensive putting-right of what was broken. Not the beginning of a spiritual feeling. Salvation.

---

I have done what I am warning against. I have preached against the sin of materialism to congregants who worked in industries structured to produce and reward exactly that materialism, and I have called that prophetic. It was not. It was the comfortable half of the biblical witness, stripped of its most expensive demands.

The reason the comfortable half gets preached is not theological confusion. It is institutional self-preservation. Addressing individual sin rarely costs the preacher anything permanent. The congregation may feel convicted, but they will be back next week, more grateful than resentful, because they have been addressed at the level of their private lives. Addressing systemic sin costs the preacher the approval of the people who benefit from the system. In a congregation that includes employers who pay poverty wages, investors in industries structured around exploitation, homeowners who benefit from housing arrangements that exclude the poor — naming the structures is not a spiritual exercise. It is an act of prophetic risk.

Amos knew this. He was told by the priest of Bethel to go back to Judah, to earn his bread there, to stop prophesying at the king's sanctuary (Amos 7:12–13). The message from the institution was clear: your prophecy is not welcome here. What Amos was doing was not personal spiritual formation for the congregation at Bethel. It was an indictment of the arrangement that made the sanctuary profitable and the poor expendable. The sanctuary did not want to hear it.

Neither does ours.

---

What the prophetic tradition requires of the contemporary church is not primarily a policy agenda. It is a refusal to accept the artificially narrowed definition of sin that serves institutional comfort.

It requires saying: the individual is accountable, and the arrangement the individual inhabits is also accountable. It requires saying: what God requires of the heart, God also requires of the law, the market, the institution. It requires saying: the congregation that worships enthusiastically while benefiting from arrangements that grind the poor into the dust is not far from what Amos was addressing at Bethel.

It requires the willingness to preach from the whole canon — not only from the portions of Scripture that address the private interior, but from the portions that address the public arrangement. Not only Romans 7, but Amos 5. Not only Ephesians 5, but Isaiah 10. Not only the Psalms of individual lament, but the Psalms of communal reckoning, where the covenant people are called to account for what their common life has become.

The Hebrew prophets did not separate theology from sociology. They did not have the category distinction that allows us to treat economic arrangements as outside the scope of the gospel. They addressed king and merchant and priest and prophet as participants in a single moral order, accountable together for the shape of the world they had built.

---

Individual sin is real. The broken will is real. Personal accountability is real, and I will not surrender it.

But so is the grain merchant's scale. So is the unjust gate. So is every system built on the theological error that some people matter more than others — every arrangement that converts human beings into inputs, that calculates acceptable casualties, that produces its beneficiaries by producing its victims.

The church that addresses one without the other has chosen the easier half of the prophetic tradition and called it faithfulness.

That is its own kind of sin. And it is, notably, the kind of sin that benefits those who commit it.

The prophets did not miss that detail. Neither should we.
