Why Every Generation Gets the Bible Wrong, Why Yours Is No Different, and What to Do About It.

Every generation believes it has finally gotten the Bible right, while all previous generations got it wrong. This is not a cynical observation. It is the record. Every major interpretive failure in the church's history was conducted by people who believed, with complete sincerity, that they were reading Scripture correctly — that all the previous interpreters had gotten it wrong, and they had gotten it right.

The medieval theologians who wrote elaborate theological justifications for the Crusades believed they had the Bible on their side. The eighteenth and nineteenth-century divines who produced detailed scriptural defenses of chattel slavery believed they had the Bible on their side. The congregations that maintained racial segregation through the 1950s and into the 1960s believed they had the Bible on their side. They were wrong. They were not lying. That is the harder fact.

## The Error Is Not Intelligence

This is not a comfortable history to sit with, and most of us do not sit with it long enough to feel its weight. We note it, nod at it, and then proceed to make exactly the same error we are acknowledging — the error of assuming that we, unlike all the previous generations, have finally gotten the interpretive problem under control.

The problem is not fundamentally about intelligence. The generations I have named produced brilliant people — scholars with real linguistic competence, serious devotional commitments, and genuine faith. What they lacked was not brainpower. What they lacked was the ability to see how completely their cultural location was shaping what felt obvious when they opened the text.

This is the structural problem. Every interpreter reads Scripture from somewhere. From a body, a century, a class, a nation, a set of economic arrangements, a family of origin that has shaped what seems obvious and what seems impossible. These are not obstacles to interpretation that can be removed by trying harder or praying more sincerely. They are the conditions of all interpretation. The question is not whether you read from somewhere. You do. The question is whether you know where you are reading from and what that location is likely to make you miss.

The philosopher Hans-Georg Gadamer argued that all understanding involves a "fusion of horizons" — the horizon of the text meeting the horizon of the reader, neither one simply overriding the other [cite — confirm; Gadamer, *Truth and Method*, 1960]. What this means for Bible readers is that our cultural moment is always in the room when we read, whether or not we invited it. The interpreters who believed that slavery was compatible with Scripture were not reading without presuppositions. They were reading with the presuppositions of a slave-holding economy, and those presuppositions had become so natural to them that they could not distinguish them from the text itself.

## The Candidates in My Own Reading

I have done this. I came to faith from atheism and brought with me a mode of argument, a confidence in the clarity of propositions, an impatience with ambiguity — all of which shaped how I read the text long after I stopped being an atheist. I have preached from those assumptions as if they were simply the Bible, and the gap between the assumptions and the text is wider than I would have admitted at the time. The log I needed to remove from my own eye was not the obvious one.

There are candidates in every contemporary reading that can be named, not as accusations aimed at individuals but as descriptions of the water that everyone swimming in this cultural moment has absorbed.

The individualism of modern Western culture shapes how we read texts that were written to and for communities. The New Testament epistles are addressed to *congregations*, the second-person plurals are communal, the "you" that receives the promise or the command is almost always a collective "you" — and yet the English-speaking church has read most of those texts as personal instructions for the individual believer's interior life, because individualism is the air we breathe. It is not the air in which Paul wrote.

Our therapeutic assumptions about the self shape how we read sin. Sin in the therapeutic culture is primarily a dysfunction or a woundedness — something that happened to us that we need to heal from. Sin in the biblical text is primarily a rebellion — a turning of the creature away from the Creator, a failure of worship, a preference of the self over the good of God and neighbor. The therapeutic reading is not without insight, but it is a reading shaped by a cultural moment that Paul and Isaiah and the author of Hebrews did not inhabit.

Our consumer instincts shape how we read discipleship. The consumer finds a church that meets his needs, with programs that fit his schedule, with a worship style that suits his preferences. The biblical text describes a disciple who takes up a cross (Matthew 16:24) — who does not find religion convenient but finds it costly, and chooses it anyway. The consumer reading and the cross-bearing reading are not compatible, and the consumer reading has the backing of the entire economy in which the church now operates.

Our particular national relationship to wealth and power shapes how we read the prophets and the Sermon on the Mount. The most powerful nation in the world's history, the one that has operated from the position of economic dominance for generations, will read the prophets' indictments of the powerful and the Sermon on the Mount's reversals of status — "blessed are the poor" — with different eyes than the nation that has always been on the receiving end of those structures. The text has not changed. The eyes have.

## The Answer Is Not Paralysis

The question this forces on every serious Christian is not comfortable: what are the equivalent presuppositions in my own reading, right now, that I cannot yet see because they are too close to my eyes?

That question could produce paralysis — the sense that since all readings are conditioned, no reading can be trusted, and the text itself has nothing stable to say. That conclusion is wrong, and it is also convenient, because it relieves the reader of the responsibility to change. The church's interpretive tradition, tested across centuries and cultures and geographies, has produced a center that holds: the canonical witness to the death and resurrection of Jesus, the love of God and neighbor, the dignity of all human beings made in the image of God, the priority of the vulnerable and the coming kingdom of justice. These are not seriously contested at the center. What is contested is almost always at the edges, and the edges are where our cultural location exerts its strongest pressure.

The answer to the problem of conditioned reading is not to abandon reading. It is to read in a way that is designed to encounter the conditioning.

Read Scripture with people who are different from you. Not different in the sense of spiritually mature people who share your class and culture and century — different in the sense of people whose cultural location will make them read different things, who will ask questions about the text that your location would not think to ask. The church's catholicity — its existence across centuries, cultures, nations, and classes — is not merely a fact about its sociology. It is a gift for the reading of Scripture. The text looks different from Lagos than from suburban Michigan. It looked different to Origen in Alexandria than it will look to a pastor in Fenton. The differences are data. Use them.

Take the interpretive tradition seriously, including the parts that make you uncomfortable. The consensus of the church across centuries is not infallible, but it is not nothing. When your reading of a text produces a conclusion that no serious interpreter in two thousand years of church history has reached, the probability that you are the first one to get it right is low. The tradition is a check on the idiosyncrasies of the present moment.

Sit under the text rather than above it. A text that is merely confirming what you already believed is not being read. It is being used. The reading that matters is the reading in which the text surprises you, interrupts you, refuses the conclusion you arrived with, measures you by a standard you did not set. That kind of reading requires a posture — a willingness to be corrected — that does not come naturally and must be chosen.

## The Monster Is Never Someone Else

The interpretive failures of previous generations are not evidence of their unusual moral weakness. They are evidence of a condition I share. The people who defended slavery from the text were not monsters by disposition. They were people formed by a world in which slavery was normal, reading a text through the lens of that normalcy, and arriving at conclusions that their world confirmed and their faith failed to correct.

My world has its own normalities. I cannot see them as clearly as I will eventually — as clearly as future generations will see them when they look back. The question is not whether my reading has blind spots. It has them. The question is whether I have enough people around me who can see what I cannot, and whether I am humble enough to hear them.

The monster in the mirror is never someone else. It is the condition of any reader who has not yet been surprised by the text they think they know. I have looked long enough to know that the log in my eye is not the one I most readily identified.

That humility does not come naturally. It has to be chosen. Repeatedly. And the choice is what distinguishes the reader who is being formed by Scripture from the reader who is merely using it.
