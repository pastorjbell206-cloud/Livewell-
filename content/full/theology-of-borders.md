Borders are very old human technology. The first thing Cain does after killing Abel is build a city — a bounded space, defined by walls, organized around his survival rather than around the image of God in his brother (Genesis 4:17). The biblical narrative does not look back on that founding moment with uncritical approval. From the beginning, the technology of the boundary is entangled with the problem of the person excluded.

This does not mean borders are inherently illegitimate. The Old Testament envisions a world of nations — the table of nations in Genesis 10, the division of peoples, the various territorial arrangements that God ordains through history. The nation of Israel has borders. The texts are realistic about political geography in ways that make any simple "borders are sin" argument misread the material.

But the tradition also insists, from within that same political realism, that the God of Israel is not bound by any border, that the justice God requires does not stop at any political line, and that the treatment of the person who stands at or crosses the border is one of the most consistent tests of a people's faithfulness. The theological question is not whether borders exist. The theological question is what they mean and what they are permitted to do to the human beings they divide.

---

The Hebrew Bible uses three words for the outsider: ger (the resident alien or sojourner), nokhri (the foreign national), and zar (the stranger who may pose a threat). The distinctions matter. The ger — the person who has left their homeland and is living among Israel — is the subject of the most intensive legal protection in the Mosaic code. The command to care for the ger appears more than thirty-five times in the Torah, more frequently than almost any other single ethical command.

"You shall not wrong a sojourner or oppress him, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt" (Exodus 22:21). The logic is not appeal to abstract principle. It is appeal to memory — the memory of what it felt like to be on the other side of the border, vulnerable, without status, without rights, dependent on the decency of those who had the power to help or harm. Israel's own experience of displacement is supposed to produce a specific kind of moral imagination: the capacity to see the current stranger through the lens of your own remembered strangeness.

This is not merely sentiment. It is built into the legal code. The gleaning laws of Leviticus 19 require the landowner to leave portions of the harvest specifically for the poor and the sojourner (Leviticus 19:9-10). The Sabbath rest commanded in Deuteronomy explicitly includes the sojourner within your gates (Deuteronomy 5:14). Leviticus 19:34 is unambiguous: "You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt." The love command is not confined to the insider community. It is extended across the border to the one who has crossed it.

---

The New Testament deepens this in ways that make any nationalism that claims Christian support theologically difficult to sustain.

The parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37) is, on one level, exactly about borders — ethnic, religious, cultural — and the capacity of those borders to determine who counts as a neighbor. The lawyer's question is a border question: "Who is my neighbor?" — meaning, where does my obligation stop? Jesus's answer, in the form of a story, does the precise opposite of drawing the border: it places the compassionate act in the most ethnically charged figure available to his audience, and makes the measure of "neighbor" not ethnic proximity but the willingness to cross the distance to help.

The Pentecost narrative in Acts 2 is deliberately constructed to reverse the Babel narrative of Genesis 11. At Babel, the diversity of languages produces dispersion and division. At Pentecost, the diversity of languages is not overcome by a single tongue — everyone hears in their own language. The Spirit does not dissolve difference into uniformity. The Spirit creates communion across difference. This is the eschatological vision the church is supposed to be enacting: not a community where everyone becomes the same, but a community where the divisions that once defined who was in and who was out have been reorganized by the new humanity in Christ.

Paul's formulation in Galatians 3:28 — "there is neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" — is often reduced to a spiritual claim with no political content. But the Jew/Gentile distinction in the first century was precisely a border claim, enforced by real legal and social mechanisms that determined who had access to the covenant community and on what terms. Paul is not saying the distinction doesn't matter. He is saying it no longer determines access. The border has been crossed by the reconciling work of Christ, and the community that gathers around that work must embody, in its actual practice, the reality that the border no longer holds.

---

None of this produces a simple Christian immigration policy, and anyone who claims it does is collapsing a complex political question into a proof-text. The church has always recognized that nations have legitimate interests in security, in the coherence of their communities, in the ordered management of their borders. These are not wicked interests. They are the interests of governors whom Paul himself describes as servants of God for good (Romans 13:4).

But the question of what a nation may legitimately do at its border is not the same as the question of what posture the church must take toward the human beings who stand at the border. Those are different questions, and confusing them is how Christian nationalism does its most damaging theological work. The political question of immigration policy is a question about which reasonable people can hold different views while remaining committed to justice. The pastoral question of how the Christian community is to regard the stranger, the sojourner, the displaced person — that question has a much clearer biblical answer.

The counter-argument, stated honestly, is this: national sovereignty is a God-given structure of order, and the church that advocates for open borders is undermining the stability that makes human flourishing possible. There is something to this. Order is a good. Stable governance is a good. The collapse of institutional structures does real harm to real people, and the church must not be naive about that harm. Walter Brueggemann's distinction between the prophetic tradition and mere utopianism is relevant here [cite — confirm]: the prophets were not naive about power; they engaged it.

But the argument that national sovereignty licenses any treatment of the person who has crossed or is seeking to cross the border goes well beyond what any serious theological tradition can sustain. The family separation, the detention without adequate care, the deportation of people who have built their lives in a country for decades — these are not neutral administrative questions. They are actions being performed on people who bear the image of God. The imago Dei does not expire at the border. It does not depend on documentation. It is not revocable by state action.

---

The American church, in the current political climate, is being asked to choose between its nationalist commitments and its theological ones, and many are choosing nationalism while insisting they are choosing theology. This is not a new temptation. The church in Germany in the 1930s faced a version of it, and the Barmen Declaration's famous insistence that Jesus Christ "is the one Word of God whom we have to hear, and whom we have to trust and obey in life and death" was written precisely to name the theological idolatry of a nationalism that had claimed divine sanction [cite — confirm].

Bonhoeffer's willingness to stake the theological claim against the political one — and to do it at personal cost — is instructive not as a prescription for identical action in different circumstances, but as a model of what theological integrity requires when the political culture presses the church to organize its life around something other than the Gospel. The church that allows its vision of the neighbor to be determined by the border rather than by the character of God has made the border into a theological category that the biblical text refuses to give it.

---

The specific pastoral question for the pastor who is reading this is simple but costly: do the people in your congregation who have come from somewhere else — who are immigrants, documented or otherwise, or who are refugees, or who are ethnically identified with nations your country regards with suspicion — do they experience your church as a place that sees them? Not as a demographic the church is trying to reach, not as a ministry category, but as people whose full humanity is honored, whose stories are considered, whose children are safe?

That is the minimum. The minimum is not the maximum. The maximum involves the kind of advocacy that is costly — speaking publicly about policies that harm vulnerable people even when the political cost is real, building relationships with advocacy organizations that work on these questions, asking the congregation to examine the gap between what it preaches about the imago Dei and what it advocates for in the public square.

God's justice transcends borders. That is not a liberal position. That is the position of the God who called Abraham to leave his borders and become a blessing to all nations, who identified Israel's own story with the story of displacement and commanded them to honor the memory in how they treated the stranger, who sent his Son not to a nation but to the world, and who is, even now, gathering from every tribe and tongue and people and nation the community that will stand before the throne.

The border is not the final word. The King is.
