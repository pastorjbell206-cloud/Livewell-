The names accumulate. Pastors of megachurches. Founders of parachurch organizations. Denominational leaders with decades of respected service. The pattern has become familiar enough that a segment of the public now reads each new revelation without surprise — as confirmation of what they already suspected rather than as an aberration that demands explanation. That readiness to file the scandal as expected is, in its own way, a form of damage that the church has done to its own witness.

The temptation when these failures become public is to treat them as individual moral failures of specific individuals with specific character defects, who happened to end up in positions of authority. This reading is not wrong — individuals make choices, and the choices these men made were real, their consequences are real, and the people they harmed deserve more than a systemic analysis. But the individual-failure reading, by itself, is insufficient. Because the structural conditions that allowed these failures to persist — in some cases for years, in some cases for decades — were not accidents. They were features of systems that had been built in ways that made accountability structurally difficult.

This is the lesson the church has been slow to learn, and slowest to apply where it costs most.

---

The concentration of authority in a single charismatic leader with no meaningful accountability structure around him. The culture of deference that frames questioning the pastor as questioning God. The financial arrangements that give a leader personal control over resources without independent oversight. The complaints that were received, taken seriously for a moment, and then managed into silence by institutions more committed to their reputation than to the protection of vulnerable people. These are not the conditions of individual moral failure. They are architectural. They were built. They can be rebuilt differently.

But first, the church has to admit what it built and why it built it.

The celebrity pastor model — the singular leader whose personal charisma and vision define the organization's identity — is not an ancient Christian form. It is a twentieth-century American import that borrowed heavily from corporate and entertainment culture. The senior pastor as CEO. The church as brand. The denomination or board as shareholders whose job is to support and protect rather than to check and correct. This model creates enormous efficiency in the short run. It creates enormous catastrophic risk in the long run, because it concentrates authority without building the structural constraints that make authority bearable for finite and fallible human beings.

And we are all finite. We are all fallible. The leader who believes himself exempt from that condition is the most dangerous kind.

---

The biblical warnings about the corruptions of unchecked power are not incidental to the tradition. They are among its most persistent themes. Samuel warns Israel that a king will take your sons, your daughters, your fields, your servants, and your harvest (1 Samuel 8:10–18). He is not only predicting what kings do. He is naming what power does to people who hold it without constraint. The prophets return again and again to the specific abuses committed by leaders who were accountable to no one but themselves — shepherds who fed themselves instead of the flock, whose indictment runs through Ezekiel 34 with the specificity of an institutional audit.

The elder qualifications in 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1 are not a list of spiritual virtues in the abstract — they are a description of a man whose character has been tested in contexts where character can actually be observed: in his household, in his community, over time. Paul is explicitly saying that the inner life of a leader can be evaluated only through visible patterns of behavior over sustained time and in multiple relationships. The New Testament does not trust personal charisma as a sufficient basis for leadership authority. It does not even trust personal piety, unseen by others. It trusts demonstrated character in relationship, verified by multiple witnesses across different domains of life.

That is not how most American churches select their leaders. And the gap between the New Testament model and the American model is where most of the damage accumulates.

---

The church that reads the current reckoning and responds primarily by better vetting individual character has not understood what the reckoning is about. Character matters, enormously. But character without structure is insufficient. Every leader, whatever his character, benefits from accountability — not because he cannot be trusted, but because trustworthiness is better sustained with oversight than without it. This is not a cynical claim. It is a realistic one, rooted in the same theology that insists on grace: human beings are not designed to hold unchecked power well. We bend. We justify. We tell ourselves that the exception we are making is warranted by the good we are doing.

The structures that prevent this bending are not insults to good leaders. They are protections for them. The leader with genuinely nothing to hide has nothing to fear from genuine accountability — and is, in fact, better protected by it, because structures that catch abuse early protect him from the years of compounding damage that happen when bad patterns are allowed to develop without check.

Here is the harder version of this: the resistance to accountability in the church is almost never named as resistance to accountability. It is named as faith. Trust in God. The freedom of the Spirit. The unique nature of ministry, which cannot be reduced to corporate governance structures. I have made some version of that argument myself, and I am not proud of it. It is a self-protective argument dressed in theological language. It confuses institutional self-protection with spiritual discernment. It is precisely the kind of argument that allows bad systems to perpetuate themselves while claiming divine sanction.

---

There is a version of the accountability conversation that the church engages fairly easily, and it is the wrong version. It is the version focused on individual moral failures and the need for better background checks. The church can discuss that without feeling threatened, because it keeps the problem at the level of individuals — bad apples, outliers, people who should not have been trusted — rather than at the level of structures that required no particular bad character to produce bad outcomes.

The harder version asks: what does our governance actually look like? Who has the authority to remove the senior leader if evidence of serious misconduct arises? Who has access to the financial records, and are those people genuinely independent or are they drawn from the same relational network as the leader himself? When someone brings a complaint against a staff member or leader, who receives it, and what prevents that person from managing it into silence? Are there anonymous reporting mechanisms that do not route through the very leadership being reported on?

These questions are not comfortable. They require admitting that the current arrangement may be inadequate, and that admitting it means doing the slower, harder work of restructuring it. That work does not come with applause. It does not generate a sermon series that fills the room. It is the institutional equivalent of preventive medicine: it protects against outcomes that, if they happen, will be devastating, and the protection is invisible until the crisis it prevented does not arrive.

---

The people who were harmed by pastoral failure in these institutions did not need a better background check on the man who hurt them. They needed a system where someone beside that man was empowered to act on what they reported.

This is not a small distinction. The individual-vetting approach to accountability improvement leaves the institutional structures that allowed harm untouched. It says: we will try to identify bad actors before they enter the system. But the institutions where the most sustained damage occurred were not institutions that had accidentally hired a single bad actor. They were institutions where the culture itself had made accountability functionally impossible — where loyalty to the leader was the dominant institutional value, where questioning was managed rather than welcomed, where the welfare of the institution's reputation was routinely weighed against the welfare of individual people and the institution reliably won.

You cannot fix that with a better background check. You fix it by building structures where multiple people share genuine authority, where financial oversight is genuinely independent, where complaints can be received by someone other than the leader being complained about, where the board has both the information and the authority to act when something is wrong, and where the stated commitment to transparency can actually be tested.

I have been in ministry long enough to know how accountability gets resisted. It gets framed as a failure of trust, as an insult to a leader's proven track record, as an institutional imposition on the freedom of the Spirit. The leadership model that resists structural accountability will eventually produce the kind of damage that the recent scandals represent — not always, not inevitably, but with enough frequency that the pattern constitutes a diagnosis.

---

The church has an opportunity now that it will not always have. The current moment of public reckoning, painful as it is, has made the accountability conversation possible in ways it was not a decade ago. Congregations who once would have defended their pastor against any suggestion of structural oversight are now, in many cases, open to that conversation. Denominations that once protected institutional reputation above individual welfare are reforming governance out of necessity. The window is open. The question is whether the church will use it to build structures that could actually protect people — including its own leaders — or whether it will wait for the window to close and the conversation to end and then return to the arrangements that are comfortable.

Build the structures that could catch you if you fell. Not because you expect to fall, but because the people who love you deserve the protection of knowing that if you did, someone would have the authority to act. Build them before you need them, because the moment you need them is the moment you have already lost the capacity to build them honestly.

The church that waits for a scandal to take accountability seriously has waited too long.

The scandal already happened. The lesson is here, for those willing to learn it.
