The Bible was not written in English. This is an obvious statement that carries non-obvious implications. The English Bible that most Christians read is a translation — an extraordinarily valuable translation, in many cases a masterwork of linguistic and theological precision, but a translation nonetheless. And every translation is a series of decisions about which range of meaning in the original language to bring forward and which to leave behind.

Most readers never see those decisions. They receive the English as the text, and the text feels self-evident, because language always feels self-evident to the person who grew up inside it. The English Bible has been part of the furniture of the church for so long that the distance between it and the original has become invisible. That invisibility has costs, and some of them are doctrinal.

## A Few Examples That Are Not Trivial

Take love. English has one word and makes it carry everything — the love of God, the love of a marriage, the affection for a particular meal, the attachment to a city. The Greek of the New Testament works with a more differentiated vocabulary, and the distinctions are not decorative. When the risen Jesus questions Peter by the Sea of Tiberias (John 21:15-17), the text moves between *agapaō* and *phileō*, and readers have argued for centuries about whether the shift carries theological weight or is simply natural variation in Greek literary style [cite — confirm the debate; scholarly opinion is divided]. Whether or not that specific passage turns on the word distinction, the broader point stands: English flattens a range that the original could articulate, and the reader who does not know the range exists cannot even form the question.

More consequential than the love example is the problem of the second-person plural. English lost its plural "you" centuries ago, and the Bible has suffered for it ever since. When Paul writes to the Corinthians "Do you not know that you are God's temple and that God's Spirit dwells in you?" (1 Corinthians 3:16), every "you" in that sentence is plural in the Greek. He is talking about the community — the *ekklesia*, the gathered people — not the individual believer's personal interior. But generations of English readers, hearing "you" as singular because that is the only "you" English has, have read the text as a comment about personal piety, about being careful with your body, about individual holiness.

The communal meaning was not merely spiritually interesting. It was Paul's whole argument. He was writing to a fractured congregation, divided over personalities and party loyalties, and his point was that the community together was the temple, and that the factions were tearing down the structure where God had chosen to live. The individualized reading misses the pastoral emergency Paul was addressing. The Greek does not allow the miss. The English nearly requires it.

## The Hebrew Side

The Old Testament carries its own irreducible depths. *Hesed* — the covenantal faithfulness of God — is among the most important words in the Hebrew Bible and among the most stubbornly untranslatable. The King James rendered it "lovingkindness." The ESV often reads "steadfast love." The New Jerusalem Bible reaches for "faithful love." Each translation captures something and sacrifices something else. *Hesed* carries at once loyalty, loving commitment to a covenant relationship, reliability across time, and the specific tenderness of a love that will not break even when the other party has given it grounds to. No English phrase holds all of that together without losing some of it.

*Shalom* presents the same problem. The word is almost always rendered "peace," and peace is not wrong — but *shalom* is not merely the absence of conflict. It is wholeness, completeness, the state in which all relationships are rightly ordered, in which every person has what they need to flourish, in which creation is functioning as it was made to function. Read "peace" where the text says *shalom* and you have received a smaller promise than the one God made. The God who promises *shalom* is not promising the cessation of noise. He is promising the restoration of everything.

The prophet Isaiah uses the word in the famous passage of Isaiah 53:5: the suffering servant is chastised so that *our shalom might come*. Read through the English, it sounds like a promise of inner calm. Read through the Hebrew, it is a cosmic claim — that the world's fracture, from creation through history, is being addressed at the cross, and that what emerges on the other side is not merely the absence of tension but the restoration of all things to their intended order.

## What This Is Not

I am not arguing that the English Bible is unreliable. It is reliable. The canon of Scripture has been served by scholars across centuries whose work of translation has been conducted with extraordinary care and genuine faith. The central things — the death and resurrection of Jesus, the love of God, the way of salvation, the life of discipleship — are clear in any serious English translation, and God has seen to it that those who do not know Greek or Hebrew can know him fully. The original languages are not the gospel's locked safe. They are the gospel's texture.

What the original languages offer is not access to a different gospel but a richer read of the one that is already there. They are the difference between hearing a symphony in a recording and reading the score — the recording is the thing, but the score shows you what was composed, and knowing both is worth more than knowing only one.

There is a guardrail here that is worth naming plainly, because the languages get misused. A little Greek is a dangerous thing, and the internet is full of people "correcting" their pastor with a half-understood word study, as though a single lexicon entry overturns the consensus of every translator and commentator in the room. That is not scholarship. It is the performance of scholarship, and the performance tends to serve the user's pre-existing conclusion rather than the text's actual meaning. The original languages, used rightly, make you more humble before the text, not less — more aware of how much careful labor stands between you and the Greek, more grateful for the community of scholars who have tended it.

I have misused them myself. I have landed on a Greek word that seemed to support a point I wanted to make, and given it more weight than the context warranted, and preached from that word as though the study settled the question when it only deepened it. The log I needed to remove from my own eye was visible enough in retrospect.

## Why Serious Readers Should Care

The history of doctrinal error is substantially a history of translation decisions and the interpretive communities that formed around them. Councils and controversies in the early church often turned on whether the Greek of the New Testament supported one reading or another — whether *homoousios* (of the same substance) or *homoiousios* (of similar substance) was the right word to describe the Son's relationship to the Father, and whether that difference of one iota was trivial or everything [cite — confirm Nicene Council, 325]. The church fathers who hammered out the Nicene Creed were doing Greek grammar in the service of pastoral survival. They knew that if they got the word wrong, they would give the congregation a savior who was merely very good, not God incarnate — and a very good savior cannot bridge the distance between God and the humanity that has broken his image.

That is not historical trivia. The same precision is required in every generation. When Paul tells the Galatians that Christ "redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13), the Greek word *ginomai* is not "bearing" but "becoming" — a distinction that matters for whether what happened at the cross was substitution in the fullest sense, Christ actually taking on the status of the cursed, or something softer. When Paul describes love in 1 Corinthians 13, the Greek word *agape* has a shape — not sentiment, not warm feeling, but the costly, chosen, persistent orientation toward another's good — that the English "love" cannot carry alone.

## The Practical Implication

You do not need to learn Greek or Hebrew to be a faithful Christian or a faithful preacher. Most believers who have never touched an interlinear have deeper Christian lives than many who have. The languages are not the gate.

But it is worth knowing the layer is there. It is worth reading commentaries that take the original languages seriously, so that the texture they provide reaches you even if you are receiving it secondhand. It is worth being a little more suspicious of confident readings that rest entirely on one English word, particularly when the doctrine seems too neat, the application too convenient, the hard passage too easily resolved.

And it is worth supporting the careful scholarship that tends this tradition — the translators, the commentators, the lexicographers who have spent their lives trying to render faithfully what was written in another language two millennia ago. They are doing something that matters. The depth they protect is not academic ornamentation.

The score is richer than the recording. The recording is still true. But there is more there than you have heard, and the more is worth knowing, because it belongs to the same God who gave you the text in the first place.

He was not being careless about the words. Neither should we.
