No one who came to ministry out of a calling to care for people imagined that one of the most pastorally demanding acts they would ever perform would be firing a staff member. And yet it is. Because the person on the other side of that conversation is a person — someone with a mortgage and a family and an identity that is probably substantially wrapped up in the role you are about to remove them from — and how you carry out that conversation will shape what they believe about the church, and possibly about God, for a long time afterward.

This is why pastors avoid it too long. Not because they are cowardly, though sometimes that is part of it, but because the pastoral instinct is toward care — and care, misapplied, can become a refusal to tell the truth.

Pastoral care that withholds necessary truth is not care. It is a kind of cowardice that hides behind compassion's language while doing something compassion would not permit: leaving a person in a role where they are failing, or in a situation that is damaging them and others, because the pastor cannot bear the grief of the necessary conversation. The mercy that never speaks is not mercy. It is avoidance.

---

Before the question of when, there is the question of whether — whether the situation actually requires termination, or whether it requires a different kind of intervention.

Not every personnel failure is a firing offense. A staff member who is struggling with a specific area of performance, who has not received clear feedback, who has not been held to an articulated standard, who has not been given a defined opportunity to improve — that person has not been given a fair chance to succeed. The pastoral leader who releases someone without having done the harder work of honest feedback and genuine support has not acted with integrity. He has protected himself from the discomfort of a development conversation and called it a leadership decision.

This is the prior question that too many pastors skip over. The months of private frustration, of vague dissatisfaction, of complaining to a spouse or a trusted elder that something is not working — those months often substitute for the direct, honest, early conversation that might have prevented the ending. If you have been noticing a problem for six months without naming it clearly to the person who has the problem, the problem is partly yours. You did not give them the information they needed to change. You deprived them of the chance to succeed by withholding the feedback that would have made success possible.

This matters before any termination conversation, because you need to be honest with yourself about whether you did that prior work. If you did not, the first step is not termination. It is the development conversation that should have happened months ago.

---

The situations that do require termination are clearer than pastors sometimes make them.

When someone's behavior violates the ethical or theological standards of the community — involves dishonesty, moral failure, or conduct that directly harms others — and the violation is ongoing rather than genuinely repented of, that is a termination situation. The clear failure to repent is the critical element here, because repentance and restoration are themselves Christian goods. A staff member who has failed morally but who responds with genuine accountability and a willingness to do whatever the restoration process requires is in a different situation than a staff member who is defensive, who minimizes what happened, who manages the information rather than responding to it.

When a staff member is actively damaging the culture — creating division, undermining trust, poisoning the relationships that the team depends on — and every reasonable intervention has failed to produce change, that is a termination situation. This category is harder, because culture damage is less visible than individual moral failure and the person causing it often has defenders who see only their gifts. But a staff member who is toxic to the team, whatever their individual gifts, is doing damage that exceeds the value of those gifts. The leader who cannot act on this because the person in question is talented or popular or has been there a long time has misunderstood his accountability.

When the role itself no longer exists, or is being restructured in a way that the current person is genuinely unable to fill, that is a termination situation that is often handled with more honesty than the others because it feels less personal. This is the one pastors typically find easiest. It still deserves the same care.

---

When the decision is made, the conversation must be honest and direct.

This is where pastoral instinct most often fails. The desire to soften the blow — to leave the person with hope that is not real, to imply that the door is not closed when it is, to attribute the termination to circumstances rather than to the actual problem — is understandable, and it is harmful. The person being released deserves to know the truth. Not every detail, not with cruelty, but clearly.

They will construct a narrative of what happened either way. Giving them an accurate account allows them to grieve honestly, to learn from the experience, to process it without the distortion of false information. Giving them a false one — or a carefully incomplete one designed to avoid discomfort — leaves them confused, possibly angry in ways that are ultimately directed at the wrong target, and likely to repeat the same patterns in the next role.

Paul's instruction to speak truth in love (Ephesians 4:15) does not offer a choice between truth and love. It insists on both simultaneously. The termination conversation that is all love and no truth leaves someone unable to understand what happened. The conversation that is all truth and no love causes unnecessary damage to someone who is already losing something significant. The discipline is the both-and: clear enough to be understood, warm enough to be humane.

Some specifics. Do not have the conversation on a Friday afternoon before a long weekend, leaving the person with nowhere to turn for two days. Do not have it over email or through an intermediary if you are the person who made the decision. Do not begin it with excessive softening that delays the actual news — the human brain, once it senses the weight in the room, cannot process anything else until the main thing is named. Say it early in the conversation: "I need to let you know that I am ending your employment here." Then give the clear reasons. Then the severance terms. Then be quiet and let them respond.

Let them be angry or hurt. This is appropriate. Do not rush to explain or defend yourself — you have said what needed to be said, and now the pastoral work is to be present to the response, not to manage it. If they raise questions you need to answer, answer them honestly. If they raise accusations that require response, respond to them directly, without defensiveness if possible. You are not trying to win an argument. You are trying to end an employment relationship with integrity.

---

The pastoral care does not end with the conversation.

What happens to the person after they leave matters — whether the severance is genuinely generous, reflecting the church's stated belief about human dignity rather than its minimum legal obligation; whether the public communication about the departure is honest without being exposing; whether the pastor makes himself available for a follow-up conversation if the person needs to process what happened.

The church is not simply an employer. It is, or is supposed to be, a community that holds a particular vision of human worth and of how people should be treated in hard moments. The termination that is legally clean but pastorally cold betrays that vision. The person who is released deserves to experience the church's care even as they experience the church's decision. Both things are possible. It requires more from the pastor, but both things are possible.

There are things to avoid. Do not allow other staff to hear about the termination through the grapevine before the terminated person has had the conversation. Do not say more to the congregation than is necessary — they need to know the person is no longer on staff, and in most cases they do not need the reasons. Do not allow the terminated person to be spoken of unkindly in the aftermath, by you or by anyone else, even when the reasons for the termination were genuinely serious.

---

I have released people from staff. It has never been easy. The ones I handled with the most integrity were the ones where I told the truth, did it directly, provided what I could for the transition, and did not leave the person wondering what had actually happened. They could grieve. They could be angry. But they knew. And several of them, in the months afterward, told me that the clarity — painful as it was — allowed them to heal in ways that the vague and managed exit does not.

The ones I am least proud of are the ones where I delayed too long, softened the truth too much, and left the person without the clear accounting they deserved. The extended vagueness was not kind. It was self-protective. I told myself I was protecting them, but I was protecting myself from their grief. That is not pastoral care. It is pastoral cowardice in pastoral clothing.

The ministry is not served by the compassion that cannot act. The person on the other side of the conversation deserves the difficult gift of your honesty, your directness, and your genuine care — all three, simultaneously, in the same conversation.

Do it clearly. Do it with care. Do not do it later than it should be done.

The moment it is necessary is the moment it should happen.
