Water appears on the first page of the Bible. Before the earth had form, before light, before any living thing — the Spirit of God was hovering over the waters (Genesis 1:2). Water is architecturally present at the beginning of creation and architecturally present at the end: the river of the water of life, flowing from the throne of God through the new Jerusalem (Revelation 22:1–2). Between those two appearances, water carries more theological weight than almost any other material substance in the canon. The parting of the Red Sea. The wilderness thirst. The rock that Moses struck. The Jordan crossing. Jesus's baptism, his conversation with the woman at the well, the water and blood from his side at the cross, the command to baptize in the name of the Father and Son and Spirit.

Water in Scripture is never merely water. It is the medium of deliverance. It is the sign of covenant. It is the substance through which God names his people and the substance through which God's enemies drown. It is what the psalmist cries out for in Psalm 42:1 — *as the deer pants for streams of water, so my soul pants for you, my God* — which means that the desire for actual water and the desire for God are allowed to rhyme in the same sentence, the physical thirst a window onto the spiritual one.

This is why the current global water crisis is a theological issue and not merely a logistical or environmental one.

More than two billion people lack access to safe drinking water [cite — confirm]. In some regions, women and children walk hours each day to reach water sources that are contaminated. People die of waterborne disease at rates that the wealthy world long since ceased to regard as imaginable. The communities most affected by water insecurity are, with remarkable consistency, the poor, the colonized, the politically marginalized — the same communities that appear in Scripture as the specific objects of God's concern.

The church has largely received this reality as a humanitarian concern — something for NGOs and mission agencies, the province of people with technical expertise. That framing is not wrong. Technical expertise matters. Clean water requires engineering. But the theological framing matters too, and its absence explains why the church has engaged this issue with such inconsistency.

The biblical account of water is inseparable from the biblical account of power. Who controls the water controls the life that depends on it. Pharaoh's Egypt sat astride the Nile — the great agricultural engine that made empire possible. The Exodus is partly a story about what happens when the God who made water in the beginning refuses to let his image-bearers remain enslaved to those who control it. Moses is not striking a rock for theological reasons only. He is demonstrating that the God of Israel can provide in the wilderness what Egypt provided in the valley — that the empire does not control the conditions of life, that the Source lies elsewhere.

The Samaritan woman at the well in John 4 is standing at a water source that is itself entangled in the long history of ethnic and religious division in that region. Jesus asks for a drink from a woman he was culturally forbidden to address, at a well that marked a contested heritage. His conversation with her — the offer of living water, the honest engagement with her actual situation, the revelation that he is the Messiah — takes place at the intersection of water, power, and exclusion. It is not an accident. The gospel enters through the door that material conditions open.

The Flint water crisis — in which the city of Flint, Michigan changed its water source in 2014 and exposed tens of thousands of residents, predominantly Black and low-income, to lead contamination — put the theological stakes in immediate terms [cite — confirm]. An American city, within reasonable driving distance of where I preach, watched its children's futures get altered by a failure of governance that followed the exact lines of race and poverty that run through every other form of injustice in this country. The people who were poisoned were not poisoned by accident. They were poisoned because their suffering was treated as less costly than the alternatives.

The church's response was mostly charity — bottled water collections, donation drives. Not nothing. But charity does not name the structural reality. Charity does not ask who made the decision and why they made it and whether the same decision would have been made in a different zip code. Charity tends the wound. Justice asks who inflicted it.

Jeremiah 2:13 records God's indictment of Israel: *My people have committed two sins: They have forsaken me, the spring of living water, and have dug their own cisterns, broken cisterns that cannot hold water.* The image is not only spiritual. It is economic and political. Israel's pursuit of alliances with Egypt and Assyria — the great water-powers of the ancient Near East — over against trust in the God who provides, is being named here as both theological betrayal and political foolishness. The broken cisterns are human-made systems of security that fail precisely because they locate provision in the wrong place.

Contemporary water privatization is worth naming here, because it raises exactly the question Jeremiah was raising. When water systems are transferred from public to private control, the logic of profit replaces the logic of provision. Communities that cannot pay market rates for water lose access. This is not a neutral technical arrangement. It is a theological one: it locates control of a basic condition of human survival in the hands of those who will profit from it. The church that has nothing to say about this arrangement is Jeremiah's audience — sitting comfortably beside the broken cisterns, having forgotten who made the water in the beginning.

The steel-man for the other side deserves honest engagement. The argument runs: water systems require massive infrastructure investment, and private capital can accomplish what public institutions often cannot. Market mechanisms, the argument continues, actually allocate scarce resources more efficiently than government administration. This is not entirely false. There are water systems in the Global South that have improved under private management [cite — confirm]. Technical solutions to water problems are not inherently unjust.

But the theological question is not only about efficiency. It is about what happens at the bottom of the market — to the people who cannot pay, to the communities whose political marginalization means they receive last attention and worst quality. The efficiency argument cannot answer that question, because efficiency optimizes for different goals than justice does. Justice is not efficient. Justice is obligatory. And the test of any water system, on biblical terms, is not whether it runs well for the people who can afford it. It is whether it serves the widow, the orphan, the stranger — the ones Leviticus 19 and Deuteronomy 24 and Zechariah 7:10 and the entire prophetic tradition name as the measure of the community's faithfulness.

Isaiah 55:1 is an invitation that begins with water: *Come, all you who are thirsty, come to the waters; and you who have no money, come, buy and eat!* The eschatological vision is of provision without price — not because provision is free, but because the provision comes from the One who does not require payment for what he made. That vision stands in judgment on every arrangement in which water is controlled by those who charge more than the poor can pay.

I am not arguing for a particular policy framework. I am not equipped to design water infrastructure. But I am arguing that the church cannot receive global water insecurity as merely a technical problem to be solved by technicians. The same Lord who hovered over the waters at creation, who parted the sea for the enslaved, who struck the rock in the wilderness, who offered living water to the woman at the well, who turned water into wine and walked on water and rose from a tomb that could not hold him — that Lord has something to say about who gets clean water and who dies from the lack of it.

The church's voice is not the engineer's voice. But the church's voice is the one that refuses to let the question of water become purely technical. The one that says: these are image-bearers dying of thirst. The one that asks which political and economic arrangements are producing this death. The one that names injustice even when the injustice is distributed so widely and operates so quietly that naming it feels like aggression against the beneficiaries.

Every Sunday I pour water into a baptismal. The act is theological — death and resurrection, the old washed away, the new given. I have done it for men and women who will walk out of the church into a world where, depending on where they live and what they earn, that water either flows freely or is a daily negotiation with scarcity.

The water connects. The theology and the material reality are not separate domains. The God who is the spring of living water has an interest in how his image-bearers access the water he made. The church that says otherwise has separated the gospel from the world the gospel enters.

Water is the first substance named in the Bible. It is the last gift in the vision of the restored city. Between those two moments, billions of people are thirsty. That is not an accident the church can afford to miss.
