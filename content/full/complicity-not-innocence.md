The comfortable Christian position in a culture with genuine injustice is neither endorsement nor active resistance. It is something more subtle and more convenient: the claim of non-involvement, the insistence that the church is above the political fray, the cultivation of a carefully maintained neutrality that allows the institution to maintain its relationships with all sides while making no enemies and taking no risks.

We have a word for what we think that is. We call it innocence. Scripture calls it something else.

It calls it complicity. And complicity is not innocence.

## The Text That Takes Away the Exit

We badly want there to be a safe third option between doing wrong and paying the cost of doing right — a place where we can keep our hands clean by keeping them in our pockets. The Bible keeps refusing to grant it. Proverbs 24:10-12 is direct: "If you faint in the day of adversity, your strength is small. Rescue those who are being taken away to death; hold back those stumbling to the slaughter. If you say, 'Behold, we did not know this' — does not he who weighs the heart perceive it? Does not he who keeps watch over your soul know it, and will he not repay man according to his work?"

Notice what the text anticipates. It does not assume that the bystander is malicious. It assumes that the bystander will have an excuse — we did not know, we were not informed, the situation was unclear, we stayed out of it to keep the peace. The text dismisses the excuse before it is offered. The God who weighs hearts is not fooled by a silence we have arranged in order to remain comfortable.

This is the priest and the Levite on the Jericho road (Luke 10:30-37). They did not beat the man. They did not rob him. They did nothing at all — they saw him in the ditch and passed by on the other side — and Jesus tells the story precisely so that their nothing becomes the sin. The Samaritan is the neighbor not because he avoided causing harm but because he absorbed cost. He stopped. He bound the wounds. He brought the man to the inn. He paid out of his own resources and promised to pay more. The parable was constructed to remove the comfortable middle position. There is no clean way to walk past the man in the ditch.

## The Historical Arc

This is not a new theological problem. The prophets of Israel were addressing it in the eighth century before Christ. Amos, writing during the reign of Jeroboam II in a period of Israeli prosperity, was not primarily angry at the oppressors. He was angry at the religious community that maintained its worship life while the economic arrangements of the society were grinding the poor into the ground, and said nothing, and thought that its continued temple attendance was innocence. "I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies" (Amos 5:21). The God who says that is not drawing a distinction between the evil and the neutral. He is collapsing the distinction. The worship that continues while injustice continues is not neutral. It is, in God's accounting, hateful.

The same logic appears in Isaiah 1, where the opening chapter of the whole book consists of God rejecting the religious performance of a community that has neglected the widow, the orphan, and the oppressed: "Your hands are full of blood" (Isaiah 1:15). The hands were full of blood not because the worshippers had been violent. They had been merely absent. They had performed the right rituals while the society they inhabited crushed the people God had commanded them to protect. The prophet calls it blood.

Ezekiel 33 uses the image of the watchman who sees the sword coming and does not blow the trumpet. The watchman does not invite the sword. He does not sharpen it. He merely fails to warn, and the text holds him responsible for the deaths that follow. Silence in the face of a known danger is not neutrality. It is complicity. The text says so.

## The Mechanism Worth Understanding

Here is how good people end up complicit without ever deciding to be. Injustice does not generally require the cooperation of the cruel. It requires only the silence of the decent. The systems that have sustained the greatest human suffering throughout history were not primarily built on the energy of the malicious. They were sustained by the routine non-participation of people who privately found the situation troubling but publicly kept their discomfort to themselves.

In the antebellum American South, the slave economy did not need every white Southerner to be a slaveholder. It needed a sufficient number of non-slaveholding whites to find the arrangement acceptable enough that they would not disrupt it. It needed the churches to stay focused on personal piety and eternity. It needed the reasonable people to believe that the question was too complicated for a simple answer, that speaking up would only make things worse, that they were protecting their family by keeping their heads down. And the silence was as much a structural support for the institution as the chains were.

I have done this. I have looked at situations where the suffering of a specific category of people was visible and the cost of saying something was real, and I have calculated that cost and decided I could not afford it. I have dressed the calculation in theological language — I don't want to be reactionary, I want to think carefully before I speak, the church should be careful about political entanglements — and the language was not entirely wrong, but it was not entirely honest either. The honest name for most of my carefully maintained neutrality was not prudence. It was self-protection.

This is a confession before it is a critique.

## The Counter-Position Answered

The danger on the other side is real and requires honest engagement. A church that makes itself the vehicle for every political grievance — that treats every issue as the hill on which the witness must be planted, that conflates the Gospel with a particular political coalition, that burns its credibility on fights that have little to do with the cross — will eventually lose the ability to speak at all, because the credibility that makes speech heard will be gone.

That danger is real. But the solution to that danger is not silence on everything. It is discernment about what the Gospel actually requires speech about. And there is a category of human suffering — particularly the suffering of those the Scripture specifically identifies as the objects of God's special concern, the poor, the orphan, the widow, the stranger, the imprisoned — where the church's silence is not careful neutrality. It is the failure of the witness.

The distinction is worth preserving: not every political question is a Gospel question, but some political questions are Gospel questions, and the ones that involve the dignity and survival of the vulnerable almost always are. The church that cannot tell the difference between the two, that lumps all public questions into "political" and therefore out-of-bounds, has made a theological decision while pretending to make no decision at all. The decision it has made is to let the present arrangements stand by declining to challenge them.

## What the Witness Costs

The world is not primarily persuaded or repelled by the church's words. It watches what the church will risk. A church that always finds a reason to stay out of it — that is reliably absent from the ditch, that never says anything that might cost it a relationship or a budget line or an uncomfortable conversation — teaches the watching world that the Gospel is a private comfort with no claim on public suffering. The world is right to find that unconvincing.

The witness of the early church was not primarily its correct theology, though the theology was correct. It was the visible practice of a community that absorbed cost for others. They took in the abandoned infants left to die on the hillsides outside Roman cities. They buried the dead during the plague when no one else would. They shared resources across the lines that the surrounding culture considered fixed. The watching world did not say: what an interesting set of doctrines. It said: look how they love — and the love was visible precisely because it cost something.

When the church stops being the community that absorbs cost for others, it stops being the evidence for its own proclamation. The silence that protects us from inconvenience purchases the silence of the watching world, which has looked and concluded that the Gospel we preach does not extend to the ditch.

There is no innocent bystander on the Jericho road. There is the one who stops and the one who passes by. The passing by always feels like nothing. It was never nothing. God weighs the hearts. He knows what we knew, and when we knew it, and what the silence cost.
