The history of Christian missions is a history divided against itself. On one side are the men and women who carried the gospel to places it had not been — who established hospitals, schools, and clinics, who reduced unwritten languages to writing, who translated Scripture into hundreds of tongues, who sat with the dying in contexts of disease and danger that killed missionaries with the same indifference as everyone else. This history is real. It is not fabricated by nostalgia.

On the other side of the ledger is the history the church has been slower to name. The same missionary enterprise that carried the gospel also, frequently, traveled under the flag of empire. In the colonial period, missionary societies and colonial governments were often not distinct interests competing for priority but allies with shared assumptions about civilization, progress, and who was equipped to provide both. The Doctrine of Discovery — the fifteenth-century papal decrees that authorized Christian monarchs to claim dominion over any land not inhabited by Christians — created the legal and theological framework inside which centuries of missionary expansion operated. **What Colonialism Actually Means**

Colonialism is not primarily about individual intention. A missionary who arrived in Sub-Saharan Africa in 1885 with a genuine love for the people and a sincere desire to see them come to faith was still operating within a system that had organized itself around the fundamental assumption that the colonized peoples were, in some essential sense, not yet fully human — not yet fully historical agents capable of determining their own future, not yet custodians of cultures worth preserving, not yet in possession of religious and intellectual resources that a proper missionary enterprise should take seriously.

The Christian gospel, as many of those missionaries understood it, was not just the news about Jesus Christ. It was also the news about the superiority of European social organization, the necessity of European educational systems, the desirability of European dress and language and domestic arrangements. The two were so thoroughly fused that the church planted in many colonial contexts was not the body of Christ expressed through a specific people's cultural forms. It was a European institution transplanted to new soil, requiring the colonized to abandon their own forms to enter it.

The consequences of this fusion have not resolved themselves. The postcolonial scholar Lamin Sanneh, himself a Christian who converted from Islam and taught at Yale Divinity School, argued that the translation of Scripture into indigenous languages was a seed that eventually subverted colonial Christianity — that when the Bible became readable in one's own tongue, the capacity to read it outside the framework of the colonial missionary increased. [cite — confirm] But the institutional habits created by colonial missions — the dependency on Western funding, the prestige hierarchies that placed Western missionaries above national church leaders, the assumption that theology was a Western possession to be exported rather than a global conversation to be entered — these persist in forms that continue to damage the global church.

**Justice as Prerequisite, Not Addition**

The claim is not that individual missionaries should have refused to preach the gospel until unjust political systems were dismantled. That framing misunderstands both the history and the argument. The claim is that missions conducted without a simultaneous commitment to the justice that is indigenous to the gospel will always distort what it carries.

The gospel of Jesus Christ is, among many things, the announcement that God has acted in history to set the world right — to restore the shalom that sin has broken, to establish the kingdom in which the powers of domination and death do not have the final word. That announcement cannot be carried faithfully by an enterprise that partners with domination, that treats the structural arrangements benefiting the carriers of the gospel as natural or neutral, that brings good news to individual souls while leaving the systems that degrade those souls undisturbed.

Isaiah understood this connection. The commission God gave in Isaiah 61:1 — "to proclaim good news to the poor, to bind up the brokenhearted, to proclaim freedom for the captives and release from darkness for the prisoners" — was not a social service checklist appended to a spiritual message. It was the single integrated declaration. Good news to the poor is good news that addresses the condition of poverty. Freedom for captives is freedom that touches real captivity. Jesus quoted these words in Luke 4:18-19 to announce his own mission. The mission was not divisible.

The church that carries a gospel of individual salvation to communities whose poverty is produced by economic systems the church supports, whose political marginalization the church ignores, whose cultural inheritance the church dismisses as superstition — that church is not carrying the full gospel. It is carrying a diminished version that requires the recipient to receive the spiritual benefits while the conditions of their material life remain unchanged, or worsen, or are actively managed by interests the missionary enterprise is allied with.

**What This Looks Like Now**

Colonial missions as a formal enterprise ended with the formal end of colonialism. The problem is that many of the assumptions and patterns that characterized colonial missions did not end with the political arrangement that housed them. They reproduced themselves in the institutions and funding structures and theological frameworks of the contemporary missionary enterprise.

The short-term mission trip is the obvious example. In a typical arrangement, a North American church group travels to a developing nation, conducts a week of construction or evangelism or vacation Bible school, and returns with photographs, stories, and a renewed sense of purpose. The local church in the destination country — if one exists — is often peripheral to the planning. The nationals who do the actual sustained work of ministry in that context, who know the language and the culture and the history, are frequently the support crew for the visiting team rather than the primary agents of the mission.

This is not malicious. It is structural. The funding flows from the sending church to the mission project. The accountability runs in the same direction. The story that gets told at the sending church — the story that sustains the donation and the emotional investment — is the story of what the visiting team experienced. That story's protagonist is the North American. The national church's agency, complexity, theological depth, and capacity for self-determination does not fit easily into a two-minute video for the Sunday service.

The result is a pattern that repeats the core movement of colonialism: resources and authority flowing from the Global North to the Global South, with the people of the South as recipients and beneficiaries rather than agents, custodians of their own mission, and teachers of what the global church in the North badly needs to learn.

**The Harder Reckoning**

The missionary enterprise needs to reckon honestly with who it has served. It has served some of the people it intended to serve. It has also served the ego of the sending culture, the institutional interests of missionary organizations, the emotional needs of donors who require a legible story about their contribution, and — in the colonial period specifically — the interests of empires that found Christianity a useful accompaniment to conquest.

Naming this is not the same as declaring the missionary enterprise bankrupt or claiming that no genuine gospel work was done. It is the same as what the biblical prophets did when they named the failures of Israel's worship: they did not conclude that worship was wrong. They concluded that worship divorced from justice was a lie. The prophet Amos put it without qualification: "I hate, I despise your religious festivals; your assemblies are a stench to me. Even though you bring me burnt offerings and grain offerings, I will not accept them" (Amos 5:21-22). The worship was real. The injustice was also real. God held both in view and rendered a verdict.

**What Missions With Justice Requires**

It requires, first, the genuine centering of national and local church leadership in every mission enterprise. Not as token representation. As actual authority — as the people who decide the strategy, control the resources, set the theological agenda, and determine what partnership with outside bodies looks like. The West has much to offer the global church in terms of material resources. It has far less to offer in terms of theological leadership to communities that have been reading the Bible under conditions of poverty, persecution, and political marginalization that produce insights the comfortable church has not discovered.

It requires, second, honest engagement with the systems that create the conditions into which mission is sent. The North American church that supports a food distribution ministry in a country whose food insecurity is produced in part by agricultural trade policies that the sending country's government promotes and the sending church does not challenge is engaged in an exercise that assuages conscience without addressing cause.

It requires, third, the willingness to receive from the global church — to accept that the church in the Global South, the church in Korea and Nigeria and Brazil and Indonesia, is not the product of Western missionary export that has reached an intermediate stage of maturity. It is the majority of the world's Christians. It has theological resources, spiritual practices, and ecclesiological wisdom that the declining Western church needs. The partnership is not from strength to weakness. It never was.

The gospel is not a Western possession. It arrived in the West as a gift from a Middle Eastern Jewish community. It passed through African Christianity before Augustine shaped it. It belongs to the whole church, which means that its carriers must carry it in ways that honor the dignity and agency of every community that receives it.

Missions without that commitment is not faithfulness. It is the repetition of an error the church has made for long enough.
