It was a Tuesday afternoon when I finally admitted it to myself. I was preparing a sermon on grief — Psalm 22, the one Jesus quoted from the cross — and I was not praying. I was workshopping. I was testing the lines. I was listening to how the material would land in the room on Sunday morning and editing accordingly. The sermon was about a man's abandonment by God, and I was producing content about it. The distance between those two things had opened without my permission, and I had not noticed because the sermons were still good. People were still moved. The machine was running.

That is the particular danger of performance-driven ministry. It works for a long time before it destroys you, and during the time it is working, you have almost no way to know the difference from the inside. The congregation cannot tell. The metrics cannot tell. Your own emotional experience cannot tell, because the performance generates its own adrenaline, and adrenaline is a reasonable counterfeit of joy if you do not inspect it too closely.

This is not a modern problem. It is a problem as old as the office.

Jeremiah carried it. The prophet who wept over Jerusalem, who dictated laments to Baruch, who told God he did not want to speak anymore and found the word burning in his bones like fire — Jeremiah 20 gives us the anatomy of a man who could not tell the difference between his calling and his crisis, who had been performing faithfulness so long that he no longer knew where the performance ended and the person began. The Pastoral Letters to Timothy understood it: Paul does not say simply *be competent*. He says *watch your life and your doctrine closely* (1 Timothy 4:16). The doctrine first, then the life. The sequence matters. The interior first, then the public work. You cannot reverse the order and keep the goods.

The Reformers had a phrase for the opposite condition: *coram Deo*, before the face of God. Life lived in conscious awareness of the divine presence, not as an emotional experience but as an ontological reality — everything done before an audience of One. Luther's early polemic against indulgences was not primarily ecclesiological; it was a protest against a system that had made religious performance profitable, that had given people a way to satisfy the demand of God without being changed by the encounter with God. The indulgence was performance made sacramental. And Luther knew it, in part, because he had watched himself do the same thing in a different register. He had performed his way through the monastery for years, flagellating himself toward holiness while missing the Gospel that made holiness possible. The Reformation came from a man who had been performing for God and finally stopped.

I came to faith from atheism, which means I entered the pastoral ministry with a convert's intensity — the kind that can look like earnestness but is sometimes closer to a man still performing for an audience he secretly fears does not exist. I had something to prove. I had left a whole framework of meaning and rebuilt it around the Gospel, and the ministry was, for the first several years, partly an argument I was still making to myself. Preaching was convincing. Prayer was a kind of self-reassurance. The pastoral work was real, but underneath it was a man still not entirely sure he was allowed to be there, still earning the right.

Performance creeps in through the smallest doors. It begins, often, with a good Sunday — a sermon that connected, a response that gratified, a moment in the room where you felt the thing you entered ministry hoping to feel. And then, imperceptibly, you begin to aim for that feeling rather than for the truth that produced it. You begin to construct the sermon not from Scripture outward but from the desired emotional landing backward. You begin to measure the week by how the congregation felt on Sunday rather than by whether you prayed on Monday. The craft — which is a real thing and matters — starts to replace the conversation with God that the craft is supposed to serve.

Eugene Peterson, in his long argument for what he called the contemplative pastor, put it this way: the pastor's most important work is not visible to the congregation and produces no immediate results [cite — confirm]. It is the slow, unhurried work of being shaped by prayer and Scripture before either is used to shape anyone else. Peterson was drawing on a tradition that runs from the Desert Fathers through the medieval mystics through the Puritan tradition of the pastor as a man of God first and a professional second — a tradition that American evangelical pragmatism has largely gutted in favor of measurable outcomes. We know what a healthy attendance curve looks like. We are considerably less certain what a healthy pastor looks like on a Monday morning in his study.

Here is what the performance costs that no one names clearly enough: it costs you the interior life that makes the external work worth doing.

A man can preach the resurrection and not believe it — not the way you believe something that has reorganized your life, but believe it the way you believe a fact you rehearse rather than a reality you inhabit. The performance keeps the words in order while the faith that animated them slowly depletes. The congregation hears a sermon. The preacher knows, somewhere beneath the craft, that he is reading from a script that used to be a letter. The script is accurate. But there is a difference between a letter and a script, and the man at the lectern is not always able to say which one he is holding.

The recovery did not come with a single crisis or a dramatic confrontation. It came slowly, the way the drift had come. It began when I started meeting weekly with two other pastors who had made a specific commitment to tell each other the truth. Not pastoral colleagues sharing sermon illustrations. Two men who agreed, explicitly, that the pastoral face was not allowed in the room. We prayed for each other with the specificity that requires actual knowledge of actual circumstances. We named what was happening inside the work, not just in the work. It was ordinary and it was essential.

The second thing was a decision to re-order the week so that sermon preparation was not the week's first priority. Prayer was. Not prayer in the professional sense — the pastoral prayer, the staff meeting invocation — but the unscheduled, unproductive, honestly formative sitting with God that has nothing to do with Sunday. Eugene Peterson called it the pastor's first responsibility, the formation of the pastor's own soul. I had known this. I had not been doing it. The reorganization of the week felt like loss at first and then felt like coming back to myself.

The third thing was harder. I had to ask whether the image I was projecting from the platform was the same person who existed Monday through Saturday. Not whether I was lying — I was not. But whether the pastoral persona had developed its own internal logic that was not fully accountable to the actual man. There was a gap. Smaller than I feared, larger than I had admitted. Closing it required years, not a weekend.

There is a steel-man for the performance argument. The pastor's role is partly liturgical in the sense that the sermon has a structure, the public self must be ordered enough to bear the congregation's projections without shattering. You are not simply yourself on Sunday morning. You are also a vessel, a representative, a person inhabiting an office. The Reformers understood this — Calvin insisted that the preacher's authority came not from his personality but from the Word he was called to proclaim. The office confers a dignity that does not depend on the individual's interior state on a given Sunday. These things are real and not spiritually corrupt in themselves.

But the vessel must be full of something. The representative must be representing something real. The office must be inhabited by an actual man whose interior life is not a performance. When the performance consumes the person, there is nothing left to offer but a better and better-crafted emptiness. The congregation may not be able to name what they are receiving, but they will feel it. The word for what they are feeling is the word they use when someone says *I love you* and you suspect they are rehearsing rather than meaning it. They are receiving something technically correct and essentially hollow.

Bonhoeffer, writing from Tegel Prison in the last years of his life, asked what it means for a man to know God not at the margins of life — in crisis, in extremity — but at the center, in the ordinary materials of Mondays and meals and the body's needs [cite — confirm]. That question is the pastor's question at every point. Not whether you can perform godliness under pressure. Whether you inhabit it when no one is watching, when the sermon is three days away, when the phone is quiet and the calendar is clear and there is nothing to produce.

That Tuesday afternoon with Psalm 22, I put the outline down. I sat with the text the way you sit with something that is going to address you, not the way you sit with something you are going to use. The sermon that came from it was different. I cannot tell you that the congregation knew the difference. I know I did. The gap between the man and the work was a little smaller. That is what recovery looks like — not dramatic, not sudden, not visible from the platform. A little smaller. Again and again. Until the sermon is a letter and not a script, and the man at the lectern is reading his own name.

Stop performing. Not today, not all at once. But begin. One honest conversation. One un-workshopped prayer. One week where the sermon is a letter to God before it is a communication to the congregation.

That is where pastoring starts. And it is a long road back from the place most of us have already gone.
