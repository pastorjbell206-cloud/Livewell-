There is a map you can pull up. It overlays two datasets — the location of toxic waste sites, industrial polluters, highways built through residential neighborhoods, lead pipe water systems — and the racial composition of those neighborhoods. You do not need a statistics degree to read it. The overlap is not subtle. It is not coincidental. It is the story of a country that decided, across a century of policy, whose air would be clean.

That is the blunt claim, and it deserves to be made bluntly before we dress it in any theological language. Environmental racism is not a conspiracy theory. It is a documented pattern of environmental burdens falling with consistent, measurable disproportionality on communities of color. It is in the siting decisions of the 1930s through the 1970s that placed freeways through Black and Latino neighborhoods. It is in the industrial zoning choices that put refineries and chemical plants adjacent to low-income communities of color. It is in Flint, Michigan — not far from this desk — where a decision made in 2014 by unelected emergency managers exposed a predominantly Black city to lead-contaminated water for eighteen months while state officials disputed the evidence their own scientists were producing.

The church that cannot name this is not preaching a full gospel. Not even close.

---

Begin earlier. The biblical theology of creation does not begin with stewardship as an afterthought. It begins with vocation. Genesis 1–2 places the human creature inside a created order that is declared good before human beings arrive in it. The land, the water, the air — they carry intrinsic value in the economy of creation before they carry instrumental value to us. To be placed in the garden was not to be given a resource extraction operation. It was to be given a home to tend and a world to protect.

The Hebrew word is *shamar* — to keep, to guard, to watch over (Genesis 2:15). It is the same word used later in the Aaronic blessing: the Lord *keep* you (Numbers 6:24). The earth is not ours to exhaust. It is a trust. And a trust can be violated.

When Ezekiel 34 accuses the shepherds of Israel of ruining the pastures and fouling the water with their feet so the sheep must drink what the shepherds have muddied (Ezekiel 34:18), it is speaking of leaders, not of factory owners. But the logic maps precisely. Power fouling what others must drink. That is the structure of environmental racism — not in every case the product of deliberate malice, but the predictable output of systems in which the powerful do not have to live downstream of the decisions they make.

The poor communities, the communities of color, the communities without political leverage — they live downstream. They always have.

---

The documented history is long, but some moments carry specific weight. Robert Bullard, a sociologist at Texas Southern University, published *Dumping in Dixie* in 1990 [cite — confirm year], one of the first systematic studies to document the correlation between race, income, and proximity to hazardous waste facilities in the American South. What he found was not random distribution. It was pattern — a consistent finding that Black communities hosted a disproportionate share of landfills, incinerators, and industrial facilities. The book catalyzed an environmental justice movement that evangelical Christianity largely watched from the outside.

In 1987, the United Church of Christ's Commission for Racial Justice released a report titled *Toxic Wastes and Race in the United States* [cite — confirm year and organization], which found that race was the most significant predictor of proximity to hazardous waste facilities — more significant than poverty, more significant than home values. This report generated significant public attention and essentially no evangelical response of record.

Meanwhile, the largely white evangelical environmental movement — when it finally emerged — organized itself primarily around climate change as an issue of stewardship and creation care, which is a legitimate frame. But it largely left the environmental justice dimension — who bears the worst costs of environmental degradation — underaddressed and often unspoken.

That silence has a shape. The shape is race.

---

Consider the particulars of Flint, and consider them slowly, because they are not ancient history. They are 2014 through 2016. They are children drinking lead-contaminated water. Lead is neurotoxic at any level of exposure. There is no safe dose for a developing brain. The children who drank that water will carry the neurological effects of that exposure into adulthood — cognitive effects, behavioral effects, health effects that follow them through life. The state of Michigan's own scientists raised alarms [cite — confirm]. Those alarms were dismissed. The community continued to drink the water. When a pediatrician named Mona Hanna-Attisha published blood-lead data demonstrating the contamination's effects on children [cite — confirm name and timeline], she was publicly challenged by state officials before being vindicated.

Flint is seventy percent Black. That fact does not explain every decision made in that crisis. But it is naive — or dishonest — to pretend it explains none of them. The structure of American governance has not, historically, applied the same standards of urgency to the health crises of Black communities that it applies to others. That is not a political accusation. It is a historical finding.

The church in Flint showed up. Local churches opened their doors as water distribution points, organized volunteers, took in donations. The generosity was real. But the church that only does charity when the crisis is acute — and does not ask how the crisis was allowed to develop, and does not name what the pattern suggests — has done something less than prophetic.

Charity distributes bottled water. Justice asks why the lead is in the pipes.

---

The steel-man case against calling this a justice issue worth prophetic attention goes roughly like this: these are complex policy questions involving competing economic interests, regulatory tradeoffs, and technical expertise. The church should not oversimplify them. The pattern, while real, does not always reflect explicit racial intent — often it reflects poverty, since poor communities of any race have less political leverage. The church risks politicization when it takes up these issues under a justice frame.

There is something to this. Policy is complex. Correlation does not always indicate explicit discriminatory intent. The church should speak with humility about mechanisms it does not fully understand.

But notice what this argument does. It begins with complexity and arrives at silence. It uses the genuine difficulty of policy questions to justify disengagement from the human consequences. The children in Flint did not need the church to have a sophisticated position on water infrastructure finance. They needed the church to see them, name what was happening to them, and refuse to be quiet about it.

And the prophetic tradition — the tradition of Amos, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Micah — did not wait for policy clarity before naming what the powerful were doing to the vulnerable. It named it. The prophets spoke about economic systems that ground the poor into the dust (Amos 8:4–6), about scales falsified in the markets, about land seizure and legal systems turned against the defenseless. They did not hedge those indictments with careful notes about systemic complexity. The complexity was real. The injustice was also real. The prophets named the injustice.

The church today is not asked to choose between complexity and clarity. It is asked to hold both — to understand the systems and still name the harm.

---

The theology underneath this is *imago Dei* — the image of God. Genesis 1:26–27 locates the image of God in every human being regardless of race, class, or any other marker. This is not a spiritual abstraction. It is a claim about who deserves clean water. It is a claim about which children deserve air that does not make them sick. It is a claim about which communities deserve to have their health crises treated with the same urgency as anyone else's.

If the image of God is borne equally by the child in a wealthy suburb and the child in a neighborhood adjacent to a refinery, then the different quality of air those children breathe is not just a policy problem. It is a theological problem. It is a violation of something fundamental about the created order.

The apostle Paul, writing to the Galatians, is unambiguous: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28). This does not erase the categories. Paul knows perfectly well those categories continue to exist and to carry social weight. What he is saying is that in the economy of the kingdom, they cannot be permitted to determine who is valued and who is expendable.

They cannot. But they do. And the church is called to name the distance between what the kingdom says and what the present order produces.

---

Some churches in communities of color have been doing this work for decades without significant support from white evangelical institutions. The environmental justice work of faith-based organizations in places like Houston's Fifth Ward and along Louisiana's Cancer Alley [cite — confirm] has been persistent and largely invisible to the broader evangelical world. These communities have lived the intersection of race, poverty, and environmental harm. They have organized against it. They have prayed over it. They have buried their dead from it.

That witness deserves more than discovery. It deserves solidarity — the theological commitment, rooted in membership in one body, to treat what happens to those communities as something that has happened to us.

We are not separate. The body does not function when it decides that some of its members do not matter.

---

What does this require in practice? It requires the church to know where it lives. To understand the environmental conditions of the community it claims to serve. To ask, specifically, whether the people in its neighborhood have clean water and breathable air. To ask whose children are bearing the worst of whatever pollution exists nearby. To speak — not vaguely, not only about creation care in the abstract, but about the concrete distribution of environmental harm and the communities that bear it.

It requires the willingness to name race. The church that discusses environmental stewardship without discussing environmental racism has named the right problem and described only part of it.

And it requires the long commitment that prophetic witness always requires. The families living near petrochemical facilities in Louisiana have been fighting those facilities for generations. Their fight is not over. The church's solidarity with them cannot be a news-cycle response. It has to be the kind of presence that shows up before the cameras and stays after they leave.

The map is legible. The pattern is documented. The children are still breathing the air.

The question the church has to answer is not whether environmental racism exists. That question has been answered. The question is whether we will name it or manage it — whether we will stand inside the evidence and speak from it, or manage the discomfort of it from a careful distance.

Managing discomfort is not prophetic. It is its opposite.

The earth is the Lord's, and everything in it (Psalm 24:1). Including the lungs of the children who live downstream.
