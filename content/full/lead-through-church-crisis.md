It comes for almost everyone, eventually. A moral failure on the staff. A split over a decision no one expected to be divisive. A death that should not have happened. A betrayal from someone you trusted with the building keys. The ordinary seasons of ministry are the long flat road. The crisis is the cliff, and a man learns what he is made of on the cliff, not the road.

Here is the hard truth no seminary class quite prepares you for: your people will remember almost nothing about your sermons in a crisis and almost everything about your bearing. They will not recall the three points. They will recall whether your hands were steady. The crisis is not, mainly, a test of your decisions. It is a test of your character under load, conducted in public, with no time to prepare.

So lead the man before you lead the moment.

---

Before we talk about technique, we need to talk about what a crisis actually is in the life of a congregation. Not every difficult situation rises to the category. A budget shortfall is a problem. A resignation of a beloved staff member is a loss. But a crisis, in the technical pastoral sense, is a moment in which the community's identity and trust are both in question simultaneously — when people do not just feel unsettled but genuinely wonder whether the community they belong to is what they thought it was.

Amos walked into that kind of moment in the eighth century before Christ. The economic prosperity of Jeroboam II's reign had created a class of Israelites who were doing very well and a class who were doing very poorly, and the temple at Bethel had accommodated both — the wealthy with their religious festivals and the poor with their crushing debt. Amos's crisis announcement was not welcome. The priest Amaziah told Amos to take his prophecies somewhere else, to earn his money somewhere that could tolerate his message (Amos 7:10–13). What Amos understood, and what Amaziah could not bear, was that a crisis unacknowledged does not go away. It goes underground, and underground it becomes something much harder to address.

That is the first principle. A crisis that is managed rather than named will fester. And the pastor who manages rather than names is not being a leader. He is being an institutional preservationist, which is a different thing entirely.

---

The first thing is to refuse the two easy exits. One exit is panic — the frantic activity that feels like leadership and is mostly fear in motion. The other is denial — the calm that is not peace but avoidance, the pretending that the thing is smaller than it is. Both feel better than the third option, which is to stand inside the trouble with clear eyes and name it honestly. The congregation can survive almost any crisis you will name truthfully. What it struggles to survive is a pastor who manages the optics while the people can feel, underneath, that they are not being told the truth.

Proverbs 11:14 puts it plainly: "For lack of guidance a nation falls, but victory is won through many advisers." The crisis moment activates a pull toward isolation — the pastor managing the information, controlling the narrative, deciding alone what the people need to know and when. That pull is almost always wrong. The people closest to you — the elders, the trusted members of your leadership team — need to know what is happening. Not to manage it for you. To help you carry it.

Gather the people who can tell you the truth. In a crisis you will quickly learn who those are, because they are the ones not managing you.

---

The second thing is to slow down exactly when everything in you wants to speed up. Crises generate pressure for the immediate statement, the fast fix, the decision made before the facts are in. Resist it. The pastor who acts from adrenaline will spend months cleaning up what he did in the first forty-eight hours. The first twenty-four hours of a crisis are usually the worst time to make major decisions and the best time to listen, pray, and gather information.

I have made the mistake of moving too fast. I will not describe the circumstances in detail, but I can tell you what fast looked like: a statement that foreclosed options I should have kept open, a decision that communicated more certainty than I had, a public posture that made it harder to change course when I needed to. The speed felt like strength. It was not. It was anxiety wearing the clothing of decisiveness.

The third chapter of James speaks to this with uncomfortable precision: "Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak and slow to become angry" (James 1:19). This is not a temperament recommendation. In the context of community conflict, it is a leadership prescription. The pastor who speaks first and listens second has reversed the order and will pay for it.

---

The third thing is to grieve in front of them, not perform for them. A pastor who shows no sorrow in a sorrowful hour does not look strong; he looks absent. The people are not asking you to be unmoved. They are asking you to be moved and still standing — to weep and to keep your feet, the way the shortest verse in the Bible shows a Lord who wept at a tomb he was about to open (John 11:35). Jesus knew what was coming. He wept anyway. Not because he did not know the ending, but because the grief of the present moment was real and he did not manage it from a distance.

Strength in a crisis is not the absence of grief. It is grief that has not lost its footing.

The pastor who weeps in front of his congregation is not showing weakness. He is showing that he belongs to them — that their sorrow is his sorrow, that the wound in the body of Christ has reached him too. The congregation can follow a pastor who is hurting. They cannot follow a pastor who seems not to notice that everyone else is.

---

The fourth thing is to keep the long view in a moment built to erase it. A crisis collapses time. It makes the present feel like the whole story. But you serve a church that existed before this and will exist after it, and a Lord who has led his people through far worse than this and kept them. Say so. Not as a way to skip the pain — the people will hear the skip and resent it — but as the floor under the pain. The reminder that the institution may be shaken and the kingdom is not, that the building may be in turmoil and Christ is still its head.

This is the function of the Psalms of lament. Psalm 22 begins in abandonment — "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" — and ends, thirty-one verses later, in confident declaration that God has not ignored the afflicted one's cry (Psalm 22:24). The beginning did not lie. The ending did not resolve the beginning too quickly. The full arc was held. The pastor who can hold that arc for a congregation in crisis — who can say "I see this, I feel this, and I know what it cannot erase" — is giving his people something no amount of competent crisis management can provide.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer, writing from prison in 1943, observed that Christians are called to share in the suffering of God in the world, not to be delivered from it [cite — confirm specific letter]. The suffering is real. The sharing is what the community is for.

---

A specific word about the case where the crisis involves a moral failure by a leader — including, potentially, yourself.

This is the hardest case, and it is also among the most common. The sexual abuse by a trusted leader. The financial misconduct that went undetected for years. The pastor who kept secrets he should not have kept. These situations are not like the other crises, because they include a dimension of betrayal that has to be named directly before anything else can proceed.

The temptation in these cases is to frame the crisis primarily as an institutional problem requiring institutional response — bringing in outside counsel, issuing statements, following protocol. All of that may be necessary. None of it is sufficient. What the congregation requires in addition is a pastoral naming of what was done, not what was alleged, not what the investigation is considering, but what happened — with honesty and without euphemism.

The people in your congregation who were directly harmed require more than your institutional management. They require acknowledgment that what was done to them was wrong. Specifically. Clearly. Not hedged in the passive voice. If you are the pastor who failed, you already know what acknowledgment costs and why it matters. If you are the pastor cleaning up after someone else's failure, the cost is still real.

There is a reason the prophets did not use euphemism. Isaiah 1:15 does not say "there have been some areas of concern in our community's treatment of the vulnerable." It says, "your hands are full of blood." The directness is not cruelty. It is the precondition of repair. You cannot repent of what you have not named.

---

And when it passes, as it will, do the unglamorous work most pastors skip: tell the truth about what happened, name what you learned, and let the congregation see you metabolize it rather than bury it. A crisis handled in the open builds a kind of trust that years of smooth weeks cannot. A crisis buried becomes the thing the church does not talk about — and the things a church will not talk about are the things that quietly run it.

The witness of the early church, described in Acts, is not a witness of a community without conflict. It is a witness of a community that walked through conflict — the dispute about widows' provision in Acts 6, the crisis over circumcision and Gentile inclusion in Acts 15, the sharp disagreement between Paul and Barnabas over John Mark in Acts 15:36–41 — and came out the other side with its center intact and often with its capacity enlarged. The crisis did not defeat the church. The way through the crisis built something.

Eugene Peterson wrote that pastoral work is fundamentally about paying attention — to God, to the person in front of you, to what is actually happening as opposed to what you wish were happening [cite — confirm specific work]. A crisis demands that attentiveness at maximum. It is exhausting precisely because it demands presence, not management.

---

You did not choose the crisis. You only choose who you are inside it. The congregation you still have, on the other side, will have been formed less by your competence than by your character under fire.

The road did not reveal it. The cliff will.

What you have to carry, in a crisis, is heavier than any single decision. You are carrying their trust. You are carrying your own uncertainty. You are carrying the memory of who you were before you got tired. And you are carrying the conviction — if you can hold onto it — that the Lord who brought his people through Babylon and through Golgotha and through the worst decades of church history is not surprised by what you are facing.

He has led people through worse.

Be the kind of man you would want at the front of the room on the worst day. Let them see the grief and the footing. Let them see you listen before you speak. Let them see the long view under the immediate weight. That is leadership. Not perfect decisions. Not unbroken composure. A man who has not pretended it is smaller than it is, and has not pretended the Lord is smaller than He is.

Both things true. Both things held. That is the work.
