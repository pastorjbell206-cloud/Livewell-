In most American cities right now, the cost of housing is consuming a proportion of working-class income that previous generations would have considered a crisis. It is a crisis. The median rent in most major metropolitan areas has outpaced wage growth for two decades. [cite — confirm] Teachers, nurses, sanitation workers, the people the city requires to function, cannot afford to live in the city they sustain. They commute long distances. They share cramped apartments. They are one unexpected expense from losing the roof they have.

The church has largely treated this as someone else's problem.

When a family in your congregation cannot afford to live within thirty miles of your building, that is not a real estate problem. It is a justice problem, and justice problems have always been the church's business.

---

The Hebrew concept of shalom — the comprehensive flourishing that God intends for human life — has a spatial dimension that the church has been slow to emphasize. To dwell, in the Hebrew imagination, is to be placed. Stability of place is part of what it means to be a creature in a world God made for habitation. The promise to Abraham is a land. The promise to Israel in Deuteronomy is a land where each family will sit under its own vine and fig tree (Micah 4:4). The return from exile in Ezekiel is described as a resettlement, a rehousing, a restoration of each person to their place. The eschatological vision of Revelation is the New Jerusalem — the city, the dwelling of God with his people (Revelation 21:3). Homelessness in the Scripture is not merely physical deprivation. It is a condition that violates the deep structure of what it means to be a creature made to inhabit a world God called good.

The book of Nehemiah begins with a man weeping over broken walls. Walls and gates — the structures that make a city habitable, that provide protection and community — are the substance of his grief. When the walls are rebuilt, when the city is reorganized and the people are settled, the text treats this as an act of faithfulness to the covenant. The work is practical and theological at once. The physical and the spiritual are not separate categories in the biblical imagination, and a theology that treats them as separate will always end up deciding that the spiritual is what really matters while the physical is someone else's concern.

---

The modern housing crisis is not primarily a failure of individual ambition or personal responsibility. It is the downstream consequence of deliberate policy choices made over decades.

Single-family zoning, established in American cities largely during the mid-twentieth century, restricted the density of housing in desirable areas, limiting supply and driving prices up. In many cities these policies were explicitly designed to limit which populations had access to which neighborhoods — the connection between exclusionary zoning and racial segregation is well documented. [cite — confirm] The language of those policies has changed. The effect on who can afford to live where has proven durable.

At the same time, federal disinvestment in public housing and affordable housing subsidies beginning in the 1970s and accelerating through the 1980s left cities without the mechanisms that had previously housed working-class families. [cite — confirm] The mortgage interest deduction — the single largest federal housing subsidy — has overwhelmingly benefited homeowners rather than renters, wealthy households rather than poor ones. The system was not neutral. It was designed, through accumulated policy choices, to make housing an asset-building mechanism for those who already had assets, and a source of extraction for those who did not.

I am aware that this sounds like policy analysis, and the objection arrives quickly: the church should not be in the business of zoning advocacy or housing policy. But the prophets of Israel did not refrain from addressing the economic and legal structures that produced poverty among God's people. Micah condemned those who "covet fields and seize them, and houses, and take them away; they oppress a man and his house, a man and his inheritance" (Micah 2:2). The specific legal and economic mechanism by which the powerful appropriated housing from the vulnerable was precisely the prophetic concern. Not a side issue. The center.

The mechanism changes. The concern does not.

---

There are people in most congregations who know the housing crisis from the inside. Not as a policy debate but as a life.

The single mother who is working two jobs and still cannot make rent. The young couple who grew up in the neighborhood and cannot afford to stay in it. The senior member who bought a home in 1978 on a single income and watches her children unable to replicate what she had. The recently arrived family navigating the rental market without the credit history most landlords require. The family sleeping in a car. These are not statistical abstractions. They are in your pews, or they were before the commute made Sunday attendance impossible.

Their housing precarity shapes everything else. Mental health. Marital stress. Children's school stability and academic performance. Capacity to participate in the life of the congregation. When the church refuses to name the system that produced their precarity, it is asking them to bring their suffering into a space that has quietly agreed not to discuss its cause. That is a form of pastoral failure. It is also a failure of prophetic nerve.

The prophet Amos did not merely express sympathy for the people the merchants were cheating at the gates. He named the transaction. He named the price. He made explicit the connection between the economic behavior of the prosperous and the suffering of the poor. "Because you impose heavy rent on the poor and exact a tribute of grain from them, though you have built houses of well-hewn stone, you will not live in them; you have planted pleasant vineyards, but you will not drink their wine" (Amos 5:11). The welfare of the poor and the economic practices of the comfortable were not separate subjects. They were the same subject.

---

The theological question underneath all of this is: what is a home?

It is not, in the biblical imagination, primarily an investment vehicle. It is not a commodity to be held for appreciation or extracted for profit. It is a place — a specific location in the world where a person can be rooted, can be known, can participate in the life of a community over time. The theological vision of Leviticus and Deuteronomy treats the land not as private property in the modern sense but as a trust — something God owns and humans inhabit, always with accountability to the covenant community and to the God whose terms the community lives under.

The Jubilee laws of Leviticus 25 make this concrete: every fifty years, land reverts to its original allotment. The point is not communism. The point is that accumulated dispossession — the slow concentration of land and housing in the hands of the few through the ordinary mechanisms of debt and misfortune — was periodically interrupted by a divine requirement that the basic capacity for self-sufficient dwelling be restored to every family. The system had a structural correction built in. It acknowledged that markets, left to themselves, tend to produce dispossession. And it said that this outcome was theologically unacceptable.

We do not live under Jubilee law. But the theology behind it — that God's economic intent for human communities includes the basic ability to dwell, and that this intent creates obligations on the prosperous and on the community as a whole — does not require Jubilee law to remain active.

---

What can the church actually do?

Speaking is not sufficient, but speaking is where faithfulness begins — naming the crisis as a theological issue, not just a policy debate, making visible the faces of the people whose housing precarity is not an accident but a consequence.

The church has assets: land, buildings, institutional relationships, organized volunteer capacity, and presence in neighborhoods that makes it one of the few actors positioned to respond to the housing crisis at a local level with both prophetic voice and practical action. Congregations have built affordable housing on surplus land. [cite — confirm] Church coalitions have engaged city councils on zoning reform. Congregations have organized to prevent the displacement of long-term residents from gentrifying neighborhoods.

This work is not the whole gospel. But the gospel that does not touch the housing status of the family sleeping in their car in the parking lot is not the whole gospel either.

Isaiah 58:7 asks: "Is it not to share your bread with the hungry and bring the homeless poor into your house?" The house in that verse is not a metaphor. It is a dwelling. A roof. A place where a person is safe enough to sleep and known enough to be called by their name.

The church knows how to talk about homelessness as a spiritual crisis. It has been slower to talk about housing as a justice crisis rooted in policies we have the power to examine and the prophetic obligation to name. The two are the same crisis. We do not get to address the spiritual emergency while ignoring the physical one.

That kind of selective theology has a name. The prophets used it.

The word is not polite, and it is not quiet. But it is accurate.
