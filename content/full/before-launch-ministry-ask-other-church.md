Before you build the food pantry, ask who already runs one.

Before you launch the recovery program, ask which church in your city has been running one for a decade with trained staff and the community relationships to sustain it.

Before you hire a counselor and hang a shingle, ask whether the counseling center two miles away is already understaffed and under-funded and could do more with your partnership than your competition.

These are not romantic questions. They are stewardship questions. And they are questions that most pastors do not ask — because the church has absorbed the logic of the marketplace, where differentiation is survival and duplication is acceptable as long as your version is better than the other person's.

That logic is not wrong in a marketplace. It is destructive in the body of Christ.

---

The history of American Protestant ministry is, in significant part, a history of parallel infrastructure built by churches that did not speak to each other. The denominational divisions of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries produced not only theological separation but institutional multiplication: mission societies, hospitals, schools, orphanages, relief organizations, each with their own governance structure, their own donor base, their own executive director, their own claim to the resources and attention of the Christian public. Some of this multiplication reflected genuine theological conviction. Some of it reflected institutional ambition dressed in theological language. Much of it simply reflected the fact that the churches did not know each other and did not ask.

The twentieth century repeated the pattern in new forms. The evangelical parachurch explosion of the 1970s and 1980s — hundreds of organizations addressing overlapping populations with overlapping programs — produced a landscape of ministries where the primary competitive pressure was not fruitfulness but fundraising, and where organizations survived not because they were doing something distinctive but because they were doing something for which there was donor appetite. The waste was, and is, significant.

The local church has repeated the pattern at a smaller scale. Drive through any mid-sized American city and count the food pantries. Some of them are coordinated. Many of them are not — they are each the project of a local congregation that identified a need and responded, without asking what already existed, and they now operate at a fraction of the capacity they could have if the resources that fund four pantries funded one. The families being served are not complaining. They are grateful. But the waste is real, and in a world where the need is larger than the church's current capacity, waste is not neutral.

---

Paul's language in Ephesians 4:11-16 does not describe a collection of independent ministries competing for the same population. It describes a body that builds itself up in love, with every part working properly, each contributing to the growth of the whole. The language is of coordination, not competition. The knee does not say to the ankle, "I have developed a better walking program." The knee bends when the ankle requires it, because they are part of the same body and the body needs both of them doing what they are designed to do.

The ecclesiology that produces ministerial cooperation is not primarily strategic. It is theological. The church that regards itself as one expression of a larger body — the body that belongs to a Head with a larger mission than any individual congregation can execute — will make decisions about what to launch and what to join very differently than the church that regards itself as a complete and independent organism. The first will ask: where do I fit? The second will ask: what can I build?

Both questions have their place. But in the American church, the second question has crowded out the first to a degree that should alarm anyone who reads Ephesians 4 with attention.

---

I have launched ministries I should not have launched. I am certain of this. There were moments when the vision felt urgent and the need felt clear and the call felt genuine — and the call was genuine — but the honest question I did not ask was whether I was being called to start something new or to strengthen something that already existed. Those are not the same calling. The second one is harder, because it requires giving up the satisfactions of ownership and credit. But it may produce more fruit.

There is a specific pastoral pathology at work in the unnecessary launch. It looks like vision. It feels like calling. It is sometimes neither. It is the desire to build something that bears the marks of your particular gifts, your particular emphasis, your particular interpretation of what the community needs — and it is dressed in the language of discernment because dressing it otherwise would require an honesty that is uncomfortable. I have worn that dress. I recognize it.

The questions that unmask it are straightforward: If the ministry you are imagining launching already existed and was run by a church you have less regard for, would you join it? Would you send your people there? Would you write a check to support it? If the answer is no — if your vision is contingent on it being yours — then the call you are hearing may be more about you than about the need.

---

The more difficult question is what to do when the program that already exists in your city is being run by a church you disagree with theologically, or by a secular nonprofit with no gospel component, or by a denomination that operates differently than yours. This is where the stewardship question gets genuinely hard. And it is exactly where the answer matters most.

A food pantry run by a Catholic parish and a Baptist church together, with their theological differences fully acknowledged and their practical cooperation fully real, is a more honest picture of the kingdom than either of them running a separate pantry while their disagreements about soteriology keep their flour and their canned goods in different buildings. The person who cannot afford groceries does not need the theological disagreement about transubstantiation resolved before she can eat. She needs someone to give her food.

A recovery program run in partnership with a secular counseling center — where the church provides community and spiritual formation and the center provides clinical expertise — may serve the person in the grip of addiction better than the church's attempt to do both alone, without clinical training, with good intentions and inadequate tools. Partnership with the secular organization is not a theological compromise. It is an honest acknowledgment that the image of God shows up in competence, wherever competence is found, and that the person who needs help deserves the best help available, not the help your church can provide without asking for assistance.

This requires conversations that are awkward. It requires phone calls to pastors you do not know well. It requires walking into meetings where you are not in charge. It requires being the person who said, "Tell me what you are already doing and what you need, before I tell you what I am thinking of launching." Most pastors never make that call. It feels like weakness. It is not weakness. It is the posture of someone who actually believes the kingdom belongs to God rather than to his church's portfolio.

---

There is a practical discipline here that I commend to any pastor discerning whether to launch a new ministry. Call it the landscape conversation — not because the word fits, but because the practice does. Before you plan. Before you budget. Before you recruit your steering committee. Make three phone calls to people who are already working in the space you are considering entering. Ask them: what exists? What is working? What is under-resourced? What has been tried and failed and why? What would help most?

These calls will do one of two things. They will either confirm that there is a gap you are genuinely positioned to fill — a population not being served, a need not being met, an approach that has not been tried — in which case you launch with confidence and with the knowledge that others in the field see the gap too. Or they will reveal that the gap you identified is already being filled, imperfectly but really, by people who need partnership more than they need a new organization entering the field. In that case, the call you are discerning is not to launch. It is to strengthen.

Both callings are genuine. Both require faithfulness. The second one is less satisfying to the pastoral ego and more satisfying to God.

---

The Pastors Connection Network has been built around the conviction that the church is more effective when pastors regard each other as colleagues rather than competitors. That conviction applies not only to relationships between pastors but to the ministries those pastors build. The city that has twelve underfunded recovery programs could have one well-funded one. The city that has six food pantries that don't communicate could have one coordinated food security network. The city that has multiple counseling ministries serving overlapping populations could have a referral system that routes each person to the right resource.

None of this requires uniformity of theology or surrender of conviction. It requires the one thing the church has historically found most difficult: genuine humility about who owns the mission.

The mission belongs to God. The church is his instrument, not his manager. The ministry that launches because it couldn't bear to be second, or to serve under someone else's leadership, or to give up the satisfaction of having built the thing — that ministry has confused the instrument with the owner.

Start there. See what already exists. Ask what is needed.

Then decide whether the call to build is actually a call to join.

The fruit of the second kind of faithfulness tends to be quieter. It does not have a ribbon-cutting ceremony. It does not produce a founder's story. But the person whose life it changed — the one who found the program that already existed because you funded it instead of duplicating it, who got the referral that worked because you made the call instead of reinventing the wheel — that person received something real.

That is what the body of Christ is for.
