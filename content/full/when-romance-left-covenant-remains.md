There is a season in a marriage when you look at your spouse across the table and the feeling you have is not warm. Not hostile — just neutral. The chemistry that was once effortless now requires effort, and effort applied to romance feels like a contradiction in terms. The feeling that used to arrive uninvited no longer arrives at all.

This is the season most couples never discuss with anyone. It feels like a verdict on the marriage, and they are afraid of what that verdict is. So they perform contentment in public and carry the quiet flatness in private, waiting for something to change or for the reason they are still doing this to become clear again.

The question that lives in this season is a real one: Is a marriage without romance still a marriage worth keeping? And the answer depends entirely on what you think romance is for.

## What the Culture Taught Us to Expect

The modern assumption is that romance is the marriage — that the covenant is the container for the feeling, and when the feeling goes, the container has lost its reason to exist. This is not a Christian idea. It is a Romantic-era idea — a product of the late eighteenth century, crystallized by Rousseau and Goethe and their literary descendants — with enough cultural saturation that most people cannot see it as an idea at all. It just feels true.

The Romantic movement placed the individual's inner experience at the center of moral authority. Feeling became the measure of the real. A love that felt powerful was genuine; a love that did not feel powerful was counterfeit or dead. Marriage entered this framework as the permanent institutional expression of a feeling that was, by its nature, impermanent — which meant that marriage as an institution was set up to fail from the moment the Romantic framework defined what success required.

We are still inside this story. The divorce rate is not primarily a product of immoral people making selfish choices. It is primarily a product of people who genuinely believe what their culture has taught them: that the feeling is the marriage, and that when the feeling fades, the marriage has ended, even if the legal arrangement has not caught up yet.

## What the Scripture Actually Offers

The Hebrew of Song of Solomon uses a word — *dodi*, my beloved — that carries both sensory delight and covenantal commitment woven together. The erotic poetry of the text is not the church's embarrassment. It is its argument: that physical and spiritual love are not opposed, that embodied delight is part of what covenant is for. But even there — in the most unambiguously romantic text in the canon — the love is particular, directed toward a specific person, shaped by a specific history. "His left hand is under my head, and his right hand embraces me" (Song of Solomon 2:6). This is not a song about the experience of romance in general. It is a song about a specific person in the presence of a specific other.

The covenantal logic of the Hebrew Scriptures runs counter to the Romantic logic at every point. Ruth's declaration to Naomi in Ruth 1:16 — "where you go I will go, and where you stay I will stay" — is not a description of feeling. It is a declaration of will, made in the teeth of circumstances that warranted departure. The covenant holds precisely when the feeling does not compel it.

In the New Testament, Paul's instruction to husbands in Ephesians 5:25 — "love your wives as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" — does not appeal to romantic feeling as the motivation for marital love. It appeals to a pattern of costly self-giving that is independent of emotional state. Christ did not go to the cross because it felt natural. He went because he was committed. That commitment is the model for marital love, and it is a commitment that survives the seasons in which feeling does not arrive uninvited.

The question is not whether romance dies. It does. The question is whether what comes after it is worth staying for.

## A Steel-Manned Counter-Position

Before I answer that question too quickly, the counter-position deserves its full weight. There is a version of covenant-theology that has functioned as a trap rather than a gift — that has told people (often women, disproportionately) to stay in marriages that were not merely romantically flat but actively harmful, citing covenant obligation as the reason. The theological argument for the permanence of marriage has been used to cover abuse, to suppress legitimate need, and to insist on the form of covenant fidelity while the substance of it was long gone.

I am not arguing for that. Covenant fidelity is not the same as the obligation to remain in a dangerous situation. The question I am addressing is not "should I stay in a marriage that is harming me" but "should I stay in a marriage where the romance has faded and I am not sure it will return." These are not the same question, and conflating them has done enormous damage.

The covenant argument for staying is a powerful one. But it is not an argument for staying at any cost, under any conditions, regardless of what staying requires of the one who stays. It is an argument that the presence or absence of romantic feeling is not, by itself, an adequate reason to leave a marriage that is otherwise intact. That is a narrower claim, and it is the one worth defending.

## The Covenant That Holds When Feeling Fades

A covenant is not a feeling. It is a commitment made in the presence of witnesses and God. It is a promise to remain even when the promise becomes hard, even when the other person disappoints you, even when you disappoint yourself.

The modern marriage culture has inverted this. We treat the feeling as the covenant and the covenant as the feeling. We ask marriage to do what only romance can do — produce the constant sense of being chosen, delighted in, pursued. And when marriage cannot do that (because it is not marriage's job), we conclude that the marriage has failed.

A marriage that lasts long enough will move through seasons. The season of effortless attraction gives way to the season of chosen commitment. The season of discovery gives way to the season of depth. The season of romance gives way to the season of covenant.

This is not a tragedy. This is maturity. And maturity has its own beauty, though it is not the beauty the Romantic tradition taught us to want.

## A Concrete Scene

I know a couple who have been married for thirty-one years. In their eighteenth year, they reached a period that lasted nearly two years in which neither of them would have described what they felt as love — not in the way the word had meant at the beginning. They were exhausted by children and debt and a period of ministry that had taken more than it gave. They were civil. They were functional. They were not intimate in any of the ways that matter.

They did not leave. They did not perform happiness they did not feel. They stayed, and they stayed honestly — which meant they told each other the truth about where they were, which was harder than leaving would have been. The honesty was the turning point. Not a dramatic revival of romantic feeling. Just two people deciding that the covenant was a reason to keep returning, even when returning did not feel like anything.

The woman told me: "I stopped waiting for the feeling to justify the commitment. I let the commitment carry us until the feeling came back. And it did come back. But it came back as something different. Deeper, I think. Less exciting and more true."

## What Happens When You Stop Waiting for the Feeling to Return

The couples who move through this season well do something counterintuitive: they stop waiting for the feeling to return and start building something else. They invest in the marriage not because they feel like it, but because they committed to it. They choose each other daily, not because the choice is easy, but because the alternative — the dissolution of something sacred — is worse.

They discover that commitment, lived out consistently over time, produces something the initial romance never could: a deep, textured, resilient love that has survived disappointment and chosen to stay anyway. This is the love that can hold you when everything else falls apart. This is the love that knows you — not the version of you that you present on good days, but the actual you, with all your failures and fears and contradictions — and chooses you anyway.

The theologian Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote in a letter from prison that it is not your love that sustains your marriage but the marriage that sustains your love [cite — confirm]. He was onto something. The structure holds the content until the content can stand on its own. The covenant provides the container in which the deeper love — the one that is less exciting and more true — is slowly formed.

## Three Questions for This Week

What did you think romance was for when you got married? What do you think it is for now?

If the feeling never returns to what it was, would you stay? What would that require of you?

What is one way you could choose your spouse this week — not because you feel like it, but because you committed to?

Tell your spouse one specific thing you appreciate about them that has nothing to do with romance — something you have discovered about their character, their faithfulness, their presence in your life.

## The Weight of What Remains

The couples who last are not the ones for whom romance never fades. They are the ones who discover that something deeper than romance can grow in its place. They are the ones who learn that covenant, kept faithfully through the seasons, becomes its own kind of beauty — less like a fire, more like a foundation, the kind of thing you do not notice until everything else is shaking and it does not move.

The flatness in your marriage right now may not be a verdict. It may be an invitation. An invitation to stop relying on the feeling to carry the commitment, and to let the commitment carry you until the feeling finds its new form.

Not romance. Something better. Something that stays.
