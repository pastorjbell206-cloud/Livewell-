There is a moment in the drift — and every pastor who has watched it happen knows it — when the church stops being recognizable by its confession and starts being recognizable by its political identity. The drift is rarely dramatic. It happens in sermon illustrations that only land for one kind of voter. It happens in the unspoken assumptions about which cultural enemies are being addressed from the pulpit. It happens in the candidates who get applause at church events and the ones who do not. And then one day someone from outside the congregation visits on a Sunday morning and walks away knowing exactly which party the church voted for, but uncertain whether they heard the Gospel.

That is the moment the church has become a political brand. And by the time most pastors recognize it, the congregation has organized a significant portion of its identity around the association.

---

This is not a new problem. It has an old name and a long history.

The term for it, in the history of political theology, is Constantinianism — the arrangement, first formalized in the fourth century under the Roman Emperor Constantine, in which the church's institutional interests and the empire's political interests become so entangled that each begins to function as a legitimating structure for the other [cite — confirm]. The church gives the empire theological sanction. The empire gives the church institutional protection and social prestige. The arrangement is mutually beneficial for both institutions and catastrophic for the church's prophetic integrity.

John Howard Yoder spent much of his career arguing that the Constantinian settlement was the most consequential and most underexamined event in the history of Western Christianity [cite — confirm]. His argument was not primarily that political engagement is wrong. It was that the specific exchange of prophetic independence for institutional favor produces a church that is no longer capable of speaking against power, because it has become dependent on power for its own survival and status.

Yoder's concern materializes in specific, visible ways in American Christianity. The pastor whose church is closely identified with a political party cannot effectively challenge that party's failures. The congregation whose identity is partly organized around political loyalty will hear a prophetic word against that loyalty as an attack on the community's identity rather than as a call to deeper faithfulness. The church that needs the political arrangement in place to sustain its cultural influence has made a trade it did not fully examine at the time, and it is now living in the downstream consequences.

---

I have seen this from inside. I have felt the pull of it myself — the way a congregation's agreement can be generated more reliably by touching political anxieties than by opening the Scripture, the way certain phrases create immediate warmth in a room that the Gospel alone does not always produce. That pull is a form of temptation, and like most temptations, it offers something real: unity, energy, clarity about who is inside and who is outside. What it does not offer is the thing it costs.

What it costs is the church's ability to speak to anyone outside its political tribe. The prophetic voice is always addressed to the whole people, and it is always capable of reaching across political lines — because the prophets were not targeting one party's sins. Amos condemned the wealthy in Israel without regard for their political affiliation. Isaiah indicted the religious establishment regardless of which faction it belonged to. Jesus managed to offend the Pharisees and the Sadducees in the same week. That breadth is the mark of a genuinely prophetic ministry. The church that can only speak to its own political friends has lost the range.

The letters to the seven churches in Revelation 2–3 were written to communities experiencing a very specific form of this temptation. The Roman imperial cult was not simply a political preference. It was an identity claim, a loyalty demand, a social pressure that organized economic and civic life around demonstrated allegiance to Caesar as Lord. The churches at Pergamum and Thyatira were accommodating to this demand — allowing the language and rituals of imperial loyalty to coexist with the confession of Christ's lordship (Revelation 2:14-15, 2:20). The risen Christ's word to them is direct and unsparing: you have compromised the confession. The two lordships cannot coexist.

The American church's temptation is structurally similar. Not identical — the coercion is not the same, and the cost of resistance is not the same. But the mechanism is the same: a worldly authority making a loyalty demand that the church, in exchange for cultural favor and institutional protection, is being gradually absorbed into.

---

The way back is not neutrality. Neutrality is its own form of failure — the decision to say nothing consequential about anything that matters. The way back is theological clarity about what the Gospel actually claims and the willingness to let those claims land with equal force across the political spectrum.

This requires something specific from the preacher. It requires preaching texts that discomfort the right and texts that discomfort the left, in the same calendar year, without softening either. It requires naming justice concerns that the progressive wing cares about and moral concerns the conservative wing cares about — not to achieve balance for its own sake, but because the canon of Scripture contains both, and selective preaching produces a selective congregation. It requires being willing to lose the applause of people who came to have their political instincts baptized rather than their lives transformed.

This is more costly than it sounds. The congregation that has organized part of its identity around political loyalty does not receive prophetic challenge to that loyalty as spiritual growth. It receives it as betrayal. The pastor who preaches it will face the same response that Amaziah gave Amos: "Go, prophecy somewhere else." Some people will leave. Some people will campaign against the pastor to people who stayed. Some people will make the pastor feel, for a season, as though the ministry is unraveling.

That is the cost. It is not optional. There is no path back from political brand status that does not pass through the willingness to be misread, mischaracterized, and temporarily depleted.

---

Practically, the path back from a political brand identity begins with a season of deliberate theological grounding. Not a sermon series called "Let's Talk About Politics" — that is guaranteed to generate more heat than light. A sustained engagement with texts that accomplish the reorientation of primary loyalty. The Sermon on the Mount. The prophetic literature. The early chapters of Revelation, which were written to a church being pressured to conflate faithfulness with imperial loyalty. These texts were written to situations not unlike ours, and they produce the same disorientation: you thought your political category was substituting for your theological category, and you were wrong.

This disorientation is the beginning of recovery. The congregation that encounters the Beatitudes seriously — "Blessed are the peacemakers," "Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness" (Matthew 5:9, 6) — is being asked to organize its imagination around categories that do not map cleanly onto any partisan platform. The congregation that sits with Isaiah 58 and hears the specific list of things that constitute genuine fasting — loosing the bonds of wickedness, letting the oppressed go free, sharing bread with the hungry — is being asked to locate its justice commitments in the text rather than in the party platform.

That reorientation does not produce political unanimity. It should not. The congregation that comes through this disorientation will not agree on every policy question. But it will share something more durable than political agreement: the conviction that its primary citizenship is elsewhere (Philippians 3:20), and that what it owes the world is the Gospel — not the Gospel as one party's mascot, but the Gospel as an announcement that destabilizes all earthly powers equally.

---

There is a long tradition within Christianity of maintaining precisely this prophetic distance. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, writing from within a regime that was explicitly demanding the church's political loyalty, drew the distinction between the church's two roles: counseling the state on justice, and sometimes being obligated to jam the wheel that is crushing the innocent — but always from a position that did not confuse the church's mission with the state's agenda [cite — confirm]. The church has a word for every political power. It does not have a loyalty to any of them.

That tradition is worth recovering not because it produces a politically disengaged church — it does not; Bonhoeffer's own life makes clear that the tradition produces extraordinary engagement — but because it produces a church whose engagement is governed by the Gospel rather than by the political alliance.

The congregation that is known in its city primarily as a political bloc has lost its distinctiveness in the wrong direction. The congregation that is known as a community of people whose common life demonstrates something that neither party can produce — genuine forgiveness, genuine solidarity across class and race, genuine care for the weak and the strange — that congregation is the political witness the world most needs. Not a party. An alternative.

That alternative is worth recovering. It always has been.

The question is whether the pastor is willing to pay the price of the recovery.

That is not a rhetorical question. It is a pastoral one.

I am asking myself.
