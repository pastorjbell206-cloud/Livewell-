The consistent use of success stories as sermon illustrations — this is how someone applied the principle and it worked out beautifully — creates a subtle but significant distortion in the congregation's understanding of faith. It implies that faithful application of biblical truth reliably produces good outcomes in visible, timely ways. This is not the testimony of Scripture, and it is not the experience of most people in the room.

The congregation member who is applying the principles faithfully and not seeing the promised results begins to wonder what is wrong with them, rather than questioning the implied framework. That is the formation damage. Not that they doubted God, but that they doubted themselves — and the doubt was generated not by the gospel but by a preaching habit that dressed something other than the gospel in the gospel's clothes.

---

The success story is a particular kind of illustration, and its appeal is entirely understandable.

It is emotionally satisfying. It creates a clean arc — problem, application of biblical principle, resolution — that mirrors the story structure human beings find most intuitive. It leaves the congregation with something concrete: evidence that the thing works, that this is not abstract theology but practical wisdom with verifiable results. Testimonials are the oldest marketing tool in the human repertoire, and they work for the same reason in the pulpit that they work in commerce — they make the invisible visible, they translate the general into the particular, they give the listener a human proxy for the outcome they hope to experience.

And there are real testimonies to tell. People's lives are changed by the gospel. Marriages are restored. Addictions are broken. The frightened person finds courage, and it is real courage, and the source of it is traceable to an encounter with the living God. Those stories are true and they should be told. The problem is not the success story. The problem is the *only* success story — the sermon that illustrates every principle with the version that worked out, and never with the version that did not.

---

Because most of the versions in Scripture did not work out in the ways we would call success.

Abel offered an acceptable sacrifice and was murdered (Genesis 4:8). Joseph did the right thing with Potiphar's wife and spent years in prison (Genesis 39:20). Jeremiah preached faithfully for forty years [cite — confirm approximate length of Jeremiah's ministry] and was rejected, imprisoned in a cistern, and watched his nation be destroyed. He did not see the restoration he prophesied in his lifetime. The success story of Jeremiah is invisible from inside his biography — it is only visible if you zoom out far enough, past his death, past the exile, to a return he did not live to see.

Job applied the principles of righteous living more faithfully than anyone in his world (Job 1:1) and lost everything. His comforters — Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar — ran the success story logic in reverse: since Job was suffering, he must have sinned; since God blesses the faithful, the unfaithful must be suffering. They were wrong. God says so explicitly (Job 42:7). The prosperity framework disguised as orthodoxy is as old as the book of Job, and God's verdict on it has not changed.

Stephen preached the most theologically precise sermon in the book of Acts and was stoned to death immediately afterward (Acts 7:54-60). Paul's missionary career, by any external measure, was a series of beatings, imprisonments, shipwrecks, and rejections, punctuated by moments of genuine fruit, and ending in execution. The success story of Paul requires the same long zoom as Jeremiah — visible only if you consider the churches that outlasted him, the letters that shaped two thousand years of theology, the faith of the millions who came after. None of that was visible from the prison cell in Rome.

This is the testimony of Scripture. Not that faithfulness is punished, but that it is often costly, that the results are often not what were hoped for in the visible near term, that the arc of faithfulness runs through loss in ways that cannot be explained by the success framework and should not be. The success story illustration systematically filters this out.

---

A concrete pastoral scene:

A man in the congregation loses his son to addiction. He was a faithful father — present, praying, consistent in his faith and his love and his persistence. He did not apply the principles incorrectly. He applied them fully, for years, and his son died anyway. He comes back to church after the funeral, and the sermon that Sunday illustrates faithfulness with a story about a prodigal son who came home.

That story is in the Bible. The man knows it. But the version he lived is the one that does not get told from the pulpit — the version where the father ran out every day to the horizon and the son never appeared. That version is also real. It is the experience of a significant portion of the people in any given congregation on any given Sunday. And the preacher who has not trained themselves to speak to that version — who has built the illustrative vocabulary almost entirely out of the stories where it worked — has no language for that man.

He leaves the service knowing the gospel is supposed to be for him, but feeling, in the bones of the experience, that the gospel as it is being preached is not quite reaching his actual situation. The gap is not between him and God. It is between his actual life and the version of life the preaching has consistently assumed.

---

Include the laments. Include the failures. Include the stories where the faithful thing was done and the outcome was not what was hoped for.

That is not pessimism. That is what the psalms of lament are for — and they constitute roughly a third of the Psalter. The lament psalms are not psalms of failure; they are psalms of brutal honesty about the distance between promise and experience, offered in the confidence that the God who made the promise can be addressed from inside the gap. Psalm 44 accuses God directly: "You have rejected and disgraced us and have not gone out with our armies" (Psalm 44:9). Psalm 88 is the only lament that does not end in praise — it ends in darkness, the last word *darkness*, and it is in the canon because it is true to human experience and the canon does not flinch from human experience.

The preacher who incorporates lament into the illustrative vocabulary is doing several things at once. They are being honest about reality. They are giving the suffering congregant the word they need to address God from where they actually are. They are building a theology of suffering that holds up under actual suffering — not the thin framework that collapses the first time the principle is applied faithfully and the outcome does not come. And they are demonstrating, by example, that it is possible to be honest with God and remain in relationship with him, which is one of the most important things a congregation can learn.

---

The counter-position has real weight.

The success story is not only emotionally satisfying — it is apologetically useful. In a culture of skepticism, concrete evidence that the faith produces genuine change in actual human lives is a form of argument. The testimony of the changed life is a witness. Testimonials function in the sermon as a kind of inductive argument: here is evidence, judge for yourself. A preaching practice that abandons them entirely loses something real.

And some congregants genuinely need to hear that change is possible — the person who has been in despair for so long that hope itself feels like a lie. They need the testimony that says: it happened. This is not theoretical. For this specific person, in this specific way, the gospel changed things. That witness is not manipulative. It is the church's oldest form of communication.

The question is not whether to use success stories. It is whether to use them exclusively, and whether the exclusive use is training a theological imagination that cannot hold complexity — that cannot remain faithful when the story does not end in visible success.

The sermon that includes both the testimony of transformation *and* the testimony of faithfulness under persistent difficulty, that holds the success story alongside the lament, that trusts the congregation to carry the complexity — that is the sermon that forms the kind of people who can actually hold their faith when life does what life does.

---

Most congregations contain, in any given week, people in both conditions simultaneously. The woman in the front row whose marriage was restored and the man in the back whose was not. The teenager whose struggle with faith became a deeper faith and the parent whose struggle became a quiet exit. The person who prayed and was healed and the person who prayed and was not.

The preacher's job is not to resolve that tension by choosing which story to tell. The preacher's job is to speak truthfully into the whole room — to have a theological vocabulary large enough to address the full range of human experience that is present in the pews. That vocabulary is built from Scripture's full range of testimony: the psalms of praise and the psalms of lament, the testimonies of deliverance and the testimonies of faithfulness in the absence of deliverance, the stories that end in triumph and the stories that end in death and the stories that end with God saying *my grace is sufficient for you* to someone who asked three times for a different answer (2 Corinthians 12:9).

That last story is Paul's. It does not fit the success story format. It is one of the most important things he wrote. It has formed more people in genuine faith than almost anything else in the epistles, precisely because it tells the truth about what God sometimes says to the person who is applying the principles faithfully and not getting the resolution they asked for.

Include that story. Include the laments. Include the failures. That is where most of the congregation actually lives, and they need to know the gospel has something to say there — not as a consolation prize for people who did not get the real thing, but as the deep thing itself, the word that is sufficient precisely because it does not require circumstances to cooperate.

That is the gospel that holds. The preacher who learns to preach it from the full testimony of Scripture is building a congregation that cannot be shaken by the fact that life is hard — because the faith they have been given was formed in hard places, and it was found sufficient there.
