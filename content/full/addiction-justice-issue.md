The language came first: addict, junkie, crackhead, drunk. The language arrived decades before the neuroscience did, and decades before the policy followed, and it has outlasted both. It is worth asking what that language was designed to do, because language is never innocent — it always sorts people into categories of deserving and undeserving, and the language of addiction has always sorted them toward undeserving. The addict is weak. Morally compromised. Responsible for the thing that is destroying him. The proper response to weakness and moral compromise in a culture organized around individual responsibility is not compassion. It is consequence.

The church absorbed this framework without much examination. It mapped moral failure onto the addiction narrative, preached about the slavery of sin and the freedom that Christ offers, offered recovery programs organized around twelve steps that have real power to change lives, and largely failed to ask whether the people in the pews came to addiction the same way that people in different zip codes did. The individual moral framework is not wrong. It is incomplete. And the church that offers only the incomplete framework to people whose addiction was shaped by forces larger than individual choice is offering a gospel that cannot reach the whole person.

---

The science took approximately forty years to make its way into public consciousness and has still not fully arrived.

The foundational work of researchers like Dr. Nora Volkow [cite — confirm] and the growing body of evidence on addiction neurology established, with increasing clarity, that addiction produces measurable, persistent changes in brain structure and function — particularly in the prefrontal cortex and the dopamine reward systems. The person in active addiction is not simply making bad choices in the way that choosing the wrong career is a bad choice. He is making choices under conditions of neurological compromise that alter his capacity for exactly the kind of rational deliberate decision-making that the moral failure framework assumes is fully available to him. This is not an argument for removing human agency from the picture. Choices matter. Change is possible. Recovery is real. But the claim that addiction is primarily a moral failure rather than a complex neurobiological condition is now, in the scientific literature, simply not defensible [cite — confirm].

More important for the church's justice analysis than the neuroscience is the epidemiology. Because addiction does not distribute randomly through the population.

The opioid crisis that devastated rural and post-industrial communities in the late 1990s and through the 2010s was, among other things, a story about the systematic pharmaceutical industry targeting of communities with high rates of workplace injury, economic decline, and social despair. Purdue Pharma's aggressive marketing of OxyContin was not directed at wealthy suburban communities — it was directed at places where people did hard physical labor and were hurting and where the medical system had limited resources to treat pain in ways other than prescription [cite — confirm]. The addiction epidemic in those communities was, in significant part, a manufactured crisis — manufactured by companies that had calculated how many people would become dependent, had done that calculation, and had chosen market share. The people who became addicted were not, in the first instance, morally weaker than anyone else. They were the targets.

The crack cocaine epidemic of the 1980s has a similar structural story. The same substance — cocaine — was treated as a criminal matter when it appeared in rock form in Black urban communities (mandatory minimums, mass incarceration, the weight of the 1986 Anti-Drug Abuse Act) and as a public health matter when it appeared as powder in white communities [cite — confirm]. Not because the pharmacology was different. The pharmacology was nearly identical. Because the communities were different. The criminal justice response to addiction has never been racially neutral, and its failure to produce recovery has been consistent across four decades of evidence. What the incarceration of addicts produces is not sobriety. It is the disruption of whatever social support exists, the addition of a criminal record that makes employment nearly impossible, and the perpetuation of the conditions that made addiction appealing in the first place.

The prophet Ezekiel, describing Israel's failure toward the poor and the vulnerable, names among the city's sins that "her priests do violence to my law and profane my holy things; they do not distinguish between the holy and the common; they teach that there is no difference between the unclean and the clean; and they shut their eyes to the keeping of my Sabbaths, so that I am profaned among them" (Ezekiel 22:26). The violence done to the law, in Ezekiel's analysis, includes the failure to distinguish — the refusal to see clearly what the situation actually is and therefore to respond to it appropriately. The priests closed their eyes. The failure of perception became the failure of justice.

The church's closure of its eyes to the systemic dimensions of addiction has produced a similar failure of response. We see the individual in front of us — struggling, broken, needing Christ. We preach to that individual. We pray for that individual. We hold a recovery group in the fellowship hall on Wednesday nights, and sometimes those meetings change lives, and I will not speak a word against them. But we do not often preach about the pharmaceutical company that targeted the community where that individual lives. We do not often preach about the policy choices that eliminated manufacturing jobs and left whole regions with nothing but despair and a prescription pad. We do not often preach about the racial double standard in criminal justice that makes addiction a medical problem for some communities and a felony for others.

That silence is not neutrality. It is a choice.

James writes in chapter 5 with an urgency that most congregations have smoothed over: "Now listen, you rich people, weep and wail because of the misery that is coming on you. Your wealth has rotted, and moths have eaten your clothes. Your gold and silver are corroded. Their corrosion will testify against you and eat your flesh like fire. You have hoarded wealth in the last days. Look! The wages you failed to pay the workers who mowed your fields are crying out against you" (James 5:1-4). The wages not paid. The fields mowed by people who did not receive what their labor was worth. The wealth extracted from communities that had nothing left after it was gone.

This is the story of addiction in post-industrial America. The wealth was extracted. The communities were left with nothing. The despair that follows the loss of meaningful work and economic dignity is not metaphorical — it is measured, documented, and traceable in the mortality statistics of the communities most affected. Princeton economists Anne Case and Angus Deaton [cite — confirm] identified a wave of what they called "deaths of despair" — deaths from suicide, alcohol, and drug overdose — concentrated in working-class white communities that had lost their economic footing. The correlation between economic displacement and substance abuse mortality is not incidental. It is the story James is telling.

---

The counter-position worth taking seriously is this: the systemic analysis risks removing moral agency from people who need moral agency to recover. Recovery is possible. People do overcome addiction. They do it through spiritual transformation, through community, through the kind of radical personal reorientation that the twelve-step tradition calls surrender and that the Christian tradition calls metanoia. If we tell people they are victims of systems, do we not rob them of the agency that recovery requires?

This is a real concern. It is also a false choice.

Naming the systemic causes of addiction does not remove personal agency. It gives that agency a clearer picture of the field it is working in. The person recovering from addiction does not recover less because she understands that her addiction was shaped by economic despair, by a pharmaceutical company's calculated predation, by a policy environment that punished rather than treated. She recovers more, because she understands that her struggle was not simply personal moral failure — it was also the result of forces arrayed against her — and understanding that can reduce the shame that is one of addiction's most powerful allies.

Shame keeps people sick. Clarity helps them recover. The church that insists on the moral failure framework because it thinks it preserves agency is often, in practice, preserving the shame that prevents recovery.

---

What does it look like for the church to treat addiction as a justice issue without abandoning the pastoral and spiritual dimensions of its response?

It looks like recovery communities that carry both the twelve-step honesty about powerlessness and the prophetic honesty about systems. It looks like congregations that are vocal — from the pulpit, in public life — about pharmaceutical company accountability, about the racial disparities in criminal justice responses to addiction, about the economic conditions that create the soil in which addiction takes root. It looks like the church refusing to accept the arrangement where the wealthy receive treatment and the poor receive incarceration for the same condition.

It looks like pastors who know the difference between a pastoral crisis and a justice crisis and who understand that the two are rarely entirely separate.

The man in your congregation who is fighting opioid addiction is not only in a pastoral crisis. He is also in a justice situation. The forces that targeted his community, extracted its economic vitality, and left the pharmacy as the most accessible form of relief are real forces, and the gospel that addresses only his soul while remaining silent about those forces is a diminished gospel — not wrong, but too small for the weight of what he is carrying.

He does not need to hear only that his sin is forgiven. He needs to hear that God sees what was done to him, that the prophet's voice is not only for his interior life, and that the community that gathers around the table also gathers around the question of how he got here.

The cry of the wounded reaches God. In the opioid crisis and the crack epidemic and in every wave of addiction that has moved through the bodies of the poor, the cry reaches God.

The question is whether it reaches the church.
