The church has a remarkably consistent posture toward gun violence in America: wait, grieve, post, move on.

We wait for the news cycle to do its work. We grieve with appropriate pastoral warmth. We post something about prayer — the genuine kind, not the performative kind, we are careful to say. And then we move on, because what else is there to do? The question is political. Ministry is not political. Our job is to love people through grief, not to wade into policy arguments that divide the congregation.

I understand that logic. I have used it. And I have come to believe that it is, in most of its applications, a failure of prophetic nerve dressed in the clothes of pastoral wisdom.

---

Begin at the level of scale. The United States experiences gun deaths at a rate that has no comparable peer among wealthy nations. [cite — confirm] This is not a disputed fact. It is a matter of international public health data. The causes are genuinely complex — firearm availability, mental health systems, urban poverty, cultural mythology about self-reliance and armed resistance. The policy solutions are contested, some more effectively than others, and the church does not have clean expertise in every dimension.

But the church has Scripture. And Scripture has something very specific to say about the deaths of the poor, the deaths of children, the deaths that result from the failure of communities to protect the most vulnerable.

Proverbs 24:11-12 is direct: "Rescue those who are being taken away to death; hold back those who are stumbling to the slaughter. If you say, 'Behold, we did not know this,' does not he who weighs the heart perceive it? Does not he who keeps watch over your soul know it, and will he not repay man according to his work?" The knowing is the accountability. Once you know that people are dying preventably, the profession of helplessness becomes a moral position. It is the position of the man who drove past Lazarus at the gate every day. He knew. He chose not to interrupt his life with the knowledge.

---

The history of the church's relationship to violence in America is not simple, and it deserves honest examination.

The Black church in America has been confronting gun violence in its communities for generations — not as an abstract policy debate but as the specific question of which funerals they were conducting and why. The prophetic tradition that produced civil rights preaching was formed in communities where violence was not a statistical abstraction but a Tuesday. The white evangelical church's arrival at the "gun violence conversation" in the early twenty-first century, largely prompted by mass shootings that affected predominantly white spaces, came with an unfamiliarity that the Black church could not share. The pastors of Watts and the South Side of Chicago had been preaching funerals for decades while suburban churches waited for the issue to become "really serious."

That asymmetry is itself a theological datum. It tells us something about who we have trained ourselves to see, whose deaths register as an emergency, whose suffering triggers the prophetic response. When Walter Brueggemann describes the prophetic imagination as the capacity to "speak against the numbness" of dominant culture, he is describing exactly this: the numbing that allows some deaths to recede into acceptable background noise while others mobilize the national conversation. [cite — confirm] The church that responds loudly to the mass shooting in a school and silently to the chronic violence in its neighboring city has not made a neutral judgment. It has made a theological one.

---

The counterargument comes in two forms, both worth taking seriously.

The first says: gun policy is genuinely contested, and reasonable people can disagree about what policies would most reduce gun deaths. Therefore the church should not speak specifically on policy. There is something true in this. The church is not a policy research institution, and its authority in the area of policy specifics is limited. The church does not have clean answers about which combination of background check requirements, red flag laws, waiting periods, and other interventions would produce what effects. The policy space is genuinely complex.

But this concession applies narrowly. It applies to the specific policy debate. It does not apply to the moral claim that gun violence is a justice issue — that the disproportionate deaths of poor people, of people of color, of children in particular circumstances are an indictment of a society's failure to protect the most vulnerable. On that the church has spoken for three thousand years. You do not need a policy position to state the moral claim. The prophets made moral claims without policy platforms. They named the injustice, named its human cost, named the moral failure of the community that permitted it, and left the specific institutional response to those whose institutional role it was to produce.

The second counterargument says: the church's role is the gospel, and the gospel is about spiritual transformation, not political advocacy. This is the more theologically serious objection, because it raises the question of what the gospel actually is and what its scope includes.

The answer the Gospels give is the answer Jesus gives to John the Baptist in Matthew 11:4-5: "Go and tell John what you hear and see: the blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, and the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them." The good news is not separable from its material consequences. The kingdom that Jesus announces is the kingdom in which human bodies are healed and human lives are restored. A church that limits its gospel to "spiritual" outcomes while treating the deaths of the poor as outside its remit has not found a biblical theology of the gospel. It has found a gnostic one.

---

There are specific things the church can actually say and do that do not require a particular legislative position.

The church can name the deaths. Not in the abstract — "gun violence claims tens of thousands of lives" — but specifically, with the pastoral specificity it uses for every other death. The family in your congregation who lost their child. The neighborhood adjacent to your building where the statistics are personal. The names.

The church can speak theologically about the culture of violence — the narrative of the armed individual as the basic unit of self-defense, the mythology that safety is primarily a matter of personal armament. This is not a partisan claim. It is a theological claim about where human security comes from, about the limits of individual self-sufficiency as a moral framework, about the communal structures that God intends as protection for the vulnerable. The theology of shalom is explicitly communal. The Lord's Prayer asks for protection for "us," not "me."

The church can refuse to allow its sanctuaries to be co-opted by any political movement's claim to own the question. The peculiar American situation in which gun rights have been treated as a quasi-theological position by portions of the evangelical community — in which the right to bear arms has been discussed in some contexts as a God-given right with religious overtones — is not a position the church as such has a stake in defending. It is a political position, and a relatively recent one, and the church's prophetic independence requires it to hold that distinction.

---

The pastoral reality is this: in most congregations, there are people who have lost someone to gun violence and people who are deeply committed to the constitutional framework around firearms. Both are in the room. The pastor who says nothing on this topic because it might divide them has not achieved peace. He has achieved a church that does not speak to reality, which is not peace. It is managed silence.

The prophetic tradition does not counsel silence in the face of preventable death. Ezekiel's image of the watchman is about exactly this: "If the watchman sees the sword coming and does not blow the trumpet, so that the people are not warned, and the sword comes and takes any one of them, that person is taken away in his iniquity, but his blood I will require at the watchman's hand" (Ezekiel 33:6).

The watchman is not responsible for manufacturing his own security force. He is responsible for naming what he sees. The naming is not optional. The pretense of not having seen anything is not available to the one who has.

The church has seen this. It has seen it for decades. The silence is not neutrality. It is a choice. And every choice in the face of preventable suffering has a name.

The family that buried a child in a city your church borders has not been served by your silence. The congregation that needed a pastor willing to stand in the difficulty of this question has not been served by a man who decided the difficulty was too great. The prophetic calling does not offer a provision for topics too culturally complicated to address.

We have seen the sword coming. The trumpet is our responsibility.
