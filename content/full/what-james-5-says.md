James 5 begins with a warning, and it is not addressed to the poor. *Now listen, you rich people, weep and wail because of the misery that is coming on you* (James 5:1). The letter does not ease into this. It arrives at full force in the first sentence of the chapter, and what follows is not a balanced treatment of wealth's complications or a careful exploration of money's mixed effects on the spiritual life. It is a sustained, specific, theologically grounded condemnation of the wealthy who have built their comfort on the exploitation of workers. It is among the most economically direct passages in the New Testament, and the American church has spent considerable energy not reading it.

The most common homiletical approach to James 5 is to treat it as a warning about the spiritual dangers of wealth in general — the idolatry of money, the false security of accumulated assets, the spiritual blindness that prosperity can produce. These things are real, and James addresses them. But the text does not stay at the level of spiritual danger. It names a specific crime: *The wages you failed to pay the workers who mowed your fields are crying out against you. The cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord Almighty* (James 5:4). This is not a metaphor for spiritual displacement from God. It is a description of an economic practice — withholding wages — that God identifies as a sin requiring urgent response.

The practice James condemns was familiar in the first-century economy in which he wrote. Day laborers — workers without permanent employment or institutional protection — were paid at the end of each day. This arrangement is encoded in Deuteronomy 24:14–15, which commands: *Pay them their wages each day before sunset, because they are poor and are counting on it.* The daily wage was, for most of these workers, the margin between eating and not eating. To hold it back was not simply to delay a financial transaction. It was to use the power differential between employer and worker — the employer's ability to wait, the worker's inability to — as an instrument of further extraction. The employer who withheld wages was not only keeping money that belonged to the worker. He was forcing the worker's desperation to compound daily, increasing his own power while decreasing the worker's capacity to resist.

James writes this into a context of significant economic stratification in the early church. The letter is addressed to dispersed Jewish Christian communities in which class differences were real, visible, and apparently tolerated in the community's practice in ways James finds intolerable. James 2 addresses the same structural problem from the side of worship: the wealthy visitor gets the good seat, the poor man stands by the wall, and the community that has arranged its liturgical space around the power hierarchy of the surrounding culture has denied the faith in the very act of assembling (James 2:1–4). James 5 addresses the same problem from the side of economic life: the wealthy who have accumulated their resources by exploiting workers, and who presumably sit in those good seats, have their wages crying out against them before the Lord Almighty.

The ears of the Lord Almighty. This is not a small claim. The Hebrew behind the title — *Yahweh Sabaoth*, Lord of Hosts — is the title used when God acts in history to right what has been wronged, to intervene against the powers that have arrayed themselves against the vulnerable. When the prophets invoke *Yahweh Sabaoth*, something is about to happen. James is invoking it here in the context of unpaid wages. The God who hears the cry of the oppressed (Exodus 3:7) hears the cries of the harvesters. The theological claim is that wage theft is not a private economic matter between employer and employee. It is a matter that rises before the God who governs history.

The application to the American economy in 2026 is direct, and the church's silence on it is conspicuous.

Wage theft — the failure to pay workers what they have legally and contractually earned — is among the most pervasive economic crimes in the United States. The Economic Policy Institute has documented that minimum wage violations alone cost workers billions of dollars annually [cite — confirm]. When overtime violations, tip theft, illegal deductions, and off-the-clock work requirements are included, the scale dwarfs all property crimes combined [cite — confirm]. The workers most affected are the ones who least have the power to challenge the arrangement: undocumented workers who fear immigration enforcement, workers in industries where retaliation is common, workers who do not know their legal rights, workers whose economic desperation mirrors exactly the first-century day laborers James was addressing.

The church has largely treated wage theft as an economic policy debate. It is that. It is also, on the plain reading of James 5, a theological emergency.

The text expands beyond wages in the verses that follow. *You have lived on earth in luxury and self-indulgence. You have fattened yourselves in the day of slaughter. You have condemned and murdered the innocent one, who was not opposing you* (James 5:5–6). James moves from the specific economic crime to the broader condition it reveals: a life organized around the extraction of comfort at the expense of others, ending in a judgment so severe he describes it in terms of slaughter. The luxury and self-indulgence are not condemned merely as spiritual risks to the wealthy person. They are condemned as the fruit of the worker's unpaid wages — the direct causal connection between what the employer has and what the worker has not.

This is the move the church consistently refuses to make. It will address wealth as a spiritual condition — the idol that competes with God for the heart, the anxiety that excessive accumulation produces, the generosity that God's people are called to practice. These are real and worth addressing. But James is addressing something structurally prior: the wealth that was built by taking what belongs to the worker. The luxury that rests on the foundation of the withheld wage. The generosity question cannot be asked without first asking how the wealth was accumulated. James asks it. The church has preferred not to.

The counter-position that must be engaged is the argument that James 5 addresses first-century economic conditions that are qualitatively different from contemporary capitalist markets, and that applying it directly to modern employment relationships requires a contextual leap that the text does not authorize. Contemporary labor law provides protections James's workers lacked; the state regulates wages in ways the Roman Empire did not; the employer-employee relationship is framed by contract and regulated by enforcement mechanisms that the day laborer had no access to.

This argument has some force. The specific mechanism James addresses — the daily withholding of cash wages from agricultural day laborers — is not the most common contemporary form of wage theft. The forms are more varied: misclassification of employees as independent contractors to avoid wage obligations, algorithmic scheduling that produces fewer hours than workers were promised, mandatory off-the-clock training, tip pooling arrangements that redirect gratuities to management [cite — confirm]. The technical forms have changed.

The theological structure has not. The wealthy using the power differential between themselves and workers to extract additional value from those who cannot easily resist it — this is exactly what James condemns. The sophistication of the mechanism does not change the theological character of the act. The wages crying out before the Lord Almighty are crying out in 2026 as surely as they were crying out in the first century. The ears that hear have not changed.

James does not offer policy prescriptions. He does not tell the early church what labor laws to advocate for, what enforcement mechanisms to demand, how to organize workers for collective bargaining. He speaks the prophetic word: this is what you are doing, this is what it costs those you are doing it to, these are the ears into which their cry has risen, this is what is coming. The prophetic function is to name reality in light of God's character and God's commitments. What the community then does with that naming — how it translates the prophetic word into pastoral practice, congregational accountability, civic engagement — is the community's ongoing work.

But the prophetic word must first be spoken. The congregation that has never heard James 5 applied to the actual conditions of its economic life has not been fully formed by the canon it claims to hold. The pastor who has found a way to preach about spiritual attitudes toward money without addressing the workers whose wages are crying out has chosen a narrower gospel than the letter offers.

I am in this. The congregation I serve includes people who own businesses. It includes people who have benefited from economic arrangements they did not design and perhaps did not question. It includes me — a man who pays the staff of a church and has not always asked hard enough questions about whether what I am offering is adequate. The prophetic word lands on the preacher first. James is not addressing someone else's congregation.

*The cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord Almighty.* The church's response to those cries should be something more than silence. It should be something more than teaching wealthy congregants to manage their anxiety about money. It should be, at minimum, the willingness to read James 5 where it actually goes, to name what it names, and to let the congregation sit with the implication that the God who hears the workers has an opinion about how his people treat them.

That is not politics. That is Scripture. The difference matters, and the church that cannot make the distinction has not protected itself from political capture. It has ensured it.
