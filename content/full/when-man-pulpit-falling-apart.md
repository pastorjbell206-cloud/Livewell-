Sometimes the crisis is not in the congregation. It is in the man at the front.

A marriage coming apart. A depression that will not lift. A grief too large to preach around. A failure that is about to become public. The pastor faces a question no class prepared him for: what do you do when the person whose job is to hold everyone else together is the one falling apart?

I have been closer to this question than I like to admit. And what I know now — what I did not know in the middle of it — is that the question itself is formed by a wrong assumption. The assumption that the man in the pulpit is supposed to be the person who holds everyone else together. That the pastoral function is to be the non-anxious presence, the steady center, the rock on which the congregation leans. That if he is the one falling, something has gone catastrophically wrong.

That assumption is a theological error. And it will kill you if you believe it.

---

The first instinct when the crisis arrives is almost always concealment, and the instinct is understandable. We have trained pastors to be the strong ones. The culture of professional ministry has specific expectations about composure, about the managed presentation of emotional stability, about the pastoral persona that is available and steady and never visibly overwhelmed. To let the congregation see the collapse feels like a dereliction — as though the man's whole authority depended on appearing intact.

So he performs wellness over an open wound. He preaches strength he does not have. He structures his public face around the management of an image while the real man bleeds out behind it. He learns to preach about suffering from a safe theological distance that protects the congregation from the knowledge that the preacher is suffering. He prays with people whose problems are smaller than his, and does not say so. He counsels marriages while his own is under water, and calls this normal, and keeps going until he cannot.

The performing is not a neutral choice. It has costs that accumulate before they become visible.

It costs the man himself first — the sustained cognitive and emotional work of maintaining a performance across every public context while managing an internal reality that contradicts it. This is exhausting in ways that compound over time. The man who performs wellness for long enough begins to lose track of what he actually feels, because the performance has colonized the space where genuine emotional experience used to live.

It costs the congregation second, in a way that is less obvious but more theologically serious. A pastor who must appear unbroken to keep his standing is quietly teaching his people that the Christian life is the management of an image — that grace is for the congregation but the pastor must be self-sufficient, that the gospel of weakness made strong (2 Corinthians 12:9-10) applies to everyone except the man explaining it. The congregation absorbs that lesson whether or not he ever says it. And they take it home, and apply it to their own suffering, and conclude that real faith means not letting your collapse show.

This is not the gospel. This is the prosperity theology that smuggled itself into the pastoral office.

---

Paul wrote about his own weakness with a specificity that the pastoral tradition has often glossed over. "I have been in labor and toil, in many sleepless nights, in hunger and thirst, often without food, in cold and exposure. And apart from all these external things, there is the daily pressure of my anxiety for all the churches. Who is weak, and I am not weak? Who is made to fall, and I am not indignant?" (2 Corinthians 11:27-29). This is not a rhetorical performance of vulnerability designed to authenticate his authority. This is a man naming what pastoral ministry actually cost him. The anxiety was real. The weakness was real. He wrote about it in a letter to a congregation he was fighting to keep.

He also wrote, in that same letter, about a thorn in the flesh — something so significant that he pleaded with God three times to remove it, and God declined (2 Corinthians 12:7-9). The reason given: "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness." This is not a consolation prize. It is a theological claim about where divine power operates. It does not operate through the performance of invincibility. It operates through acknowledged limitation. The man who is honest about what he cannot do creates the space where God can do what only God can.

The pastor who hides his weakness does not protect his congregation. He deprives them of the testimony that matters most: that the Gospel is true when it is tested, not only when it is proclaimed.

---

There is, of course, wisdom in how and what you disclose. This requires more than a blanket call to transparency.

Not every detail belongs in front of the congregation. Some crises are not only the pastor's — they involve his family, and the members of his family who have not chosen public life deserve their privacy. A marriage in crisis cannot be fully disclosed from the pulpit without exposing the other person in the marriage to a congregation that will carry that information in ways neither of them can control. The pastoral crisis that involves sin must be handled through the appropriate structures of accountability rather than through public disclosure that can substitute for genuine repentance and restoration.

Discernment matters. The question is not "how much do I tell?" but "how do I stop pretending in ways that allow the people I lead to see that I am a man under the same grace I preach?"

That distinction matters. The pastor does not owe the congregation the details of his crisis. He owes them the truth that he has one, and that he is being a Christian in it — that he is doing the things he has told them to do, rather than doing the things the pastoral performance culture has told him to do. He is not performing composure. He is letting himself be carried. He is in the room where real things are happening to him, and he is bringing those real things to the God he preaches on Sunday.

The congregation watching their pastor honestly walk through a hard season is not losing confidence in the Gospel. They are, for perhaps the first time, seeing evidence that it is real. That it holds the weight of an actual life. That the man who preaches it on Sunday is not exempt from needing it on Monday.

---

Elijah in 1 Kings 19 is the textbook for what this looks like from God's side. Elijah has just experienced one of the most dramatic prophetic victories in the Old Testament — the confrontation at Mount Carmel, the fire coming down, the prophets of Baal defeated — and he immediately collapses. He runs. He sits under a juniper tree and asks God to take his life. "It is enough," he says. "I am no better than my fathers." He falls asleep.

God's response is not rebuke. God does not tell him to recover his composure and get back to work. God lets him sleep. Then an angel wakes him and says, not "pull yourself together," but "arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you" (1 Kings 19:7). The acknowledgment is direct: this is too much. The provision is direct: food, water, rest. The invitation back into ministry is extended after two rounds of sleep and eating, not before.

The God of Elijah is not the God of the pastoral performance culture. He does not require the prophet to pretend he is fine. He meets him where he has collapsed. He provides for the practical need. He asks, gently, "What are you doing here?" — and then he listens to the answer, even when the answer is a long complaint that is only partially accurate. And then he re-commissions him.

This is the pattern. The man who collapses, brought to God honestly, is the man God re-commissions. Not the man who manages the collapse into invisibility and keeps going on empty.

---

Practically, what does this look like?

It means getting help before you need it and certainly when you need it. It means having people in your life — outside the congregation, not subject to your pastoral authority, not dependent on your professional function — who can hold what is actually happening. It means being willing to take time away, to see a counselor, to let the elders carry more than usual, to be honest with the board about the level of your struggle when honesty is required. It means trusting that the institution you have given your life to can survive knowing you are human.

Some of this trust will be betrayed. Some congregations and boards, when the pastor is honest about a significant personal struggle, will respond not with support but with pressure, or with the first serious questioning of his fitness. That is a real risk. The culture of pastoral invincibility did not grow without reason — vulnerability has been used against pastors in genuinely damaging ways.

But the alternative — the man who keeps going behind the mask until the mask no longer fits, who breaks in public what he refused to name in private — is worse. The managed collapse is more damaging than the honest one. The congregation can handle more truth than the pastoral culture assumes. And the pastor who lets himself be held, in controlled and pastoral ways, usually discovers that what he feared would destroy his standing actually deepened it.

---

I was loved for my function. That was the fear underneath the concealment. If the function faltered, the love would go.

The healing, when it came, came partly through discovering that the fear was a lie. The people who actually loved me wanted the man, not the performance. And letting them see the broken version did not cost me my ministry. It deepened it. They had heard me say that we are saved by grace and not by strength. Now they watched me need it.

The man in the pulpit will fall apart. Not might. Will.

The only question is whether he does it hiding or held.

Choose held. It is the truer sermon.

And it is the only one that will keep you.
