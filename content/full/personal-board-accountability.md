Every CEO has a board. Every doctor has a licensing body. Every pilot answers to a tower. The pastor — who handles people's souls, money, marriages, and secrets — often answers to no one who could actually stop him. We have built a profession of unusual spiritual power and unusual practical isolation, and then we are surprised, every few months, by another name in the news.

This is not mainly about scandal, though it ends there often enough. It is about the ordinary erosion that happens to any man with no one above him. Authority without accountability does not stay neutral. It curdles. Not because pastors are worse than other people, but because they are not better — and we have arranged the job as though they were.

The biblical tradition understood this before modern psychology named it. The kings of Israel were instructed to keep the Torah beside them, to read it daily so that "his heart may not be lifted up above his brothers, and that he may not turn aside from the commandment, to the right hand or to the left" (Deuteronomy 17:20). Not because the king was presumed corrupt. Because the structure of unchecked authority, even in godly men, produces a predictable drift — upward, into isolation; sideward, away from the people being led. The commandment assumed that the drift would come, and legislated against it in advance.

David is the case study. He was the man after God's own heart (1 Samuel 13:14), and by the time he sent Joab out with the armies and stayed in Jerusalem, the architecture of isolation had already done its slow work. Nathan had to come to him from outside the system — had to use a parable to get past the palace insulation — because David had no one inside his life with the standing to wound him for his own good. The fall with Bathsheba was not a sudden event. It was the predictable terminus of a man who had slowly stopped being accountable to anyone close enough to matter.

We tell that story every year from pulpits, and then we reproduce the same conditions in our own lives.

---

So build the thing the job did not give you. Not a committee. A board — a small, deliberate set of people whose relationship to you is not managed by you.

The word "accountability" has been so cheapened in evangelical usage that it requires rescue before it can do any work. What most pastors call accountability is a periodic conversation with someone they selected, can drop at will, and would never answer truthfully in a genuine crisis. That is not accountability. That is a performance of accountability, which is more dangerous than nothing because it lets a man relax exactly where he should be vigilant.

Real accountability has structural features. The people must have actual information — access to the calendar, the finances, the marriage — not the curated summary you choose to share. They must have genuine permission to ask the question you would most prefer they not ask, and the relationship must be old enough and deep enough that they will actually ask it. And their word must carry weight you cannot simply overrule when the answer is inconvenient. Accountability that you can dismiss at will is theater. You are performing for your own conscience.

What are the actual roles?

You need someone who knew you before the title. This person is immune to your platform because they remember who you were before you had one. The ministry success that impresses your congregation does not impress them in the same way, which means they can still see you. When you are tempted to believe that the position defines you — a temptation that grows more powerful the longer you are in the role — this person remembers the man who existed before the office, and they are not willing to lose him in the persona.

You need someone older in the faith, preferably a retired pastor or senior minister, who has lived past the crises you are only beginning to encounter. Age gives a different kind of sight. They have watched the patterns play out. They can name in January what you will be facing by September, because they have seen the arc before. And crucially, they are past the career stage where they need anything from you — which makes their counsel uncorruptible by the normal social pressures of ministry relationships.

You need a peer. Another pastor, outside your denomination or network if possible, who understands the peculiar weight of the work from the inside. This person does not need the dynamics explained. He has sat in the same chair on Thursday afternoon. He knows what it costs to preach through a season when nothing is coming. And because he is outside your system, he has no stake in your decisions — no budget tied to your choices, no job security connected to your approval. He can tell you the truth without losing anything by it.

You need at least one person who is not impressed by you at all. This sounds harsh. It is a gift. A man surrounded entirely by people who admire him will slowly lose the ability to hear the truth, because truth-telling rarely comes from people who are emotionally invested in your success. The unimpressed person asks the obvious question that everyone else is too deferential to raise.

---

The defining feature of a real board is this: they can say no to you, and it costs you something to ignore them. The men who fall are almost never the men with no one watching. They are the men who arranged their watchers carefully — chose the ones who would not look too closely, kept the relationships shallow enough to stay comfortable, and called it community.

I have had to ask myself the harder version of this. Not *do I have accountability* — every pastor will say yes — but *is there a single person who could tell me to stop, and would I actually stop?* For long stretches, the honest answer was no. I had admirers I called advisors. I had a board on paper and no one in my life with the standing to wound me for my own good. That is not safety. That is the appearance of safety, and the appearance is the more dangerous condition.

The Wisdom tradition in Scripture is ruthlessly clear about this. *Where there is no guidance, a people falls, but in an abundance of counselors there is safety* (Proverbs 11:14). The assumption is not that the counseled man is weak — the assumption is that all men, left entirely to themselves, drift, and that the remedy is not more willpower but more voices with genuine authority to contradict. Proverbs does not say "in an abundance of encouragers." It says counselors — people with the freedom and standing to tell you what you need to hear rather than what you would prefer to believe.

Paul understood this structurally, which is why his letters are so full of named relationships, specific partnerships, networks of mutual accountability across cities. He asked the Corinthians to evaluate him (1 Corinthians 4:3-4), not because he expected their judgment to be definitive, but because a pastor who is fully insulated from the community's assessment of his ministry has severed something essential. He wrote to Timothy and Titus as younger men he was willing to correct in writing, because correction is what genuine mentorship requires.

---

The steel-man position deserves honest attention: there is something to the pastor's argument that accountability structures can become mechanisms of control, that some church governance models have used "board oversight" to manipulate pastors rather than protect them, and that the wrong accountability relationship can become a cage rather than a guardrail. This is true. I have seen denominations treat pastoral accountability as a way to suppress prophetic voices rather than correct moral failures.

But that abuse of the principle does not discard the principle. It clarifies what genuine accountability is not. It is not hierarchical control by institutional power. It is a self-chosen, trust-built community of people with genuine access to the whole man, whose only stake in his life is his flourishing and his congregation's health.

The way to build it is simple enough to state. Choose the people on purpose — do not drift into accountability; select it. Tell them plainly what you are asking, because a relationship that begins with false premises will produce false safety. Give them the actual access that accountability requires: the financial records, the marriage, the private calendar, the digital life, because the places you most resist opening are almost always the places that need the most light. Meet with enough regularity that the relationship is alive before the crisis arrives — a board assembled after the fall is just a jury.

And return the favor. Offer yourself to the men in your circle with the same seriousness you ask them to bring. Accountability that flows only downward is supervision, not fellowship.

The man at the top of an organization with no one able to stop him is not strong. He is exposed, and the exposure is invisible to him right up until it is the only thing anyone can see. He is David, sitting in Jerusalem in the spring, with no Nathan already inside the room.

Build the board now. Before you need it. While you can still choose the people honest enough to hurt you — and while the hurt will cost you only your pride, not everything else.

That window does not stay open forever. The longer a man leads without genuine accountability, the more the isolation thickens around him, and the harder it becomes to invite the very people who could save him. The men in the newspaper did not set out to destroy their ministries. They set out to lead, and the structure that surrounded them — the structures they built or permitted or simply failed to dismantle — made the fall almost inevitable.

We have no excuse for building the same structures again.
