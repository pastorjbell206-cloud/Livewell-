Every year, thousands of American churches send teams to places of concentrated poverty — to communities in Central America, Haiti, sub-Saharan Africa, or the low-income neighborhoods of their own cities — where they build things, paint things, and hand things to people. They return with photographs. The photographs are often shown in church, sometimes with tears. The testimonies describe transformation — primarily the transformation of the team members, who speak about having their perspective changed, about realizing how much they have, about learning that people in poverty are happy.

None of this is mission. Most of it is tourism with a good conscience attached.

I am not exempt from this indictment. I have participated in these trips. I have benefited from the feeling of having done something. I have used the photographs. The critique I am pressing here is not pointed outward at a practice I have only observed from a distance. It is pointed at a pattern I have been inside, and that I have taken too long to examine honestly.

The short-term mission trip as it has been institutionalized in American evangelical culture over the last four decades [cite — confirm] is a peculiar form of consumer experience that serves the spiritual formation of wealthy participants at the expense of the communities they visit. The economics are straightforward and damning: a team of ten Americans flies to Guatemala at a cost of several thousand dollars per person. That money, spent in the receiving community rather than on the transportation and lodging of the visiting team, would accomplish more durable good by several orders of magnitude. The construction work the team does in a week — which requires months of preparation by local staff, disrupts community life while the visitors are present, and frequently needs to be redone by local professionals afterward — could have been completed more quickly, more cheaply, and more competently by local workers who would have been paid a wage and returned to their families at night.

The short-term trip persists not because it is the most effective mechanism for serving the communities being visited. It persists because it effectively serves the communities doing the visiting — it creates an experience of encounter with poverty and with the global church that is genuinely formational for participants, it builds congregational investment in the mission budget, and it generates the kind of emotional engagement with global Christianity that a financial contribution alone does not produce.

That is not nothing. Formation matters. Emotional connection to the global church matters. The problem is when the formation of the visitor is purchased at the cost of the dignity and the development of the community being visited.

Robert Lupton, who spent decades in urban community development in Atlanta, documented in his work [cite — confirm] what community development practitioners have long observed: repeated short-term mission interventions in a community, in which outsiders arrive to fix and provide and then depart, tend to reinforce the dependency and passivity that genuine development must work against. The community that is perpetually visited by helpers who treat its members as recipients of charity rather than agents of their own flourishing absorbs a message about itself — a message about who acts and who is acted upon, who has knowledge and resources and who needs them delivered from outside.

The New Testament pattern of mission does not resemble the short-term trip. Paul's missionary work was characterized by extended presence — years in Corinth, years in Ephesus, months in Thessalonica and Philippi. His goal was not to deliver goods and depart. It was to see the church planted, led by local people, rooted in local culture, capable of sustaining itself and multiplying without his continued presence. In Philippians 4, he thanks the Philippian church for their support of his work — but the support flows from a congregation that has become a community, not from a team that flies in and out. The sending culture Paul describes in his letters is long-term, relational, and culminates in the full agency of the receiving community.

Acts 6 records the Jerusalem church's response to the complaint that Hellenist widows were being overlooked in the daily distribution. The apostles do not appoint a visiting team to address the problem. They identify people within the community — people "of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom" (Acts 6:3) from among the overlooked group itself — and give them the authority and resources to administer the distribution. The model is local leadership, community agency, the development of capacity within the people most affected rather than the ongoing delivery of services from outside.

This is not an argument against any form of cross-cultural engagement. It is an argument against the specific form that serves the visitor more than the visited, that frames the relationship primarily in terms of what the visitor delivers rather than what the community already possesses, that produces photographs and testimonies about changed perspectives without producing sustained partnership, accountability, or genuine investment in the capacity of the local community to define and pursue its own flourishing.

The counter-argument deserves a hearing. It goes like this: even imperfect engagement is better than disengagement. The trip that changes a nineteen-year-old's understanding of global poverty may produce, over that person's lifetime, decades of advocacy, giving, and genuine partnership that was made possible by the initial encounter. The formational value of contact with the global church — of praying in a language you don't speak, of sharing a meal in a home where the table is a different size than yours, of seeing a faith community that has less by every material measure and more joy than the ones you inhabit — is not zero.

This is true. The counter-argument is not wrong. But it is incomplete. The question is not whether some good is produced. The question is whether the good produced for the visitor justifies the cost imposed on the community being visited — and whether the church has been honest about who the trip primarily serves.

Mission is not a feeling produced in the person who goes. Mission is the participation of a community in the ongoing work of God to restore the world — to establish justice for the poor, to proclaim good news to the captive, to reconcile the estranged. That work requires presence, not visits. It requires mutuality, not delivery. It requires the kind of sustained partnership in which the so-called receiving community has genuine authority over how the relationship is structured and what the collaboration accomplishes.

The difference between mission and poverty tourism is not in the destination. It is in the posture. Tourism positions the visitor as the subject and the community as the scene. Mission positions the community as the agent and the visitor as a learner, a companion, someone arriving with resources but not with the answers.

The church has resources. Significant resources. The question is whether those resources are deployed in ways that strengthen the communities we claim to serve or in ways that strengthen our own sense of having served — which are not the same question, and the answers to them are not the same.

Genuine partnership with communities in the Global South and in low-income neighborhoods in the United States looks like multi-year relationships with organizations and communities that have defined their own goals and developed their own leadership. It looks like funding that follows the priorities of the receiving community rather than the interests of the donor. It looks like the willingness to be taught — to receive from the community you are accompanying something that you could not have produced on your own. It looks like accountability structures that make the partnership genuinely mutual rather than charitable.

It is slower. It is less photogenic. It does not produce the same kind of testimony on a Sunday morning. It asks the congregation to invest in something they cannot hold and turn over in their hands.

But it is mission. The trip, alone, is not.

There is a harder question underneath the practical one. The short-term mission trip has been sustained, in part, by a theology of service that frames the wealthy Christian's primary relationship to the poor as one of delivery rather than accompaniment. We go. We give. We fix. We return. The agency runs in one direction — from the one with resources to the one without. The relationship is constituted by the differential between the giver and the receiver. What this frames out entirely is the possibility that the community being visited has something to give to the visitor that cannot be obtained any other way — a theological wisdom forged in poverty and persistence, a joy that is not contingent on material security, a community coherence that the visiting congregation would do well to study and receive. The church in communities of concentrated poverty has historically been one of the most theologically alive expressions of the Christian faith anywhere in the world. The short-term visitor who goes to serve and never stops to receive has missed more than he brought.

The question is whether the church is willing to do what mission actually requires, or whether it is content with the version that produces the feeling without the cost.

The feeling is not the point. The kingdom is.
