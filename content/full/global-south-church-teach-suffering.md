There is a prayer meeting happening right now in northern Nigeria, or in a house church in Henan province in China, or in a village in Eritrea, where the people gathered are praying in full awareness that their faith may cost them something before the week is out. They are not praying for comfort. They are praying for courage. They are praying for each other's families in the event of arrest. They are praying, many of them, with the kind of specificity and desperation that is almost entirely absent from the prayer life of the average American congregation.

I have led prayer meetings in Fenton, Michigan. I know what they sound like. The comparison is not comfortable.

The theology of suffering that emerges from the persecuted church is not an academic product. It is not developed in seminaries, though it is reflected in their finest theological thinking. It is developed in the experience of communities that have had no option but to discover what Paul actually means when he writes in Romans 8:18 that "the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed." They have not discovered this because they are more spiritual than American Christians. They have discovered it because they have had no comfortable alternative to the text.

---

The American church's theology of suffering is, by contrast, largely a theology of avoidance. We believe in a God who heals, who protects, who provides — and we are right to believe those things, because they are true. But we have often stopped the sentence there, at the part where God removes the difficulty, without proceeding to the part where God is present in the difficulty that does not get removed.

Hebrews 11 does not end with verse 33, where the heroes of faith conquered kingdoms and stopped the mouths of lions and received their dead back to life. It continues to verse 37, where others were sawn in two, and verse 38, where they wandered in deserts and mountains and caves, and verse 39, where none of them received what was promised.

Both halves of that chapter are the witness of faith. The American church preaches the first half with great enthusiasm. The second half makes us uncomfortable, because it does not fit the framework we have built for understanding what faithfulness looks like. We have built that framework in the context of a society in which the premium religious product is consolation, and in which suffering is primarily framed as a problem to be solved or at minimum explained — a pastoral emergency to be managed until it lifts, not a condition to be inhabited with faithfulness.

The Psalms are more honest about this than most contemporary American preaching is. The lament psalms — which constitute roughly a third of the Psalter — do not resolve. Psalm 88 ends in darkness: "You have caused my beloved and my friend to shun me; my companions have become darkness" (Psalm 88:18). No resolution. No pivot to praise. Darkness is the last word. The Hebrew canon preserved that psalm. The canon made room for the prayer that does not get answered the way the pray-er hoped.

The American church has largely edited those prayers out. We rush to the resolution because unresolved suffering makes us theologically anxious — it raises questions about God's goodness, God's power, the mechanism of prayer, the relationship between faith and outcome. Rather than sit in the tension, we move toward the answer. Usually a specific kind of answer: the one in which God intervenes and the suffering ends.

---

The church in the Global South — particularly in communities experiencing active persecution — has had to work with both halves of the Hebrews 11 text, because their experience includes both. They have seen the miraculous. They have also buried people for whom the miraculous did not come. Their faith has been formed in the tension between those two realities rather than in the avoidance of it.

This does not make suffering good. Lamin Sanneh, the great Gambian-born historian of World Christianity who taught at Yale for decades [cite — confirm], spent his career tracing how the gospel was received by communities who had no reason to expect that Christianity would align with their social comfort or political power. His work describes communities whose faith was forged in the absence of cultural support, in which the text spoke to people facing exactly the kind of extremity that the text addresses most directly.

The witness of those communities is not theoretical. It is not a chapter in a global theology textbook. It is the testimony of people — people with names, families, histories — who have found that God is present in conditions where the comfortable theology fails. Who have discovered that the prayer for removal of the cup was answered, for Jesus himself, by the cup's not being removed, and yet by the presence of the Father through what followed.

That is a different discovery than anything most American prayer meetings produce.

---

Philip Jenkins, in his account of the global South's Christian explosion, describes communities that read the biblical text of suffering without the hermeneutical filters that a culturally established church develops to soften the hardest passages [cite — confirm]. The passages about persecution, about the cost of discipleship, about the enemies of the cross — these are not metaphorical in communities that have literal enemies. They are read plainly, because the plain reading is the one that corresponds to the community's actual experience.

That plain reading produces a specific theological posture: not the expectation that faith produces safety, but the conviction that faith produces the capacity to remain faithful even when it does not produce safety. The distinction is everything. The first expectation — faith equals protection — is the expectation that collapses when the suffering arrives and does not lift. It is the expectation behind the "crisis of faith" that many Western Christians experience when life goes badly. The second conviction — faith equals the capacity for faithfulness within suffering — is the conviction that has sustained the church through every century of serious opposition.

Peter writes to communities who are already suffering: "Beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery trial when it comes upon you to test you, as though something strange were happening to you. But rejoice insofar as you share Christ's sufferings, that you may also rejoice and be glad when his glory is revealed." (1 Peter 4:12-13) He does not tell them the trial will end. He tells them what to do with it while it is happening. He has a theology for the situation they are in. Most American pastoral theology has a theology for the situation in which the trial ends quickly.

---

What this means practically for the American pastor is a question worth sitting with.

The first thing it means is that genuine relationship with the global church — relationship sustained over time, relationship that involves actual listening rather than project management — is not a nice addition to a well-rounded ministry. It is a theological necessity for any pastor who wants to preach honestly about suffering to people who suffer. The pastor whose theology of suffering has been formed entirely in comfortable circumstances will eventually face a congregant whose circumstances are not comfortable, and he will have words that are insufficient for what that person is carrying.

I have had that experience. I have sat with someone in a suffering that my theology could not hold, and I have watched myself reach for the platitudes — the God-is-in-control language, the Romans-8:28 application, the promise of eventual resolution — because I had not been formed by any tradition that could do better. I was not being dishonest. I was being inadequate. And the person across from me deserved more than my inadequacy.

The second thing it means is that we need to recover the lament tradition — not as a program or a series, but as a genuine liturgical practice. The community that cannot lament together cannot grieve together. The community that cannot grieve together will eventually find that suffering is an event to be managed rather than a human reality to be inhabited together. The church in Nigeria or the house church in Henan knows how to weep with those who weep (Romans 12:15) because it has had to. We can choose to learn it.

The third thing it means is about the prosperity theology that has metastasized through significant portions of American Christianity. The persecuted church is not the church that believes its faithfulness will be rewarded with health and wealth and favor in this life. The persecuted church knows, from hard and recent experience, that faithfulness is sometimes rewarded with prison and death. Any theology that cannot account for those outcomes is not the theology of the New Testament. It is a theology that has been constructed to serve a comfortable audience, and it will shatter when the comfort is removed.

The church in the Global South holds the mirror up to this. Not because they have something we do not — they have less of almost everything by material measure. But because the conditions they have been formed in have forced them to read the text without the insulation we use to soften it.

---

What that formation produces is a different quality of hope. Not optimism — which is a confidence that things will improve — but hope, which is a confidence that God is present regardless of whether things improve. The distinction is not semantic. It shapes how a community prays, how it sustains commitment across discouraging seasons, how it understands the death of its members, how it preaches to people who are suffering.

The American pastor who has access to this tradition through relationship with the global church — through genuine, sustained partnership rather than conference exposure — is receiving something that cannot be manufactured in a comfortable environment. He is receiving the testimony of people who have found God sufficient in conditions where nothing else was.

That testimony does not make suffering good. It does not make the persecution of the church in Nigeria or China or Eritrea acceptable. It demands our advocacy, our resources, our solidarity, our political voice where we have one.

But it also carries a word for us — for the comfortable church in the comfortable country — about what faith actually looks like when the scaffolding of cultural support and social acceptability and institutional favor is removed.

The word is: still faithful.

We need to hear that word more than we know. We need to hear it from people who have not said it theoretically, who have said it while standing in the ash, who say it still with nothing but the promise of the one who said it first.

Still faithful.

That is the whole of the tradition. And we have been too comfortable to fully receive it.
