In 597 BCE, the Babylonian army under Nebuchadnezzar took the first wave of Jerusalem's population into captivity. Among those deported were the king, the royal household, the craftsmen, the military leadership — the skilled and influential, the people the empire found useful and the people it found dangerous. They were relocated to Babylon, the most powerful city in the ancient Near East, the center of an empire that had just dismantled everything they had organized their lives around.

Into that situation, the prophet Jeremiah wrote a letter. It is preserved in Jeremiah 29, and it is one of the most surprising documents in the entire prophetic tradition.

---

The situation demanded a clear prophetic response, and the exiles were already receiving it. Other prophets — Hananiah is the named example in the surrounding chapters (Jeremiah 28) — were telling the exiles what they desperately wanted to hear: this would be brief, the exile would end in two years, God would restore the temple vessels and return the people to Jerusalem before the decade was out. The message of these prophets was emotionally satisfying and structurally coherent with the tradition. God had always acted to restore his people. God had rescued Israel from Egypt. God had preserved the remnant through every historical catastrophe. Surely God would do it again, and soon.

Jeremiah's letter says something else.

*Thus says the Lord of hosts, the God of Israel, to all the exiles whom I have sent into exile from Jerusalem to Babylon: Build houses and live in them; plant gardens and eat their produce. Take wives and have sons and daughters; take wives for your sons, and give your daughters in marriage, that they may bear sons and daughters; multiply there, and do not decrease. But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the Lord on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare* (Jeremiah 29:4-7).

This is astonishing. The exiles were expecting a rescue. They received an instruction to settle.

---

The word translated "welfare" in verse 7 is *shalom* — the comprehensive Hebrew concept of peace, flourishing, wholeness, the right ordering of all relationships: between God and humanity, between human beings, and between humanity and creation. Seek the *shalom* of the city. Pray for the *shalom* of Babylon.

Babylon is the city that destroyed Jerusalem. The city whose army killed Jeremiah's contemporaries in the siege. The city that pulled the exiles from their homes and marched them across the desert. And God — through the prophet, in the letter — instructs the exiles not to resist Babylon, not to pray for Babylon's destruction, not to maintain themselves in pure separation from Babylon's culture until the rescue arrives. They are told to plant gardens. To build houses. To give their children in marriage. To pray for the city.

The theological claim underneath the instruction is the one that makes it possible: *for in its welfare you will find your welfare.* The fate of the exiles and the fate of the city are not separable. The people of God in exile are not merely passing through Babylon on their way to the real story. They are inside the real story, and the real story includes Babylon.

---

The New Testament returns to this image explicitly and deliberately. Peter writes to Christians scattered across Asia Minor using the language of exile: *I urge you as sojourners and exiles to abstain from the passions of the flesh, which wage war against your soul. Keep your conduct among the Gentiles honorable* (1 Peter 2:11-12). The scattered church is positioned as a continuation of the exilic community — people whose true citizenship is elsewhere, who are living in a foreign city, who are called to the same posture Jeremiah commanded: engaged, present, seeking the welfare of the place, not withdrawn from it.

Paul in Romans 13 — the passage about submission to governing authorities, which has been so systematically misused — is read most clearly in light of this exilic posture. The church in Rome was not commanded to identify the Roman Empire with the kingdom of God. They were commanded not to position themselves as a revolutionary counter-state, because that posture would prevent them from doing what the exilic calling requires: being present in the city, serving its common life, bearing witness by their presence and their conduct rather than by their separation from it.

What Jeremiah's letter was resisting — and what remains relevant — is the posture of the exile who has separated himself so completely from the host culture that his life no longer has any contact with it. This posture feels pure. It is, in the logic of Jeremiah 29, a form of faithlessness.

---

The hermeneutical question — how to apply an ancient letter to a displaced Israelite community to the situation of contemporary churches in American cities — is real and cannot be waved away with easy analogies. American Christians are not exiles in the literal sense. Most of them hold full citizenship in the most powerful nation in history. The power differential is reversed: far from being the vulnerable immigrants seeking shalom in Babylon's streets, American Christianity has often been the dominant cultural force, the Babylon if you will extend the metaphor, against which minority communities have needed protection.

That complication matters. The letter to the exiles cannot simply be lifted and applied to the culturally dominant church in America without significant rearrangement. The congregation that uses Jeremiah 29 to baptize its civic engagement without asking which direction the power is flowing has misread the text.

But the letter still has purchase on the church's imagination, particularly as the cultural dominance of Christianity in America has eroded and as Christians increasingly describe themselves — rightly or wrongly — as a minority presence in a post-Christian culture. The posture the letter recommends — long-term commitment, generative presence, investment in the common good rather than retreat to a protected enclave — is the posture that faithful witness requires in any context of genuine minority.

And the specific instruction — *seek the welfare of the city* — is one that the contemporary church has specific forms of ability to follow.

---

What does it mean, practically, to seek the welfare of a city?

It means the church's ministry extends to the city's health, not only to the church's membership rolls. The school district, the public health infrastructure, the housing supply, the criminal justice system, the quality of the water — these are not secular concerns that the church is graciously condescending to address. They are precisely the domain of *shalom* that Jeremiah's letter places in the exilic community's hands.

The church that operates only as an internal service organization for its own members — providing religious services to the people who subscribe — has misunderstood the letter. The community is not called to build only its own houses. It is called to seek the welfare of the city where God has placed it, and in that welfare to find its own. The grammar is important: the welfare of the church and the welfare of the city are not competing claims. They are mutually constituting. A church that flourishes by privatizing its blessing — by capturing the goods of the kingdom and keeping them within its own walls — has not understood the economics of *shalom*.

This has specific implications for the way churches relate to their cities. It means developing genuine relationships with the institutions and communities that share the city — the other churches, the neighborhood organizations, the city government, the schools — not as mission field targets but as partners in the common work of building a city that is livable for the people who live in it. It means that the pastor who knows only his own congregation's demographics and concerns has not yet received the full scope of what Jeremiah's letter is asking.

It means praying for the city. Not generically — not the vague invocation of blessing that passes for civic prayer in Sunday morning services — but the specific, informed prayer of someone who knows the city's actual condition. Who knows that the water quality in the east side neighborhoods has been degraded for years and that the families there have been trying to be heard. Who knows that the third-grade reading levels in the poorest schools are falling. Who knows that three businesses closed on the main commercial corridor this month and what that means for the families that worked in them. Prayer rooted in knowledge is intercession. Prayer divorced from knowledge is sentiment.

---

Jeremiah's letter was written to people who were terrified. They had lost everything. The land, the temple, the monarchy — the three pillars of Israel's sense that God was present and active in their national life — had been shattered in a single campaign season. They were in a foreign city, practicing their faith without the structures it had always depended on, surrounded by people who worshipped other gods, wondering whether the God of Israel was even accessible in Babylonian territory.

Into that terror, the letter says: *Be here. Build. Plant. Multiply. Pray. The welfare of this city is the welfare of your children.*

It is not the word the exiles wanted. It is the word that preserved them for the seventy years until the return, and that gave them the habits — the capacity to be present, generative, and faithful in a foreign city — that would eventually make the return possible.

The church is always, in some sense, an exilic community. We are here, but not only here. Our citizenship is elsewhere, but we live in this city, on this street, in this neighborhood, right now.

The letter has arrived. The instruction is the same.

Seek the welfare of the city where God has placed you. The welfare you find there will be your welfare too.
