There is a man in my congregation who has worshiped God in a wheelchair for twenty years. He comes early, parks in the accessible spot, enters through the side door that the rest of the congregation does not use, and sits in the space we have designated near the back. He does not complain. He is generous with his presence and quieter than most about what his Sunday morning costs him.

I have never preached a sermon on disability justice. That is a confession, not a preamble.

The image of God — the *imago Dei* of Genesis 1:26-27 — is the most foundational claim Christian theology makes about human beings. Every person, without exception, bears this image. The text does not say: every person who can hear the sermon, every person who can climb the steps, every person whose body works in the way the architecture assumes. It says every person. The theological weight of that word has implications the church has largely refused to follow to their conclusions.

The history of Christian theology on this point is not uniformly inspiring. Augustine's reading of the *imago Dei* located it primarily in the rational soul, which led to implicit hierarchies of image-bearing based on intellectual capacity [cite — confirm]. Aquinas followed a similar line, associating the image with the intellect and will in their capacity to know and love God [cite — confirm]. These were not neutral academic positions. They had pastoral and ethical consequences: the more the image was located in cognitive capacity, the easier it became to suggest that those with severe cognitive disabilities bore it less fully — or, in the darkest versions of this reasoning, barely at all.

The text does not support this move. Genesis 1:26-27 uses the image language with striking breadth: *Let us make mankind in our image, in our likeness, so that they may rule over the fish in the sea.* The *imago Dei* is not primarily an intellectual faculty. Scholars today tend to understand it in the ancient Near Eastern context as a royal designation — the one who bears the image represents the king's authority in a territory, and the king's honor is implicated in how the image-bearer is treated [cite — confirm]. Applied to humanity, the claim is that every person — regardless of capacity, regardless of function, regardless of what the body can or cannot do — represents God in the creation and carries a dignity that is therefore not negotiable.

What the body can do is not the measure of the dignity it bears. This is not a difficult theological conclusion. It is simply the *imago Dei* followed to where it leads.

We have, as institutions, treated disability as a pastoral problem rather than a justice problem. This distinction matters. A pastoral problem calls for compassion and accommodation. A justice problem calls for examination of the systems that produce exclusion and structural change to those systems. The wheelchair ramp added to the side door is pastoral accommodation. Asking why the front entrance was built without one — and who decided it wasn't necessary — is the beginning of justice.

The Americans with Disabilities Act was signed into law in 1990 [cite — confirm]. It required physical accessibility in public accommodations and prohibited discrimination based on disability. It was a legal codification of what Christian theology had always implied but rarely enforced at the institutional level. The fact that the civil law got there before most churches is worth sitting with. The church that should have been the leading advocate for the full inclusion of people with disabilities was, in most cases, not leading. It was accommodating, imperfectly and under legal pressure.

The Gospels are striking in how consistently Jesus is surrounded by people whose bodies do not meet the expectations of the religious establishment. The blind, the deaf, the lame, the hemorrhaging woman who could not enter the temple courts. In each case, Jesus does not simply heal and move on. He reinstates. He restores the person to full participation in community life, because the exclusion they have suffered is not merely physical. It is social, religious, economic. The healing is not only of the body. It is of a system that has used the body as a reason for exclusion.

The man born blind in John 9 is the clearest case. Before Jesus touches him, the disciples ask: "Who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" (John 9:2). The question assumes that disability is punishment — that the condition of the body is a theological verdict, a sign of divine disfavor. Jesus refuses the premise: "Neither this man nor his parents sinned." The blindness is not moral judgment. It is the location where God's work will be made visible. The rebuke of the disciples' question is a rebuke of the entire framework that treats disability as stigma.

The early church seems to have grasped this with more clarity than its descendants. Paul's description of the body of Christ in 1 Corinthians 12 is deliberate: the members that seem weaker are indispensable, the parts we think less honorable we treat with special honor (1 Corinthians 12:22-23). This is not sentimentality. It is ecclesiology — a claim about what the church is. The body of Christ cannot be fully itself if it is composed only of the conventionally able. The exclusion of people with disabilities is not a minor oversight in church programming. It is a distortion of the body.

The counter-position that requires honest engagement is this: the church's primary task is spiritual, and physical accessibility concerns, while important, are not theological matters in the strict sense. The church can love people with disabilities spiritually even in buildings that are not fully accessible; the accessibility is a practical, not a theological, question.

This argument underestimates what architecture communicates. The philosopher Charles Taylor has argued that our moral frameworks are "embedded in practices, institutions, and ways of relating" [cite — confirm] — that what we believe is expressed and reinforced by what we build and how we organize. The church building that is inaccessible to wheelchair users does not merely inconvenience them. It communicates a message, however unintentionally, about who the gathering is designed for and who is an afterthought. The message is received. People with disabilities have been receiving it for a long time.

I have visited churches with no sign-language interpretation where Deaf people sat through services designed entirely for the hearing. I have been in buildings where a person using a mobility device could not reach the communion table. I have watched programs for children with cognitive disabilities treated as a ministry for the few — a niche, a specialty — rather than a standard expectation of what the church is for. Each of these is an architectural and programmatic choice. Every architectural and programmatic choice is a theological statement.

What would it mean for the church to take *imago Dei* seriously enough to make disability justice a design requirement rather than an afterthought? It would mean consultation with disabled people in the planning stages, not after the building is built. It would mean hiring people with disabilities into positions of actual authority, not only into the programs designed for them. It would mean sermons that name the theological stakes — that say plainly: the exclusion of a person who bears the image of God is not a facilities problem. It is a sin against the one whose image that person carries.

I am learning this later than I should have. The man in the wheelchair near the back of my sanctuary has been a patient teacher. His patience is greater than I deserve. He enters through the side door because I built — or inherited, and did not change — a building that required it.

The kingdom of God, in the vision of Isaiah and the announcement of Jesus, includes the blind seeing and the lame walking — not as the elimination of disability, but as the restoration of full participation in the life of God's people (Isaiah 35:5-6; Luke 7:22). That vision begins here. It begins with who we build for. It begins with who we design our gatherings around. It begins with whose presence we treat as indispensable rather than accommodated.

The image of God arrives in every body. The question is whether the church is ready to receive it — through the front door.

Amos Yong, a Pentecostal theologian who grew up with a brother with Down syndrome, argues that disability theology is not a subspecialty of theology but a site where the whole of theology must be reconsidered [cite — confirm]. The church's systematic theology was developed, largely, by men whose bodies were not the subject of the conversation — who could assume their own capacity as the norm and everyone else's difference as the variation. When you center the person with a disability in the theological conversation, the conversation changes. The question of what it means to be made in God's image cannot be answered abstractly. It must be answered in the presence of specific bodies, specific stories, specific exclusions.

Nancy Eiesland's *The Disabled God* (1994) proposed a theology of disability rooted in the resurrection appearance of Jesus — who appears to Thomas not with a glorified body that has transcended injury, but with wounds still visible, still available to touch (John 20:27) [cite — confirm]. The risen Christ is not a Christ whose scars have been erased. He is a Christ who carries them into the new creation. This has implications for how we understand disability in relation to the eschaton: not the elimination of all difference, but the presence of difference in a community where it is no longer the basis of exclusion.

The person with a disability in your congregation is not waiting to be healed before she can participate fully in the life of God's people. She is already bearing the image of God. The question is whether the church's architecture and practice are adequate to receive what she brings.

Most of the time, they are not. The answer is not despair. The answer is the same answer it always is when the church has failed to follow its own theology to its conclusions: repentance, which is the turning around that makes movement toward faithfulness possible.

The building can be modified. The liturgy can be made accessible. The programs can be redesigned. These are not primarily architectural or logistical questions. They are theological ones, and the answer to them begins with the confession that we have excluded people God told us were indispensable.

That confession is overdue. It is also the beginning of something better.
