The congregation you serve is not one thing. It is a room full of people who disagree about almost everything that dominates the news cycle, who love each other anyway — at least on Sunday mornings — and who are quietly waiting to find out which side you are on. That is the trap. The moment you step into the partisan frame, you have already lost something that is very difficult to get back.

But the alternative is not silence. Silence from the pulpit on the great moral dislocations of the age is not pastoral prudence. It is abdication.

The prophetic tradition offers us a third way, and it is harder than either of the popular options.

---

We have been here before. The American church has stood at the intersection of faith and politics in every generation of its existence, and it has not always distinguished itself. The colonial pulpit provided theological cover for the revolutionary cause in ways that blurred the boundary between the kingdom of God and the American project in ways we are still sorting out. The antebellum church split over slavery — not over whether Scripture spoke to the question, but over what it said and who it served. In the early twentieth century, figures like Walter Rauschenbusch insisted that the gospel had social and structural implications that the revivalist tradition had been suppressing. In the mid-twentieth century, Billy Graham negotiated an uneasy relationship with every president from Eisenhower to Obama, demonstrating at great cost what happens when the prophetic voice gets too comfortable with power.

Then came the political sorting of the 1970s and 1980s, when the Religious Right wed evangelical Christianity so thoroughly to the Republican coalition that the two became functionally indistinguishable in the public imagination. And then the backlash — the progressive wing of the church leaning so completely toward the Democratic platform that it mirrored the same error from the other direction. By the early twenty-first century, the American church had managed to make itself legible almost entirely in partisan terms, which meant that the actual claims of the gospel — which cut across every coalition — were being drowned out by the background noise of the culture war.

That is the situation the pastor in 2026 inherits. It is not neutral territory.

---

Amos did not endorse a political party. He named the specific economic arrangements that were crushing the poor — the merchants who sold the needy for a pair of sandals (Amos 2:6), the women of Bashan who crushed the poor and commanded their husbands to bring more wine (Amos 4:1). He spoke at Bethel, the royal sanctuary, directly to the power structures of the northern kingdom. He was expelled for it (Amos 7:12-13). His speech was not ideologically neutral. It was theologically precise. There is a difference.

The pastor who conflates political precision with theological cowardice has misread the prophets entirely. Prophetic speech names what God names, with the vocabulary God uses: the widow, the orphan, the stranger, the poor. It does not map cleanly onto a party platform. Both parties spend heavily on things that do not feed the hungry. Neither party has a monopoly on the betrayal of the vulnerable. When you preach from the text rather than from a news feed, you will find yourself making people uncomfortable in ways that cut across every aisle in the room — and that is exactly where the pulpit is supposed to stand.

Isaiah 58 indicts the religious community not for false doctrine but for the gap between their liturgical performance and their actual treatment of the hungry, the homeless, the captive. "Is not this the fast that I choose: to loose the bonds of wickedness, to undo the straps of the yoke, to let the oppressed go free?" (Isaiah 58:6). The preacher who reads that text in the twenty-first century does not need to name a party. He needs to hold his congregation accountable to the question: are we doing this? That question is not partisan. It is prophetic.

---

I have gotten this wrong. I have avoided the hard text because I knew it would disturb someone I did not want to disturb. I have preached applications so carefully triangulated they reached no one. I have also, on worse days, let something slip from the pulpit that sounded more like a political point than a gospel claim. Both failures carry the same root: I was managing the room instead of serving it.

The congregation does not need a pastor who tells them what to think about the next election. It needs a pastor who holds them accountable to the kingdom. Those are not the same thing.

Here is what I have learned about the difference. Theological speech names the idols — power, security, consumption, ethnic supremacy, the reduction of human beings to their utility — regardless of which political tradition has adopted them this election cycle. Political speech names the candidates, endorses the platforms, and shrinks the gospel to fit the current coalition. The former builds a congregation that can think. The latter builds a congregation that will split.

The pastor who wants to address political division without destroying the church must become fluent in the distinction between the proximate political cause and the deeper theological disorder. Immigration is in the news. The stranger in your midst is in Leviticus 19:34 and Deuteronomy 10:19. You do not need to solve immigration policy from the pulpit. You do need to hold your people accountable to the God who said, "Love the foreigner as yourself, for you were foreigners in Egypt." Let them sit with the text. The text does the work.

Economic inequality is in the news. Proverbs 14:31 says that whoever oppresses the poor insults his Maker, and Proverbs 22:22 says do not rob the poor because they are poor. Luke 4 records Jesus announcing his mission in terms that are unmistakably structural: good news to the poor, release to the captives, sight to the blind, freedom for the oppressed. You do not need to endorse a tax policy. You do need to ask whether your congregation's engagement with wealth aligns with what the New Testament actually requires.

---

There is a pastoral objection worth taking seriously here. Some pastors, reading the prophetic tradition, become convinced that every sermon must have a political application. They shift from one kind of partisanship to another — trading the culture-war sermon for the progressive-activist sermon, and leaving the congregation no more formed by the gospel and considerably more alienated from each other. This is a real failure mode, and it is worth naming honestly.

The prophets were not policy analysts. They were not primarily interested in producing correct political opinions in their hearers. They were interested in the posture of the heart before God — the posture that produces right action, including right civic action. The congregation formed by the Beatitudes will engage politics differently than the congregation formed by talk radio. But the sermon whose goal is political formation rather than theological formation has confused the instrument for the end.

Preach the text. Preach the whole text. Let it implicate everyone in the room, including you. Trust the Spirit to apply it. That is the work. It is not safer than partisan preaching — the Sermon on the Mount is deeply threatening to every political position ever devised — but it is faithful to something that the partisan sermon never can be.

---

What ruins churches is not theological seriousness. It is pastoral cowardice dressed as neutrality and pastoral partisanship dressed as prophecy. The congregation can tell the difference between a pastor who is afraid and a pastor who is faithful. They respect the latter even when they disagree with him.

You will not keep everyone. Some people in your congregation have so thoroughly fused their political identity with their faith that any theological challenge to either will feel like an attack on both. They will leave when you preach the Sermon on the Mount without the caveats they require. Some will leave from one direction; some from the other. That departure is painful. It is not failure.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer — who understood better than most of us what it costs to refuse the political captivity of the gospel — wrote in his letters from Tegel prison that the church's task was to speak to the world in ways the world could not hear from itself. He also wrote that cheap grace, grace without demand, was the enemy of the church. [cite — confirm] The demand he had in mind was the demand of the cross — the cross that stands over every political arrangement and refuses to be domesticated by any of them.

The pulpit is not a podium. It is an office. What it requires is not courage in the abstract — it requires the specific courage to say what the text says to the people in the room, without checking the polls first. Without calculating what the major donors can tolerate. Without managing the anxiety of the board. With the full weight of the prophetic tradition behind you, and the full weight of the pastoral tradition in front of you, knowing that both are required and neither is enough alone.

---

There is a congregation on the other side of this. Not a politically uniform congregation — those congregations are not churches, they are caucuses — but a congregation that has learned to hold its political convictions loosely enough to hold its brothers and sisters tightly. A congregation that disagrees on candidates and policies and has decided that the brother across the aisle is more important than the victory in November. A congregation that has been formed by the text to see the stranger, the poor, the captive — and to ask what faithfulness requires of them in a specific time and place.

That congregation is built over years of faithful preaching that refuses both silence and partisanship. It is built by a pastor who has learned to love the room enough to trouble it.

Everything else is management. And the church does not need more managers.
