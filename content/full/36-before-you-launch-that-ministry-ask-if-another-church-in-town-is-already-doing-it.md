There is a particular kind of pastoral excitement that accompanies the vision for a new ministry initiative. The need is clear, the gifting is present, the timing feels right, and the energy in the room when the idea is presented confirms that something real is being tapped. The next steps are obvious: form a team, develop a plan, build a budget, launch.

Before those next steps happen, there is a question worth asking that pastoral culture almost never asks: Is someone else already doing this? Not as a reason to abandon the idea — the need may be genuinely larger than any one church can meet — but as a starting point that might lead somewhere much more effective than a separate launch.

## Why Churches Do Not Ask

The failure to ask this question is not laziness. It is structural, and the structure runs deep.

American evangelicalism has been shaped by a broadly competitive ecclesiology — a view of church life in which each congregation is primarily accountable to its own vision and its own growth, in which the church across town is, at best, a sister institution and, at worst, a competitor for the same population of potential attenders. The language of "our mission" and "our calling" reinforces this frame. The instinct to launch rather than to collaborate is the natural product of a culture in which the success of the individual congregation is the primary unit of measurement.

This competitive ecclesiology is not theologically neutral. It assumes that the church in a given city is best understood as a collection of discrete organizations rather than as a single body of Christ that has taken multiple institutional forms. The Pauline vision in 1 Corinthians 12 — where the body's diversity of gifts is precisely the evidence of its unity, where each member's function serves the whole rather than competing with it — does not fit easily into an ecclesiology where each congregation's ministry is defined primarily by its own vision rather than by its relationship to the larger mission.

The pastor who launches a food pantry without asking whether the church six blocks away already operates one has not sinned. But he has operated from an assumption about the church's structure that is worth examining.

## Why Duplicate Ministry Exists

Duplicate ministry exists primarily because churches do not know what each other is doing. The food pantry that three different churches operate independently in the same neighborhood — none of them large enough to have real impact, each of them proudly featured in its own church's announcements — are a collective resource that, if pooled, would produce a genuinely effective community resource. The afterschool tutoring program at your church and the identical program at the church six blocks away are each running at partial capacity while the need in the neighborhood remains unmet. The duplication is not malicious. It is the product of churches operating in silos, each pursuing its own vision without reference to what the broader church community is already doing.

The waste is real. A 2020 survey of urban ministry organizations found that duplication of services was consistently identified by community organizations as one of the primary inefficiencies in faith-based community engagement [cite — confirm]. The financial resources, the volunteer hours, the organizational energy that go into maintaining duplicate programs could instead be directed toward meeting needs that are currently going unmet.

And beyond the efficiency argument, there is the witness argument. The watching city sees not a unified community of people who love it, but a collection of churches each promoting its own programs in competition with each other. That is not the witness the gospel intends the church to provide.

## The Pastoral Scene

A pastor named Kevin told me the story of how he spent fourteen months developing a recovery ministry for people coming out of addiction — building the curriculum, recruiting the volunteers, designing the space, building the budget. Three weeks before the launch, he received a call from a pastor across town who had heard about the program and called to ask whether they might want to partner.

That pastor had been running an identical program for four years. Kevin had not known it existed.

The conversation that followed was, by Kevin's account, one of the most uncomfortable and most important conversations of his ministry. The other pastor was gracious. The redundancy was clear. The question of what to do with it was genuinely difficult — Kevin had invested enormous energy and his congregation had significant ownership of the program they had helped develop.

They merged them. It took six months of negotiation and produced a program that serves three times the number of participants that either of the original programs could have served alone. Kevin told me that the merger produced something unexpected: his own congregation's sense of mission expanded. They were no longer doing their program — they were part of something larger than themselves.

## The Practical Case for Asking First

Asking the question — "Is anyone else already doing this?" — requires a level of pastoral relationship with other churches in the community that many pastors simply do not have. You cannot ask the question if you do not know the people to ask. This is one of the most practical and concrete arguments for investing in pastoral relationships across congregational lines: they make you more effective at your own church's mission by connecting you to the broader ecosystem of ministry in your community.

The conversation that begins with "we're thinking about launching X — are you doing something similar?" can lead in several directions, all of them more productive than the default silo launch. It might reveal that the other church is indeed doing the same thing and would welcome partnership or expanded capacity. It might reveal that they tried and found it did not work, which saves you from a predictable failure. It might reveal that you are filling a gap they have identified but not been able to address, opening the door to genuine collaboration from the beginning rather than after the fact.

The Pastors Connection Network exists, in part, to create the relational infrastructure that makes this kind of asking possible. Pastors who know each other can ask each other questions that strangers cannot ask. The relationship is the prerequisite.

## When to Launch Anyway

After doing the survey, there will sometimes be a clear answer that no, nobody else is doing this, and the launch is warranted. Sometimes the existing ministry is genuinely insufficient — it does not have the capacity to meet the need, or it serves a different population, or it approaches the work in a way that is genuinely inadequate to what the situation requires. In these cases, launching is the right call.

The steel-manned objection to the general approach here is worth stating directly: the command to pursue the mission does not include a prior obligation to check whether someone else is already pursuing it. The Spirit moves through multiple channels simultaneously. The fact that someone else is already running a food pantry does not mean your congregation is not also called to feed people, and forcing collaboration on congregations with different theological frameworks, different leadership cultures, and different relationships with the community may actually produce less effective ministry than two separate programs with genuine ownership from two separate communities.

This objection has real merit. Forced collaboration is worse than no collaboration. The goal is not a unified ministry bureaucracy in which every church must check with every other church before it can act. The goal is a pastoral culture in which the default question before launch is whether the mission is already being served and whether a relationship exists that could produce something better than separate action.

Ask first. Then decide. The church that has developed the habit of asking — of situating its own ministry vision within the broader context of what the church in its city is already doing — tends to produce ministry that is more effective, better resourced, and more genuinely collaborative than the church that operates as though its own ideas are always the beginning of the story.

The city is the mission. Every church in it is a partner in that mission, whether the partnership is acknowledged or not. Acknowledging it changes what is possible.

Make the call before you build the budget. What already exists out there might be exactly what you were going to build, and the pastor who built it has been waiting for someone like you to ask.

There is a deeper issue here that the efficiency argument does not fully capture. The church that perpetually reinvents what already exists has, in some fundamental sense, decided that its own vision is more important than the mission's outcomes. That is a form of institutional pride dressed as pastoral initiative. The question "is someone already doing this?" is not a bureaucratic checkpoint. It is an act of humility — the acknowledgment that the Spirit may have been at work in this city before your congregation arrived at the idea, through people you have not yet met, in a ministry that is already serving the people you want to serve. Finding it is not a defeat. It is an invitation. Take it.
