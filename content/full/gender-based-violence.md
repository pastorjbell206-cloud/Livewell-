The statistics exist. They have existed for decades. In the United States, approximately one in four women will experience severe intimate partner violence in her lifetime. [cite — confirm] Globally, the World Health Organization estimates that roughly 35 percent of women have experienced either intimate partner violence or non-partner sexual violence at some point in their lives. [cite — confirm] The numbers are not improving at any meaningful rate. They describe a pattern of harm so consistent, so widespread, and so embedded in the structures of intimate life that it cannot be understood as a series of individual failures. It must be understood as a system.

The church has not wanted to understand it as a system. The reasons are not hard to find.

To name gender-based violence as a system requires asking questions about the cultural soil in which it grows. It requires examining the theology of headship and submission as it has been taught and practiced, and asking whether some versions of that theology have provided ideological cover for the exercise of coercive control. It requires hearing survivors — women who have sat in our pews, who have come to us for pastoral care, who have been sent home to unsafe situations by pastors who prioritized the appearance of marital stability over the physical safety of the woman in front of them. It requires the church to consider whether its own institutional patterns of male authority, combined with its theological culture of forgiveness and reconciliation, have created conditions in which abusers thrive and victims are silenced.

These are not comfortable questions. They have, in many ecclesiastical contexts, been treated as attacks on Scripture or on the family rather than as pastoral assessments of what the Scripture itself demands. That response is its own indictment.

The biblical witness on violence against the vulnerable is not ambiguous. The prophets are relentless on the defense of those who cannot defend themselves. Ezekiel condemns the leaders of Israel specifically for having used violence against the people in their care: "You have not strengthened the weak, healed the sick, or bound up the injured. You have not brought back the strays or searched for the lost. You have ruled them harshly and brutally" (Ezekiel 34:4). The pastoral metaphor is explicit. Those who were given the authority of shepherds over the flock used that authority to harm rather than protect. This is a text about governance. It is also a text about every relationship structured by authority and trust — including marriage, including pastors and congregants, including every context where power differentials exist.

The law of the Hebrew Bible contains specific provisions for the protection of women in conditions of vulnerability. The legislation in Deuteronomy about divorce, about the treatment of women taken in war, about the conditions under which wives could be put away — these texts require careful handling and their patriarchal assumptions must be acknowledged honestly. But running through the Mosaic legislation is a consistent thread: the law constrains the exercise of male authority. It does not simply authorize it. The law that protected the female slave, the captive woman, the divorced wife — imperfectly by our standards, but present against the backdrop of surrounding cultures that offered no such protection — was doing something specific: it was marking a limit on what the powerful could do to the vulnerable.

Proverbs 31 closes the book with a portrait of female excellence that the tradition has often read as a domestic ideal, but its closing verse makes the evaluative criterion explicit: "Give her the fruit of her hands; let her works praise her at the gates" (Proverbs 31:31). The gates were the place of public legal adjudication. The woman of Proverbs 31 is being praised in the space where justice is dispensed. Her excellence is not merely domestic. It is civic, public, recognized in the arena of covenant community life.

And then there is Tamar, in 2 Samuel 13. Her half-brother Amnon rapes her. Her father King David is enraged but does nothing. Her full brother Absalom tells her to keep quiet about it, and Tamar — who has just been violated — lives "a desolate woman" in her brother's house (2 Samuel 13:20). The text names this with a specificity that should disturb any reader who takes the Scripture seriously. It does not moralize the situation into resolution. It shows us the desolation. It names Absalom's eventual murder of Amnon as the consequence of institutional failure — the failure of a father-king who should have acted and did not. The text is not endorsing the silence. It is recording the cost of the silence, paid by Tamar, in her body and in her life.

I have sat across from women who told me what happened in their homes. I have also — and this is harder to say — been in conversations where the institutional pressure was toward keeping the family together, toward counseling rather than protection, toward the language of redemption before the conditions of safety were established. I participated in a pastoral culture that did not always ask first whether the woman in front of us was safe. That is not a neutral failure. It has a name.

The theological framework that has most damaged the church's response to gender-based violence is a misreading of the theology of suffering and sacrifice. When a woman in an abusive marriage is counseled to endure because suffering has redemptive value, when she is told that her cross is her marriage and Christ calls her to carry it, when the language of covenant faithfulness is deployed to prevent her from accessing safety — the church has weaponized the theology of the cross. It has turned an instrument of liberation into an instrument of captivity.

The cross is not a template for absorbing the violence of the powerful. The cross is God absorbing the violence of the powerful so that the vulnerable do not have to. This distinction is not a minor adjustment. It is the difference between a theology that protects abusers and a theology that names abuse as the violation of the image of God in the person being harmed.

Paul's instruction to husbands in Ephesians 5 — to love their wives "as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25) — places the weight of sacrifice on the one with authority, not on the one with less. The hermeneutic that has read this passage primarily as a warrant for female submission, while attending only minimally to the demands it places on the husband, has inverted the text. The sacrificial love demanded of the husband in Ephesians 5 is not a pleasant aspiration. It is a structural claim that the one with authority is required to use that authority for the benefit and protection of the more vulnerable party.

Repentance here is institutional, not only individual. The church that has protected its own reputation by silencing survivors, that has required reconciliation before safety, that has treated the leaving of an abusive marriage as a greater sin than the abuse itself, that has refused to name coercive control as an abuse of power — that church owes a specific and public acknowledgment of what it has done. Not as a therapeutic exercise. As truth-telling. As the precondition for genuine change.

What change looks like is specific. It looks like training — mandatory, recurring training for all pastoral staff in the recognition of domestic abuse and intimate partner violence, including coercive control, which leaves no visible marks and is among the most dangerous patterns. It looks like formal protocols for pastoral response that prioritize safety before reconciliation. It looks like the congregation becoming a community where survivors can speak and be believed, where the question "are you safe?" is asked before the question "how is your marriage?" It looks like the pulpit naming this from time to time without euphemism, not as a social program but as a covenantal obligation.

The woman who is unsafe in her home on Sunday night and sitting in a pew Sunday morning is not there by accident. She came, often, because the church has told her it stands for human dignity, for the protection of the vulnerable, for a vision of life together shaped by the love that gives itself up rather than taking by force.

Whether she finds that church or finds a different one depends on decisions that are within our authority to make.

The church that becomes a place of genuine safety will not look primarily different on the outside. The worship will be recognizable. The theology will be orthodox. What will be different is the texture of trust — the sense that a person carrying a hidden weight can say what the weight is and not be sent home to carry it alone. That texture does not form through a policy. It forms through the repeated experience of being believed, protected, and held by a community whose stated theology and actual practice are the same.

The desolate woman in 2 Samuel 13 is still waiting.

We are not required to make her wait any longer.
