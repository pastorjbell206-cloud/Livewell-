The congregation I stood before had people who despised each other's politics. Not quietly, the way polite people manage these things — but openly, with the specific contempt that comes from years of algorithm-fed outrage. They were in the same pews. They took communion from the same table. And after the service, some of them could barely bring themselves to shake hands.

I am not describing a church that failed. I am describing almost every church in America right now.

The political division in the American church is real, and it is deep, and it did not arrive from nowhere. It arrived from a decades-long drift in which the church allowed its primary identity categories to be supplied by the political culture rather than by the theological tradition. When Christians are sorted by political affiliation more reliably than by theological conviction — when exit polling shows that church attendance predicts voting behavior more consistently than doctrinal confession does [cite — confirm] — the church has a deeper problem than its policy disagreements. It has allowed itself to be colonized by a foreign framework for what matters most.

---

The standard pastoral advice when this topic comes up is to avoid politics from the pulpit, to stay in the safe center, to speak in broad enough terms that no one feels targeted. I understand the impulse. I have felt it. But that posture has a cost that pastors rarely name: it trains the congregation to believe the Gospel is compatible with any political allegiance, that following Jesus requires no specific reordering of loyalties, that the Kingdom of God is a spiritual reality with no purchase on Monday morning.

The congregation that hears that for long enough will eventually organize its life around something else. And in this cultural moment, that something else is politics.

Here is the harder truth: the politically divided congregation is often a symptom of Gospel-thin preaching, not a management problem to be handled with careful neutrality. The pastor who has never preached the Sermon on the Mount to its actual economic edge. Who has never asked the congregation to examine their spending against the parable of the rich young ruler. Who preaches eternal salvation every week but rarely asks what it would look like to live, right now, as if the Kingdom of God is real and present and making claims on the household budget and the community's obligations to the poor.

That pastor has not avoided politics. He has chosen a politics — the politics of the status quo — by declining to disturb it.

This is not a comfortable thing to say, and I include myself in the indictment. There have been seasons of my ministry when I softened the political edges of the Gospel to keep the peace — when I knew the text demanded more than I was willing to preach. That is not neutrality. It is cowardice dressed in pastoral language.

---

The Gospel is not politically neutral. This needs to be said carefully, because it can be misused in both directions. The Kingdom of God is not the Democratic Party platform, and it is not the Republican Party platform, and the pastor who identifies it with either has committed theological error of a serious kind. But the Kingdom of God is also not content to leave the political order undisturbed. It is, from its first announcement by Jesus in Luke 4 — good news to the poor, release to the captives, recovery of sight to the blind, liberation for the oppressed — a declaration that the existing arrangement is subject to revision by the incoming reign of God.

The first-century listeners who heard that announcement understood immediately that it was political. The authorities who crucified Jesus understood it was political. The early church that proclaimed Jesus as Lord — a title that belonged, in Roman imperial theology, to Caesar — understood it was political. The claim was not that Christianity was one political option among others. The claim was that there is a King whose rule supersedes every earthly political claim and whose character defines justice in ways that earthly powers may conform to or resist, but cannot supersede.

This is the framework. Not a party. Not a platform. A Lord whose kingship relativizes every other claim to ultimate loyalty.

The church that preaches this clearly will frustrate people on both sides. That is, historically speaking, a reliable sign of faithfulness. It frustrated Herod and it frustrated the Pharisees. It frustrated the Roman authorities and the Jewish establishment simultaneously, which is one of the more remarkable things about Jesus's ministry and a reasonable benchmark for whether the contemporary church is actually preaching him.

---

What this requires in practice is not the avoidance of political topics but the consistent proclamation of what Scripture actually says about power, about the poor, about the neighbor, about the stranger, about the use of wealth — and the willingness to let those texts land where they land, regardless of who is sitting in the room.

A congregation that has heard the Sermon on the Mount preached straight for five consecutive years will not agree on every policy question, but it will have a common framework that transcends party. They will know that the blessed are the poor in spirit, not the culturally powerful. That the merciful receive mercy. That those who hunger and thirst for justice — not merely personal righteousness but the social ordering that reflects God's character — will be satisfied. They will have absorbed a vision of the human community that is simply incompatible with contempt, with indifference to the vulnerable, with the logic of the strong having no obligation to the weak.

That framework is the work. Building it is slower than conflict management. It requires preaching the uncomfortable texts rather than the confirming ones. It requires the kind of consistency that does not let either side off the hook — the texts that make conservatives uncomfortable and the texts that make progressives uncomfortable, in the same year, without flinching at either.

Some practical notes. The pastor who addresses only the justice texts that challenge the right and never the identity texts that challenge the left has taken a side, whatever his intentions. The pastor who challenges wealth from the pulpit but never addresses the breakdown of the family has taken a side. The credibility of prophetic preaching depends on its consistency — on the congregation's lived experience that the pastor is not a partisan who invokes Scripture selectively, but a preacher who actually believes the whole counsel of God has something to say to everyone.

---

You will lose some people. Let me be honest about that.

Some people have fused their political identity so completely with their spiritual identity that any prophetic challenge to one feels like an assault on the other. They have built a coherent worldview in which the party they support is the faithful party and the party they oppose is the apostate one, and they have recruited sufficient Scripture to defend the coherent worldview without much difficulty because Scripture can be recruited to almost anything if you work at it. Preaching the Kingdom seriously to such a person does not feel like a return to Scripture. It feels like the pastor taking the other side.

You cannot pastor that out of them in a single series. Sometimes you preach the text, they experience it as partisan, and they leave. That is painful. It is also not a failure of your leadership. It is the normal cost of preaching the Kingdom in a nation that wants a chaplain.

The question is not whether you will lose people by preaching the whole Gospel. You will. The question is what you are building with the people who stay. A congregation formed on the steady proclamation of the Kingdom — who have had their categories stretched by sustained engagement with what Jesus actually taught — that congregation is building something. Not agreement on every policy question. But a common center that is stronger than their differences.

---

The relational dimension matters more than the preaching, and this is easy to underestimate.

Brené Brown has written that shared vulnerability is the precondition for genuine connection, and whatever one makes of her broader project, this observation is pastorally exact [cite — confirm]. The politically divided congregation cannot be argued into unity. It can, in some cases, be loved into it — loved through the slow work of relationships that refuse to honor the political boundary as a final one. That work begins with the pastor but cannot be confined to him.

Consider who you actually eat with. Whether your relationships model across-the-aisle connections in practice or only in principle. Whether the congregation has experiences — shared service to the poor, shared engagement with the community, shared suffering through grief — that create the common ground that preaching can build on but cannot manufacture by itself. The unity that transcends political division is not primarily a cognitive achievement. It is a relational one, grown through time and shared life and the repeated, costly choice to remain in relationship with people who organize their politics differently than you do.

This is the long work. There is no sermon series that accomplishes it in six weeks. But it is the work the Gospel requires, and the pastor who does it faithfully is doing something more durable than conflict management. He is building the thing that conflict management cannot build: a community that knows what it shares is more permanent than what divides it.

---

Name the Gospel explicitly and often. Call the congregation to primary loyalty to Christ above every other allegiance, and then trust the Spirit to do what only the Spirit can do.

That last part is not a resignation from leadership. It is the recognition that the Spirit's work in forming a people is not finally contingent on the pastor's program management skills. You preach. You stay faithful. You love across the divide. And you hold the long view that the Kingdom of God is coming and that the community that has organized itself around that coming has chosen the most durable thing available.

That is not political neutrality. That is the Gospel. They are not the same thing. Do not confuse them. Do not let the congregation confuse them. Preach into the confusion with patience and with conviction that the one who said "on this rock I will build my church" (Matthew 16:18) was not joking about the foundation.

The church was not built on a party platform. It will not fall because one party wins and one loses. It will stand or fall by whether it is actually organized around the King.

Keep preaching the King.
