I want to be honest about how skeptical I was going in. Reformed theology and charismatic practice have been on opposite ends of a particular theological divide for most of their modern histories, and the conversations between representatives of the two traditions have often generated more heat than light. The cessationist and the continuationist have met at many tables over the years, and the encounters have rarely ended in genuine breakthrough.

What I did not expect was what actually happened when the Pastors Connection Network brought a group of us together — Reformed Baptist pastors, Word of Faith-adjacent charismatics, classical Pentecostals, and a few people who described their theology as "post-charismatic but not cessationist, which I realize doesn't make sense" — to simply spend time together and talk honestly.

## The Long History of a Particular Divide

The charismatic-cessationist divide is not as old as it feels. Classical Pentecostalism emerged in the early twentieth century — most historians trace its institutional birth to the Azusa Street Revival of 1906 in Los Angeles, which produced a form of worship and a set of convictions about the ongoing gifts of the Spirit that spread rapidly across racial and economic lines in a way that surprised everyone, including those at its center [cite — confirm]. The Reformed tradition, by contrast, had been working out its cessationist convictions since at least Benjamin Warfield's systematic arguments in the late nineteenth century, and the encounter between these two streams produced genuine suspicion on both sides.

The charismatic renewal of the 1960s and 1970s moved the gifts of the Spirit into mainline Protestant and Catholic communities, which raised the stakes further. By the time the "Third Wave" of the Spirit associated with John Wimber and the Vineyard movement emerged in the 1980s, the conversation had been going on for nearly a century and had accumulated layers of caricature on both sides — the Reformed tradition seen by charismatics as dry, intellectualist, and practically cessationist out of fear rather than conviction; the charismatic tradition seen by Reformed pastors as emotionally manipulative, theologically shallow, and prone to the kind of subjectivism that makes genuine accountability impossible.

These caricatures, like most caricatures, contained enough truth to be plausible and enough distortion to be unjust.

## The First Obstacle: We Expected to Debate

Almost everyone in the room arrived expecting a theological negotiation — the careful positioning of positions, the finding of areas of agreement while clearly marking the disagreements. This is the default mode for ecumenical conversations between traditions that have real differences, and it produces a kind of theological diplomacy that is usually polite and rarely productive.

What disrupted the expected format was the decision to begin not with theology but with story. We went around the table and each person shared, for five minutes, how they had come to faith and what had shaped their understanding of the Holy Spirit. These were not position papers. They were human beings talking about encounters with God that had been definitive for them.

Something shifted. The cessationist heard the Pentecostal describe an experience of healing that bore no resemblance to the televangelism he had associated with the tradition. The charismatic heard the Reformed pastor describe a life of extraordinary fruitfulness built on careful, disciplined engagement with Scripture that did not fit the stereotype of dead orthodoxy. Before the theological questions were even on the table, the categories had already begun to break down.

## A Steel-Manned Counter-Position

The strongest version of the case against this kind of cross-tradition gathering needs to be heard. It goes like this: the differences between cessationist and continuationist theology are not peripheral. They shape ecclesiology, they shape how the congregation is formed, they shape the criteria for evaluating spiritual experience, and they shape how the church reads the New Testament. Gathering for meals and sharing personal stories creates warmth without clarity, and warmth without clarity is not the same as unity. The unity the New Testament envisions is unity in truth, not merely in affection. A Reformed pastor and a Word of Faith pastor may enjoy each other's company. That enjoyment does not resolve the question of whether the prosperity gospel is a distortion of the gospel. Pretending it does is not ecumenism. It is avoidance dressed as friendship.

This is a real concern and it deserves a real answer. The answer is not that the theological differences do not matter. They do. They shape real pastoral practices and real congregational lives, and treating them as though they can be dissolved by personal warmth is intellectually dishonest.

The answer is that genuine theological engagement requires the kind of trust and goodwill that does not exist between parties who only know each other as theological positions. The relationship is not a substitute for the theological work. It is the precondition for it. The cessationist who has never met a Pentecostal who is theologically serious, personally disciplined, and genuinely humble before the Scripture is arguing against a caricature. The relationship breaks the caricature. What happens after that — the theological wrestling — can be genuine rather than performative precisely because the people in the room have stopped performing for each other.

## What the Disagreements Actually Were

The disagreements were real. We did not pretend otherwise. On the question of tongues, healing, prophecy, and the ongoing presence of the apostolic gifts, people in the room held genuinely different convictions rooted in genuinely different interpretations of Scripture and experience. These were not peripheral matters for any of us — they had shaped our ecclesiology, our discipleship practices, and our pastoral approaches in significant ways.

The most substantive exchange came on the question of how to evaluate claimed experiences of the Spirit — what the criteria are for distinguishing genuine from counterfeit, and who bears the authority to make that judgment in a community. The Reformed pastor argued from 1 John 4:1 — "test the spirits to see whether they are from God" — that the testing function belongs to the community's theological discernment and requires objective criteria that transcend individual experience. The Pentecostal pastor argued that the same text assumes the ongoing activity of the Spirit that the cessationist has already ruled out, and that the criteria for testing cannot preclude the kind of encounter the text envisions.

We did not resolve this. But in the context of the relationships forming across the table, the disagreement became something different: not walls, but honest differences between people who were genuinely trying to be faithful to the same God and the same Scriptures.

## What We Discovered We Had in Common

The list of shared convictions was longer than anyone expected: the authority of Scripture, the necessity of genuine repentance and faith for salvation, the centrality of prayer, the importance of genuine discipleship over mere attendance, the desperate need for genuine revival in the American church, and a deep weariness with the tribalism that had kept our traditions at arms' length from each other at precisely the moment when the mission most needed them to collaborate.

We also discovered shared struggles: the isolation of pastoral leadership, the challenge of reaching a post-Christian culture, the difficulty of sustaining a genuine prayer life in the midst of the demands of ministry. These were not theological conversations. They were human ones. And in the humanity, the barriers that the theology had built began to look more surmountable than they had before.

What Lesslie Newbigin observed about the ecumenical movement applies here: the unity of the church is not primarily achieved through theological agreement but through shared witness to the world [cite — confirm]. The mission creates the conditions for unity that the theological debates alone cannot. When Reformed and charismatic pastors discover that they are serving in the same city, working with the same underserved populations, and needing each other's resources and relationships to accomplish the work effectively, the theological differences become something to manage together rather than something to separate over.

## What Happened After the Table

The table did not resolve the theological differences. What it did was create the conditions for genuine friendship across those differences, and genuine friendship is the foundation on which real collaboration becomes possible. We have since partnered on community outreach in a shared city. A Reformed congregation and a Pentecostal congregation now share resources in a food ministry that neither could sustain alone. That would not have happened if we had not first sat down to eat.

There is something almost sacramental about that — the meal that precedes the mission, the shared table that makes the shared work possible. The table of Jesus in Luke 15 is open to the tax collector and the Pharisee, not because their differences do not matter but because they both needed what only the table could give. The mission begins there, at a table where the people who should not be together have chosen to be present to one another.

## What the PCN Has Learned

The Pastors Connection Network was not designed to produce theological consensus. It was designed to create the conditions for genuine pastoral community across the divisions that have kept pastors isolated from one another. The Reformed-charismatic gathering was one expression of that — an experiment in finding out whether genuine relationship was possible across a divide that both sides had assumed was too wide for anything but careful, guarded diplomacy.

It was possible. It is possible. Not because the theological differences are trivial, but because the mission is larger than the differences, and the isolation of pastoral life is lonelier than the disagreements are important.

The table is worth setting. Even when — especially when — the people around it are not supposed to get along.
