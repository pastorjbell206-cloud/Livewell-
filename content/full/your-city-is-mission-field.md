When Jeremiah wrote to the exiles in Babylon, he did not tell them to keep their distance from the city until the rescue came. He told them to build houses, plant gardens, marry, raise children — and to "seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare" (Jeremiah 29:7). Seek the shalom of Babylon. The place of captivity was also the place of calling.

This is the instruction that most of us have not fully received.

The word Jeremiah uses — *shalom* — carries more weight than the English "welfare" can hold. It does not mean simply the absence of conflict. It means the comprehensive flourishing of a community: right relationships, just structures, material provision, the kind of order in which people and creation can thrive together. The exiles were being instructed to invest themselves in the comprehensive flourishing of the city that had conquered them. Not to assimilate — Jeremiah's letter opens with commands that presuppose maintained identity. But to serve. To be present. To let their welfare be bound up with the welfare of the people around them.

That is the posture that missional theology, at its best, has tried to recover. And it is the posture that most American churches, in practice, have not adopted.

There is a particular version of Christian mission that locates the real work somewhere else — in the Global South, in the unreached people groups, in the countries where missionaries go and churches pray for and support financially. None of that is wrong. The global mission of the church is real and costly and necessary. What is wrong is the proportion — the amount of missiological imagination we pour into distant places relative to the amount we invest in the specific block where the church building sits.

I have sat in churches that sent teams to Africa while their immediate neighborhood had no Christian presence with any relationship to the people who actually lived there. I have sat with pastors who could name unreached people groups in Southeast Asia but could not name the dominant ethnic community within six blocks of their congregation's front door. I have been that pastor. I have been more conversant with global statistics than with the actual human beings whose daily life takes place in the shadow of our steeple.

The global mission does not excuse the local one. It actually depends on it. The New Testament pattern of mission is centrifugal — moving outward from the community where discipleship is practiced to the communities adjacent to it and then beyond. Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8). The ends of the earth are the culmination of the movement, not the substitute for the beginning. A church that skips Jerusalem and Judea for the ends of the earth has confused the direction of the arrow.

The historical pattern is instructive here. The remarkable expansion of the early church through the cities of the Roman Empire — Antioch, Corinth, Ephesus, Rome — happened not primarily through strategic missionary deployment but through the daily life of ordinary Christians whose neighbors noticed that they lived differently. Rodney Stark has argued, controversially but with evidence, that the early church's growth was substantially powered by its response to urban crises — the epidemics of the second and third centuries that drove wealthy Romans to flee the cities while Christians stayed to care for the sick and bury the dead [cite — confirm]. Whether or not one accepts all of Stark's argument, the basic pattern is well attested in early church sources: the church was present where others were absent. It was in the city. It was in the suffering. It was seen.

The local mission is harder than the distant one in a particular way. Distant mission allows a kind of abstraction — you send money, you pray for names, you support work you will never see and people you will never know. That abstraction is not nothing. But it can also insulate you from the specific, demanding, human work of loving your actual neighbors — who are complicated, who may not be interested in what you are offering, who know things about your neighborhood that you do not, who have needs that your current programming is not designed to address.

The counter-position deserves honest engagement: there are genuine reasons why the local can feel more elusive than the distant. The church that is embedded in a middle-class suburban neighborhood may be genuinely limited in its capacity to address urban poverty across the city. The distance is real. The cultural gap is real. Parachuting into a community you do not inhabit with solutions developed by people who do not live there can cause real harm, and some churches have learned to stay in their lane as a form of humility, not evasion.

True. And insufficient. The call to seek the shalom of the city does not require you to solve everything from a distance. It requires you to be actually present to the people God has placed around you — however proximate they are, however complex the relationship. You do not need to understand everything before you begin. You need to show up. You need to be a neighbor, in the sense that Jesus meant when he told the story of the man who stopped at the roadside: not the one who had the best theory of neighbor-hood, but the one who was actually there.

The work of loving your city begins with knowing it. Not knowing about it — the demographics and the statistics and the economic profile — but knowing it the way you know a person: slowly, through presence, through repeated encounters that build the kind of familiarity where real conversation becomes possible. It begins with the question that Nehemiah asked when he arrived in Jerusalem: what is the actual condition of this place? Not what I assume the condition to be, but what I see when I stop performing activity long enough to look (Nehemiah 2:11-13).

In most American cities, what you will see when you look is a profound isolation. Neighbors who do not know each other. Communities fractured along economic lines that the church building often reinforces rather than crosses. People in genuine need with no relationship to any institution with the capacity to help. And in the midst of that, a congregation that has the theological resources, the community structure, and the social presence to be a genuine force for human flourishing — if it is willing to direct those resources outward rather than inward.

The mission field is not abstract. It is the homeless man outside your building who sees the congregation arrive and depart each Sunday without eye contact. It is the apartment complex three streets over where no one from the congregation has ever been. It is the school where the neighborhood's children spend their days, where the teachers are desperate for partners. It is the small business owner who serves the neighborhood but does not know a single person in the church. It is the family that moved in eight months ago and has not spoken to a neighbor.

These are not secondary mission concerns while the real work happens elsewhere. These are the first call.

Leslie Newbigin spent decades arguing that the local congregation is the hermeneutic of the gospel — the primary way the world encounters what the good news is [cite — confirm]. The congregation does not primarily demonstrate the gospel by what it says. It demonstrates the gospel by what it is: a community of people who love each other across lines the world does not cross, who serve the neighborhood without requiring payment, who are present when presence is costly. The argument is not verbal. It is enacted.

That enactment begins with the decision to be actually present to the place you are in. To know it. To serve it without requiring it to come to you. To seek its shalom with the same seriousness that Jeremiah instructed the exiles to seek Babylon's welfare — because in its welfare, you will find your own.

The exiles in Babylon did not choose Babylon. They were sent. Most of us did not choose our zip codes for missiological reasons. We went where the housing was affordable, where the schools were acceptable, where our people were. We were sent anyway. The sending is real regardless of the original intention.

Go where you are. Look at what is actually there. Begin with that.

The ends of the earth can wait. Jerusalem cannot.
