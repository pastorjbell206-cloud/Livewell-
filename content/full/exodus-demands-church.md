Before there was a theology, there was a cry.

"And the people of Israel groaned because of their slavery and cried out for help. Their cry for rescue from slavery came up to God. And God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob. God saw the people of Israel — and God knew" (Exodus 2:23-25). Four verbs. Heard. Remembered. Saw. Knew. This is the first theological statement about who God is and how God operates — not a doctrine about divine attributes in the abstract but a record of divine response to human suffering in the concrete.

The Exodus is not just ancient history. It is the grammar of everything that follows.

---

The story begins in an empire. Pharaoh is not simply a bad king. He is the head of the most sophisticated civilization in the ancient Near East, a man who controls not just military force but the narrative — the official account of the world, who matters and who does not, what counts as a person and what counts as a resource. His system worked by making the labor of the enslaved invisible while making the monuments visible. The pyramids stood. The people who built them were counted as inputs, not as persons. The empire ran on the conversion of human beings into economic units.

Moses's call begins with a burning bush and ends with a demand: "Let my people go." That demand is not primarily about theology. It is about the specific, material condition of specific, material people in a specific, material situation. God does not tell Pharaoh to improve his values. God tells him to release his captives. The content of liberation is concrete. The enslaved must actually go free. The land must actually be crossed. The sea must actually part. Redemption in the Exodus tradition is not a spiritual transaction that leaves material conditions unchanged. It is a disruption of the existing order at every level — economic, political, social, symbolic.

This is what Walter Brueggemann calls the "prophetic imagination" [cite — confirm]: the capacity to see the existing social order not as natural or necessary but as a constructed arrangement that can be imagined differently. Pharaoh's power depended on his subjects believing that the existing arrangement was simply the way things were. Moses's message was that the existing arrangement was not the way things had to be — that there was a God more powerful than Pharaoh who had a different plan for these people, who had heard a cry that Pharaoh had organized the whole apparatus of empire to suppress.

---

The New Testament church received the Exodus as its own story. Paul, writing to a community of mostly Gentile believers in Corinth, says flatly: "Our fathers were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea, and all were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea" (1 Corinthians 10:1-2). Our fathers. He appropriates the Exodus narrative for people who were not ethnically Israelite because the pattern it reveals is not about ethnicity. It is about the God who liberates. The Christian who claims this God claims the history of this God — including the history of what this God has done with the enslaved and what this God requires of those who follow him into freedom.

Luke 4 makes the same move. Jesus, reading from Isaiah 61 in the synagogue at Nazareth, announces his own program in language drawn directly from the Exodus and Jubilee traditions: good news to the poor, release to the captives, recovery of sight to the blind, freedom for the oppressed (Luke 4:18). He does not treat these as metaphors. He treats them as descriptions of what the kingdom's arrival produces. When John's disciples ask from prison whether Jesus is the one who was to come, his answer is not a theological proposition — it is a list of concrete transformations: the blind see, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, the poor have good news proclaimed to them (Luke 7:22). The evidence of the kingdom is the reversal of the conditions the Exodus pattern named as incompatible with the character of God.

---

The church has always been tempted to spiritualize the Exodus — to make it a story about individual souls being freed from the slavery of sin, which is true as far as it goes but radically incomplete. The pattern the Exodus establishes is not only about interior liberation. It is about the God who enters history to disrupt arrangements of power that crush the vulnerable. The spiritual dimension and the material dimension are not in competition. The God who liberates souls from sin is the same God who heard the cry of those building bricks without straw, and he is not satisfied with a church that addresses the spiritual while ignoring the material, any more than he was satisfied with an Israel that offered sacrifices while consuming the poor.

This is the point Amos made to a prosperous Israel in the eighth century BC. This is the point Isaiah made to a confident Judah in Isaiah 58: "Is not this the fast that I choose: to loose the bonds of wickedness, to undo the straps of the yoke, to let the oppressed go free, and to break every yoke? Is it not to share your bread with the hungry and bring the homeless poor into your house?" (Isaiah 58:6-7). This is the point Jesus made in Matthew 25, where the criterion of judgment is not doctrinal correctness but whether the hungry were fed, the stranger welcomed, the naked clothed, the sick and imprisoned visited.

The Exodus demands a church that takes the cry of the oppressed as seriously as God takes it. Heard. Remembered. Saw. Knew. Four verbs that describe God's orientation toward human suffering. The church that represents this God inherits this orientation.

---

What does this demand look like concretely in the twenty-first century? The question is easier to avoid than it is to answer honestly.

Mass incarceration in America disproportionately affects Black men and poor communities at rates that have no parallel in the developed world [cite — confirm]. The United States imprisons more people per capita than any nation on earth. The carceral system functions, in many respects, the way the forced labor systems of antiquity functioned: it extracts productive years from people whose communities are already depleted, criminalizes poverty rather than addressing it, and produces generational damage that compounds over time. The church that reads Exodus and has nothing to say about mass incarceration has missed the point of the story it claims to be living inside.

Immigration enforcement separates families, detains children, deports people to countries they have not lived in since childhood. The same nation that produced the Statue of Liberty and the promise of refuge has periodically reorganized its immigration apparatus to function as an engine of fear for the most vulnerable people who arrive at its borders. Leviticus 19:34 is not ambiguous: "You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt." For you were strangers in Egypt. The Exodus is the reason for the command.

I am not proposing a partisan program. I am describing the pattern that the Scriptures themselves establish and demanding that we look at what that pattern requires of us. The specific policy questions are genuinely complex, require wisdom and prudence, and admit of disagreement among people who share the same theological convictions. But the pattern itself is not ambiguous. God hears the cry of the oppressed. God requires his people to hear it too.

---

The church has two temptations in response to the Exodus demand.

The first is to spiritualize it so thoroughly that it makes no material claims. This is the temptation of comfort — to read liberation as a metaphor and thereby preserve the existing social arrangements from scrutiny. The enslaved who built Egypt are not available for metaphor. They were people whose bodies belonged to Pharaoh, whose children were subject to state-sponsored murder, whose cry God heard. A church that turns their liberation into a spiritual lesson and never asks about the people in its own time whose cry is similarly loud has not followed the God of the Exodus. It has domesticated him.

The second temptation is to make the Exodus a simple map from oppressor to oppressed and assume that the church always knows which side it is on. This is the temptation of self-righteousness. The history of the church includes chapters in which it played Pharaoh's role — providing theological justification for chattel slavery, blessing the conquest of indigenous peoples, organizing institutional opposition to the liberation movements that drew on Exodus imagery to describe their struggle. The church that reads the Exodus must read it with the self-implicating honesty that the text demands. We are not always Moses. We have sometimes been Egypt.

---

The demand the Exodus places on the church is not a political program. It is a theological orientation — a set of questions that the story requires us to ask and keep asking. Who is crying out in our time? Who has organized a system that converts people into resources and suppresses the cry of those being consumed by it? What would it mean for the people who bear the name of this God to hear that cry, descend to where it is rising, and act in a way that disrupts the arrangements that produce it?

These questions are not comfortable. The Exodus was not a comfortable story for Pharaoh. It was not entirely comfortable for Moses, who spent forty years in the desert before God was ready to send him. It was not comfortable for Israel, who built the golden calf before they reached the promised land and complained repeatedly that Egypt was better than the wilderness.

But the direction was always away from Egypt. The cry was always heard. The God who said "I have come down to deliver them" has not stopped coming down.

The church that is still in Egypt has not yet followed him out.
