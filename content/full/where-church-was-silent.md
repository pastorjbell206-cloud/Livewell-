The church in America has a history that is not primarily the history of courage and faithfulness, though it contains that. It is, in significant stretches, a history of silence in the face of injustice — a silence that was often theological, often institutional, and almost always served the interests of the church's own comfort and cultural position more than the interests of the people the silence abandoned.

That is the sentence I would prefer not to write. It is also the sentence the church would prefer not to hear. That preference — the preference to tell the flattering half of our story and call the omission discretion — is itself a form of the problem.

So let us be specific, because specificity is where the reckoning gets real.

The silence during chattel slavery: when the dominant theological tradition not only failed to oppose the institution but provided the biblical justifications for it. Theologians wrote careful treatises. Pastors preached from the text to defend the buying and selling of human beings made in God's image. The denominational machinery split not because the church chose conscience over culture, but because it chose culture over conscience — and the fractures along those lines persist in American church life to this day. Albert Barnes, writing in 1846 [cite — confirm publication details], catalogued the rationalizations and found them wanting. But Barnes was the exception. The majority held the comfortable position.

The silence during Jim Crow: when the white church largely acquiesced to a system of racial terror and legal apartheid that its own theology should have made intolerable. The cross stood in front of churches whose members rode in the night, or who knew the riders and said nothing, or who considered their silence the better part of wisdom. Emmett Till was fourteen years old. The murderers were acquitted in sixty-seven minutes. The churches in that county held services the following Sunday and the text was not Micah 6:8 and the application was not repentance.

The silence during the civil rights movement: when the majority of white evangelical and mainline churches were not on the side of the movement but were, at best, uncomfortable bystanders and, at worst, active opponents of the very justice the prophets had announced. Martin Luther King Jr. wrote his Letter from Birmingham Jail not to the Klan but to the clergy — the white moderates who preferred order to truth, who found his methods too disruptive, who kept asking him to be patient. [cite — confirm exact letter recipients.] That letter is one of the most theologically precise documents produced in twentieth-century American Christianity, and its primary audience was the church that would not move.

This is not comfortable history. It is not the history the church tells about itself in its promotional materials. But it is the history — and the church that refuses to reckon with it honestly is a church that has chosen comfort over truthfulness, which is itself a continuation of the pattern.

---

Here is where I need to be careful, because this kind of reckoning gets weaponized in both directions.

Some use the history to indict the faith itself, as though the failures of the church disprove the gospel. They do not. They prove, rather, that the gospel was not applied — that the institution substituted cultural allegiance for theological conviction and called the exchange faithful. The standard by which we can name these things failures is the gospel itself. A Christianity capacious enough to produce Harriet Tubman and Dietrich Bonhoeffer [cite — confirm Bonhoeffer's explicit anti-racism work] and the abolitionist wing of the church is not the problem. The problem is what happens when that Christianity gets domesticated, when it makes peace with the systems it was always supposed to confront.

Others refuse the reckoning entirely. They hear any honest account of the past as an attack on the faith, and they retreat into the flattering half of the story. They point to the abolitionists — correctly — and then stop there, as though the abolitionists' courage excuses the majority's silence. It does not. Courage in some parts of the church does not baptize cowardice in the rest.

Both evasions miss the same thing: the capacity for repentance that is the church's greatest asset and most consistently abandoned gift. We cannot repent of what we will not name.

---

And it is ours to name. Not theirs. Ours.

This is the part the comfortable Christian wants to skip. It is easy to condemn the slaveholding preacher across the distance of a century and a half — it costs nothing, and it allows me to locate myself on the right side without examining whether I am actually on it. I would almost certainly not have been the brave exception. The people who defended the indefensible were not monsters. They were ordinary believers who let their culture, their comfort, and their economic interest read the Bible for them. They had prayer lives and family devotions and genuine piety in other respects. Their failure was not a failure of religion but a failure to let religion mean what it meant — a failure to let the God of Exodus be more determinative than the comfort of empire.

That is not a nineteenth-century disease. It is the permanent temptation of the church in every age, and I am not outside it. I know what it is to let the cost of a sermon suppress the text it should have come from. I know what it is to preach around the thing that would cost something. The mechanism has not changed.

---

The biblical pattern is unambiguous and worth sitting with longer than we usually do.

The prophets in Israel were not sent primarily to the pagan nations. They were sent to the people of God — to a worshiping community whose songs God could not tolerate because their hands were full of injustice. "When you come to appear before me," God says through Isaiah, "who has required of you this trampling of my courts?" (Isaiah 1:12). "Your hands are full of blood" (Isaiah 1:15). The indictment falls on the faithful, not the faithless.

Amos is even harder. He speaks directly to religious people who kept the feast days and offered burnt offerings — people who had a rigorous liturgical life — and God tells them: "I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies" (Amos 5:21). Why? Because justice was not rolling down like waters. Because the poor were being ground down while the worshipers sang. The religious performance and the social indifference were not separate problems. The second made a mockery of the first.

Jesus did not soften this. The harshest judgment in Matthew 25 is not pronounced on the overtly wicked but on those who saw the stranger, the hungry, the imprisoned — and passed by. Not malicious. Not cruel. Simply absent. "I did not know you" is the verdict, and it comes after a lifetime of apparently religious activity (Matthew 25:44-45).

Ezekiel catalogued the sins of Sodom and found, at the head of the list, not the sexual immorality that dominates our cultural memory of that story, but this: "Behold, this was the guilt of your sister Sodom: she and her daughters had pride, excess of food, and prosperous ease, but did not aid the poor and needy" (Ezekiel 16:49). Excess and ease, combined with the failure to aid those in need. That indictment has more purchase on contemporary American Christianity than most of our pulpits have been willing to admit.

---

Consider a steel-manned version of the counter-position.

The argument goes: the church should not become a political organization. Conflating the gospel with any particular social agenda distorts the church's primary mission, which is the proclamation of salvation through Christ. History shows that churches that lead with social reform eventually lose the gospel altogether, and the progressive mainline denominations stand as evidence. Better to stick to what the church knows — worship, Scripture, the sacraments — and let political engagement remain the work of individual Christians.

That argument has real weight. The social gospel movement of the early twentieth century did produce churches that traded away theological substance for progressive politics, and those churches are dying, not because justice killed them but because the gospel departed before justice arrived. The concern about politicization is not paranoia; it is the memory of a real catastrophe.

But the argument proves too much. It assumes that the church's choice is between the gospel and justice, when the Hebrew prophets and the New Testament make them inseparable. Jesus does not present the kingdom as a spiritual reality floating above the material conditions of human beings. He announces it in a world where the poor are crushed, where the widow is ignored, where the prisoner is forgotten — and he says the kingdom's arrival changes that. Not as a secondary application of a spiritual gospel. As constitutive of what the gospel is.

The churches that traded the gospel for progressive politics did not fail because they cared about justice. They failed because they stopped believing in the resurrection, the unique authority of Scripture, the reality of sin — and justice untethered from those convictions becomes simply the politics of the moment wearing clerical vestments. The answer to that failure is not to abandon justice. The answer is to recover the full theology that makes justice coherent — the theology in which human beings have dignity because they bear God's image, in which injustice is offense against a holy God, in which the arc of history bends toward a kingdom not built by human effort alone.

---

A concrete scene, because the history can remain abstract in ways that protect us.

A pastor in the 1950s South, by all accounts a good man — kind to the families in his congregation, faithful to his visiting schedule, a good preacher, a loving husband and father. He knows what is happening. He has seen the signs on the water fountains. He has heard the stories. He reads his Bible; he knows what Micah says. But there are powerful men in his congregation who built their businesses on the existing order, and there are families whose sons are on the county police force, and there are deacons who have made it clear that the church is not the place for this. And so he preaches. He preaches faithfully on many things. He is not a bad man. He never preaches a word of support for the system. He simply never preaches a word against it either.

And the people in his congregation, the ones without property and without protection, go to bed each Sunday night knowing that the man who opened the Bible this morning does not think they are worth the risk of a hard sermon.

I know that pastor's calculation because I have made versions of it. Not in the same stakes, not with the same consequences, but the calculation is the same: this will cost me something, and I am not sure the congregation is ready, and maybe I will get to it eventually, when the moment is right. The moment is never right when comfort is the standard. The prophets did not wait for the right moment. They spoke and took the consequences, and the word of God was not bound (2 Timothy 2:9).

---

That is why this reckoning is not primarily about the past.

A church that cannot tell the truth about where it was silent before will not recognize the next silence being prepared for it now. The mechanism never changes: the issue is contested, taking a side is costly, the comfortable position is to call silence prudence and wait. Every generation has its version. Every generation's faithful remnant looks, to the comfortable majority, like people making the church political — until enough time passes that the verdict is clear and everyone claims they were on the right side.

The reckoning is not self-flagellation. It is not despair. It is repentance — a Christian word, a word that implies the possibility of turning. But repentance begins with naming. A church that owns its history of silence is not weakened by that confession. It is freed by it — freed to be, in this moment, the body that speaks rather than the majority that waited.

We would rather not own this. That preference is precisely what the prophets were sent to break.

The history is ours. The silence was ours. The question now is whether the voice will be ours too — or whether we will perfect the art of being present and quiet at the moment when God was asking for something else.

The prophets called that unfaithfulness. The text is not kind about it. We should not be easy about it either.
