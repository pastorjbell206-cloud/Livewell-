The most effective way to keep the church silent on poverty is to convince it that poverty is a personal problem.

Once poverty is primarily a moral category — a consequence of poor choices, bad values, weak discipline — the appropriate response is charity and evangelism: help people make better choices, convert them to better values, and poverty will sort itself out. The church can maintain its comfort, its donors, and its political alliances without ever asking the harder question. The harder question is this: who benefits from the arrangement that produces so much poverty, and why has the arrangement persisted?

That question is political. I do not mean partisan. I mean that it concerns polis — the ordering of common life, the distribution of power, the rules by which society decides who gets what. Poverty is political because the conditions that produce and sustain it are produced and sustained by policy choices, not merely by the personal failures of poor individuals.

## The Prophets Already Said This

The prophets knew this without the vocabulary of modern economics. Amos watched the wealthy merchants of the eighth century B.C. north kingdom manipulate grain weights, use legal mechanisms to dispossess small farmers of their ancestral land, and trade debt bondage as a commodity — all within the bounds of a system designed to make such extraction possible. He did not say: those poor farmers should have worked harder. He announced the word of the Lord against the system itself. "You trample on the poor and take a levy of grain from them" (Amos 5:11). The trampling is structural. The levy is extracted through economic and legal power. The sin is embedded in the arrangement, not merely in the hearts of individual transactors.

Amos is specifically angry at the merchants who wait impatiently for the Sabbath and the new moon to end so they can resume selling — "making the ephah small and the shekel great and dealing deceitfully with false balances" (Amos 8:5). They are not violating the law in ways that are visible from the outside. They are manipulating the mechanisms of commerce — the weights, the measures, the timing — in ways that systematically disadvantage the poor, extracting from every transaction a tiny additional portion that, multiplied across thousands of transactions, produces enormous wealth at the top and grinding poverty at the bottom. The prophet sees this. He names it as sin. He does not call it commerce.

Proverbs 13:23 observes that "the fallow ground of the poor would yield much food, but it is swept away through injustice." The poor have productive capacity. What they lack is access — to land, to credit, to legal protection, to the basic infrastructure through which that capacity could be exercised. Injustice sweeps it away. That is not a personal failing. That is a system working as designed.

Isaiah 10:1–2 offers the most direct indictment of unjust legislation: "Woe to those who decree iniquitous decrees, and the writers who keep writing oppression, to turn aside the needy from justice and to rob the poor of my people of their right." Unjust decrees. Written oppression. The poverty of the vulnerable is the product of legislated arrangements — laws and legal structures that systematically turn aside the poor's legal claims and rob them of their rights. This is not a description of individual sin. It is a description of politics. The prophet is naming what legislators do.

## The Structural Argument

The steel-man of the personal-responsibility position deserves a hearing before the verdict. Personal behavior does affect economic outcomes. Marriage, work ethic, educational attainment, and impulse control are real factors in individual economic trajectories. Denying this entirely is dishonest. There are genuine differences in the habits and choices that produce different economic results, and those differences are not entirely explained by structural factors.

But these factors operate within systems that either amplify or constrain their effects. The same behavioral profile — the same work ethic, the same educational level, the same family structure — produces wildly different economic outcomes depending on where you were born, what neighborhood you grew up in, what schools were available to you, what credit was accessible, and what legal protections existed for your wage claims. The behavior is the same. The system is not. And the system is a political product.

The example that makes this most visible is the Federal Housing Administration's policies from 1934 to 1968 [cite — confirm]. The FHA's underwriting standards explicitly designated Black and minority neighborhoods as poor credit risks and refused to insure mortgages in those areas — what became known as redlining. Simultaneously, the FHA massively subsidized homeownership in white suburbs through guaranteed mortgages at low interest rates. The result was the construction of an enormous wealth gap: white families built home equity through a government-subsidized mechanism, while Black families were systematically denied access to the same mechanism. Home equity is the primary vehicle through which middle-class American families transmit wealth across generations. The racial wealth gap that persists today [cite — confirm] is not a natural fact. It is the compound interest on a political choice.

The Black families who were redlined did not fail because of poor personal choices. They were strategically excluded by a government policy, and then the results of that exclusion were attributed to personal failure by a society that preferred not to examine what it had built.

## Jesus on Poverty

Jesus preached about money more than about any other single subject except the kingdom of God — and in his theology, the two are inseparable. Luke 6:20–26 is the starkest passage: "Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God. Blessed are you who are hungry now, for you shall be satisfied." The woes are equally direct: "Woe to you who are rich, for you have received your consolation. Woe to you who are full now, for you shall be hungry."

These are not primarily spiritual categories. Luke, unlike Matthew, does not spiritualize. He is not talking about the "poor in spirit." He is talking about the poor — the people who actually lack money, who actually lack food, who actually weep while the comfortable prosper around them. And he is announcing a reversal that is not merely personal but social.

I have preached on the parable of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31) as a story about afterlife consequences, which it certainly is. I have been slower to notice that Lazarus was not merely unlucky. He was lying at the gate of a man with enormous resources and receiving nothing. The gate was the place where transactions, legal claims, and social distributions were settled. Lazarus was dying at the junction point between wealth and power, and the arrangement between them produced his death. Jesus does not explain why Lazarus was poor. He simply shows us a man who had resources and a man who had none, and announces where God stood.

## The Political Courage the Gospel Requires

The church that only addresses personal behavior without ever addressing the systems in which that behavior takes place has chosen the easier conversation. Charity is easier than justice. Charity requires my surplus. Justice might require my vote, my voice, my willingness to name what the comfortable people in the room do not want named.

The prophets were not primarily social workers with a better methodology. They were messengers of a God who cared — with specific, named specificity — about the arrangements of power that crushed the poor. The indictment in Amos is not generalized moral concern about poverty as a social condition. It is specific: these merchants, these weights, this grain, this legal mechanism for dispossessing farmers of their ancestral land. The specificity is theological. A God who cares in general but not in particular is not the God of Scripture. The God of Scripture cares about particular people in particular circumstances who are being harmed by particular arrangements, and his prophet names those arrangements.

To preach what they preached is to accept that sometimes the most faithful thing the church can say is not "here is how to help the poor" but "here is why there are so many of them, and what God thinks about it." That is political. That has always been political. The prophets were expelled from their communities for saying it. The question is not whether it is appropriate for the church to say political things. The question is whether we have the courage to say the political things the prophets said.

I have found that courage selectively. I have preached with more clarity about individual sin than about systemic sin, because individual sin does not threaten anyone's investment portfolio. Systemic sin — the kind Amos names, the kind Isaiah names, the kind that is embedded in legislation and economic arrangement and the structure of credit markets — that naming has costs. People leave. Giving declines. The pastor is accused of being political.

He is. The prophets were political too. It cost them.

The only question is whether we have the courage to say it. That question is sitting there every Sunday morning, in the distance between the gospel we preach and the system we have not named, waiting to be answered.
