The Mosaic law was not neutral on the question of lending money to the poor.

Exodus 22:25: "If you lend money to any of my people with you who is poor, you shall not be like a moneylender to him, and you shall not exact interest from him." Leviticus 25:35–37 extends the prohibition and ties it explicitly to the memory of Israel's own bondage: you were in Egypt, you know what it is to be crushed by systems that extract from the vulnerable. Deuteronomy 23:19 is categorical: "You shall not charge interest on loans to your brother, interest on money, interest on food, interest on anything that is lent for interest." These are not suggestions offered as pastoral guidance to those who feel led in that direction. They are commands embedded in the economic structure of the covenant community, because God understood something American Christianity has largely forgotten: the capacity to extract interest from the desperate is the capacity to trap them permanently in dependency. It is the mechanism of bondage wearing the clothing of a transaction.

Payday lending — short-term loans at annual percentage rates that commonly exceed 300% and sometimes approach 600% [cite — confirm] — is not a new concept. It is usury with modern paperwork. The person who borrows $200 to cover a gap between paychecks and repays $260 two weeks later, only to need another loan immediately because the $260 came out of the rent money, is not experiencing a free-market service. They are in a trap designed by people who understood exactly what they were building.

I have not said this from a pulpit. I have preached about personal financial responsibility — the dangers of debt, the virtue of savings, the discipline of the tithe. I have not named the industry that profits specifically from people who cannot practice those virtues because they are already underwater. That silence was not neutrality. It was a choice about whose interests it served.

## The Biblical Architecture Against Usury

The Hebrew word for interest that these passages prohibit is *neshek* — literally, a bite. The image is deliberate. The word pictures interest on loans to the vulnerable as something that consumes the person from whom it is taken, the way a predator's bite begins the process of death. The legislation is built around that image because the legislators understood what high-interest lending does over time: it does not merely inconvenience the borrower. It devours them.

Ezekiel places lending at interest alongside murder and adultery in his catalog of sins that defile the covenant community (Ezekiel 18:13). Not peripheral offenses. Defiling ones. The person who lends at interest to the poor is placed in the same category as the person who commits violence — because the moral logic of the community regards both as acts that destroy the human beings God has made.

The Psalms make the connection to character. Psalm 15 asks who may dwell on God's holy mountain, and the answer includes: one who "lends money without charging interest" (Psalm 15:5). The absence of extractive lending is a mark of the righteous person. Not an external legal requirement alone, but a disposition of the heart toward the neighbor — a refusal to treat the neighbor's desperation as an opportunity for profit.

Nehemiah, when he returned to Jerusalem and found that wealthy Jewish landowners were charging interest to their poor countrymen during a famine — some of them forcing them to mortgage their fields, their vineyards, their children into debt slavery — was "very angry" (Nehemiah 5:6). He did not immediately forgive. He assembled a great meeting, named the sin publicly, and demanded that the wealthy return the fields, the vineyards, the houses, and the hundredth part of the money they had been taking (Nehemiah 5:11). Correction in this account is structural. It requires the return of what was taken, not merely the stopping of the practice.

## What the Industry Is

Payday lending is a $30 billion annual industry in the United States [cite — confirm]. It operates through approximately 23,000 storefronts — more than the number of McDonald's locations in the country [cite — confirm]. The industry's location strategy is explicit: payday lenders cluster in low-income communities, near military bases, in predominantly Black and Hispanic neighborhoods [cite — confirm]. This is not because poor people have worse access to banks and therefore genuinely need emergency credit — though that is true. It is because the business model requires clients who are financially desperate enough to pay 400% interest, and those clients are concentrated in specific ZIP codes.

The design of the loan is the design of a trap. A two-week loan at a fee of $15 per $100 borrowed sounds modest until you calculate the annual percentage rate: 390% [cite — confirm]. The term is short enough that most borrowers cannot repay in full without immediately needing another loan — which is the intended outcome. The industry's own data [cite — confirm] shows that a majority of payday loan revenue comes from borrowers who take out ten or more loans per year. The business is not structured to help people out of a temporary bind. It is structured to ensure that the bind becomes permanent.

The defenders of the industry make a recognizable argument: in the absence of payday lending, where do the poor go for emergency credit? Banks will not lend to them. Traditional credit cards are unavailable or at similarly punishing rates. The payday lender is the lender of last resort, and eliminating it leaves the desperate with nothing. This deserves honest engagement. There are people who would face genuine catastrophe without some form of emergency credit access. The critique of the industry cannot be naive about that reality.

But the existence of a gap does not justify filling it with extraction. The question is not only "is this better than nothing?" It is "what does this do to people over time, and what does our endorsement of it say about us?" A system that profits specifically from the financial desperation of its clients — that is structurally designed to extend and renew loans rather than resolve them — is not providing a service. It is selling a trap. And the church that is silent about it while preaching about personal financial responsibility has separated individual virtue from the structural vice that makes such virtue nearly impossible for millions of people.

## The Church's Silence and What It Costs

The pastor who preaches about stewardship while remaining silent about predatory lending is not being neutral. He is protecting something. He may be protecting relationships with donors whose investment portfolios include financial services companies. He may be protecting the political affiliations of congregants who have internalized a deregulatory economic ideology. He may simply be protecting his own comfort, because naming what is happening in the payday loan office three blocks from the church building will make people angry in ways that sermons about personal savings do not.

The woman in that payday loan office at noon on a Tuesday is not a stranger to the biblical text. She is its primary concern. She is the widow of Deuteronomy 24, the poor of Leviticus 25, the neighbor of Proverbs 19:17 whose condition is a loan to the Lord. The church's silence in the face of the system that is extracting from her is not a neutral posture. It is the silence that Amos named when he said the people "sell the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals" (Amos 2:6).

I am not arguing that every congregation needs to become a financial advocacy organization. I am arguing that the prophetic tradition requires naming what is happening. It requires saying from the pulpit: there is an industry in our city designed to extract from the most vulnerable members of our community, and the law of Moses named this sin twenty-five centuries before the industry had its current name, and the church that follows Moses's God cannot pretend not to know what is happening three blocks away.

## What the Tradition Recommends

The tradition has concrete resources here as well. The concept of lending without interest to the poor was not merely a prohibition of the harmful. It was a positive vision of what economic relationships within the covenant community should look like. Paul's vision of abundance meeting need in 2 Corinthians 8–9 is a vision of redistribution based on covenant solidarity, not a market transaction. The early church community described in Acts 2 and Acts 4 distributed to anyone who had need — not as charity in the sense of surplus given from abundance, but as a structural commitment to ensuring that no one in the community lacked what they needed.

Several credit union movements and community development financial institutions (CDFIs) have been built on precisely this alternative logic — offering emergency credit at non-extractive rates to low-income borrowers, specifically as an alternative to payday lending [cite — confirm]. Some of these have been church-based. Most have not. The church, with its institutional credibility, its community presence, and its explicit theological mandate to care for the vulnerable, is arguably better positioned than any other institution to build and sustain this kind of alternative. Most have not done it. Most have not thought about it. The silence about what the payday lender is doing has been accompanied by silence about what the church could be doing instead.

## The Logic of Egypt

The law of Moses was embedded in a story: you were slaves in Egypt, and a God who hears the cry of the poor brought you out. That story is the foundation of every economic command in the Torah. You know what it is to be extracted from. You know what it is to have no power over the terms of your existence. Therefore: do not do that to each other. The prohibition is grounded in memory, and the memory is supposed to generate solidarity.

The church that has forgotten that story has forgotten something foundational about who it is and what it owes. It has allowed itself to become the kind of institution whose silence ratifies the contemporary equivalent of the Egyptian system — the arrangement that extracts from those who cannot resist being extracted from, that builds its prosperity on the perpetual desperation of those at the bottom.

The people in the payday loan office are not strangers to the biblical text. They are its primary concern.

The law of Moses knew what 400% interest was. It called it a bite. It commanded the people of God not to do it. The church that has not yet said anything about it has not yet arrived at the conversation the tradition requires.
