The church that has never sent anyone has usually told itself a reasonable story about why. The mission field is far away. The budget is tight. We are still growing into our own community. And every one of those explanations is true enough to delay action for another decade.

I have told versions of that story. I have preached about the Great Commission in Matthew 28 and then presided over a calendar with no sending, no commissioning, no moment where a congregation gathered around one of its own and said: *go, and we are behind you.* The sermon was good. The obedience was absent.

Here is what most church growth literature will not tell you about mission culture: it cannot be installed. You cannot hire a missions pastor, create a missions committee, and adopt a missions line item in the budget and declare that you have a sending culture. What you have built is a missions program. Programs are not cultures. Cultures are the sediment of repeated decisions, accumulated over years, pressed down into the imagination of a congregation until people begin to assume that this is who we are and what we do.

That sediment does not form overnight. It forms through friction.

The story of the church begins, in one real sense, with a sending. The disciples receive the Spirit in Acts 2 in Jerusalem, but the commission was already given on a hillside in Galilee: "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19). The directional logic of the command is outward. The community gathers not to preserve what it has received but to extend it. The word for church — *ekklesia* — was not a religious term in the Roman world. It described a called-out assembly, a group convened for public purpose, not primarily for its own benefit. The sending is not a program addition. It is definitional.

But most congregations in North America have absorbed a different definition. The church is where you go — for worship, for teaching, for pastoral care, for the community of believers in a fragmented world. These are not wrong. They are partial. The congregation that exists primarily as a gathering place, without a corresponding impulse toward the world, has mistaken half the picture for the whole.

The New Testament church at Antioch did not simply hold a vote and agree to send Paul and Barnabas in Acts 13. The text tells us the church was worshiping and fasting when the Holy Spirit spoke. That is not the posture of a congregation that treats mission as a departmental concern. That is a church whose ordinary life — its prayers, its fasting, its gathered attention — had already been shaped toward participation in what God was doing in the world. The sending was the overflow of a culture that had already formed in the soil of sustained devotion.

If your congregation has never sent anyone, the work is not primarily administrative. It is formational. It begins in the pulpit, because congregations absorb what the preacher returns to repeatedly, and they become suspicious of what the preacher treats as optional. A congregation whose pastor mentions the nations only when visiting missionaries stop through will conclude, reasonably, that mission is a specialty concern for specialists. The preacher who weaves the Abrahamic covenant of Genesis 12 — *through you all the families of the earth shall be blessed* — into his teaching, and returns to that thread in Galatians 3 where Paul calls the Gentile inclusion the fulfillment of Abraham's promise, and again in Revelation 7 where the multitude "from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages" stands before the throne — that preacher is teaching his people that they are living inside a story that moves toward the whole world. That is the beginning of a sending imagination.

The imagination has to precede the program by years. There are no shortcuts.

But the pulpit is not enough. Sending cultures require the congregation to know specific people being sent. Abstraction does not generate conviction. What generates conviction is sitting across from a twenty-six-year-old from your church who has spent eighteen months in your small group, whose children you have watched in the nursery, who has now said plainly that she believes she is called to East Africa — and then deciding, as a congregation, whether you will actually resource her. That decision will cost something. It will cost money that could have updated the fellowship hall. It will cost pastoral attention. It will cost the congregation's willingness to grieve the absence of someone they love.

That cost is not a problem to solve. It is the price of becoming a sending church, and it is the very thing that will teach your congregation what mission actually is. Cheap commitment to distant people produces shallow mission sentiment. Costly commitment to real people produces a culture.

The sending churches I have observed that built this culture from nothing — congregations with no sending tradition, no denominational support structure for global mission, no history of commissioning anyone — did it through one common move: they made the mission personal before they made it programmatic. They did not launch a missions initiative. They identified one person who was already leaning toward the field and they built a relationship around that specific person — not as an institutional project but as an expression of genuine pastoral care. They prayed for that person by name from the pulpit. They celebrated the stages of their preparation. They asked hard questions about calling and sustainability. They sent them with a real commitment — financial support that required the congregation to choose it over other things, regular correspondence, genuine pastoral care of someone who would no longer be in the room.

When that person sent back reports, the congregation received them with the same attention they would give announcements about people they actually knew — because they did know her. When she described a village in Malawi or a neighborhood in Phnom Penh, she was describing somewhere a person their congregation loved was spending her life. The abstraction dissolved. The mission became specific.

And then, when the next person in the congregation began to feel the pull of something beyond the local — a short-term trip that did not go home, a conversation that kept returning, a persistent sense of call — they did not have to imagine what it would mean to be sent. They had watched it happen. They had participated in it. They knew, from what they had lived through together, what the congregation was capable of.

The theology underneath this is not complicated but it requires genuine conviction to hold. The Lausanne Covenant of 1974 [cite — confirm], shaped significantly by John Stott, articulated the essential unity of evangelism and social action in the church's mission — not as competing priorities but as two dimensions of the same participation in the kingdom of God. The congregation that understands its mission as both announcing the gospel and embodying the justice of the kingdom will send people into both dimensions: to proclamation among the unreached and to solidarity with the suffering. Both forms of sending require the same culture of costly investment in specific people pursuing a specific call.

What this does not look like is the short-term mission trip as the primary formation mechanism. Short-term trips are not without value. They expose people to the global church, build relationships across cultures, and often generate the kind of theological friction that shifts a person's understanding of the gospel's scope. But they do not build sending culture by themselves. What they can do is create the pool of people who have been disturbed enough by what they saw to start asking whether their next step is longer. The sending culture receives those questions, holds them seriously, and creates the infrastructure — pastoral care, financial support, sustained relationship — to turn them into actual sending.

This is slow work. The congregation that begins this work now will not see a sending culture in two years. It will see the beginning of a culture in five, and something that the next generation inherits and assumes in ten. The pastor who is not willing to plant trees he will not sit under will not build a sending church.

But the generation that inherits it is worth working for.

Begin with one person. Commission them publicly. Support them genuinely. Grieve their departure honestly. Celebrate their work with the same attention you give things that actually matter to you. When they return — or when the next person begins to lean toward the field — your congregation will already know, from what they have lived through together, what it means to send.

The church that has never sent anyone will not become a sending church through a missions conference alone. It becomes one the same way every culture is formed: through the repeated practice of costly faithfulness, year after year, until the next generation cannot imagine being any other kind of church.

That generation is worth waiting for. Begin the waiting now.
