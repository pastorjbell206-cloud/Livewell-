H. Richard Niebuhr spent a book cataloguing the ways the church has answered the culture question — Christ against culture, Christ of culture, Christ above culture, Christ and culture in paradox, Christ transforming culture. [cite — confirm] The catalogue itself is instructive not because it provides the answer but because it reveals that the question has been live in every generation, and that every generation has reached for a different settlement.

We are living through a moment when the settlement our parents inherited is dissolving, and we do not yet know what will replace it.

The Constantinian arrangement — the broad alignment between Christian faith and Western cultural assumption that began in the fourth century and shaped Western civilization for more than a millennium — is not quite over, but its legitimacy is. The culture no longer defers to Christian claims. The institutions that once carried Christian assumption into public life without argument — schools, courts, civic ritual, the default moral vocabulary of the neighborhood — have either abandoned those assumptions or are actively contested about them. We are not post-Christian in the sense that Christianity is gone. We are post-Christian in the sense that Christianity is no longer the default.

---

For many Christians, this is primarily experienced as loss. The cultural conditions that made the faith easy have been removed, and the faith now requires more active choice, more deliberate formation, more willingness to be countercultural in ways that are actually costly rather than merely nominal.

This is disorienting. And the instinct it produces is understandable: fight to restore the conditions. Recover the cultural establishment. Legislate the norms. Force the culture back into a shape where the faith is at home.

I have felt this instinct. I understand it. And I think it misreads both the moment and the tradition.

The church was not at its most prophetic when it was culturally established. The church at its most prophetic was the church under pressure — the early communities that held a different account of reality while the imperial account surrounded them, who were outlawed and intermittently persecuted but who maintained the practices and the witness with a clarity that shaped the ancient world more permanently than any of the powers that tried to destroy them. It was the Confessing Church in Germany in the 1930s and 1940s, which maintained the integrity of the gospel while the German church capitulated to nationalist ideology. It was the Black church in America during the century of Jim Crow, which kept alive a theology of human dignity and divine justice in the very society organized to deny both. It was the underground church in contexts where Christianity carries genuine legal risk.

What those communities share is not cultural comfort but theological clarity. They knew what they believed, because they had to. The pressure removed the option of comfortable ambiguity.

---

The pressure that post-Christendom creates is not without grace. It is clarifying. It forces a congregation to ask what it actually believes, as opposed to what it has inherited and never examined. It forces a distinction between the gospel and the cultural form the gospel wore in a particular time and place.

This distinction matters enormously and has been lost in the political alignment of American Christianity in recent decades. The gospel is not Western. It is not American. It is not a product of any civilization, though every civilization has both received it and distorted it. The church that conflates the gospel with a particular political arrangement or cultural moment will discover — is already discovering — that the arrangement's decline feels like the gospel's decline, and that recovery of the arrangement becomes confused with faithfulness to the gospel.

These are not the same thing. They have never been the same thing. And the confusion between them has cost the church something very difficult to recover: the capacity to speak to the culture from outside the culture's own assumptions.

Lesslie Newbigin returned to England after decades as a bishop in India and wrote about what he saw: a culture that had been shaped by the gospel but had forgotten the gospel, and a church that had become so accommodated to that culture that it no longer knew how to speak with a distinct voice. [cite — confirm] He argued that the church's challenge in the West was missionary in the fullest sense — not bringing the gospel to people who had never heard it, but bringing it afresh to people who thought they already knew it and had decided it was not relevant. That missionary challenge requires that the church know the difference between what it believes and what the culture around it assumes.

---

The contemporary temptation runs in two directions, not one.

The first temptation is toward culture war — the recovery of cultural establishment through political force. This strategy misunderstands both what was lost and what the church's purpose is. What was lost was not the kingdom. What was lost was a particular cultural arrangement that, at its best, provided favorable conditions for Christian practice and, at its worst, allowed the church to mistake cultural dominance for gospel faithfulness. The culture war response doubles down on the mistake. It treats the restoration of cultural dominance as the goal of Christian witness, which converts the church into a political faction and the gospel into a platform.

The second temptation is toward relevance — the church that reshapes its message and practice to remain acceptable to a post-Christian culture. This strategy also misunderstands the moment. A church that has nothing to say except what the culture is already prepared to hear is not a church. It is an echo. The post-Christian person who encounters an echo of their own values in religious language is not encountering the gospel. They are encountering themselves.

Both strategies fail because they are responses to the same anxiety: the fear of being culturally irrelevant. The church that is desperate to remain culturally relevant will do almost anything to stay in the room. The church that is confident in its calling can afford to be strange — to practice the hospitality, the forgiveness, the economic solidarity that the surrounding culture finds implausible — and trust that the strangeness is its own kind of argument.

---

Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote in 1943 from his prison cell about what he called "religionless Christianity" — the possibility of a faith that did not depend on the cultural scaffolding of Western religion for its vitality. [cite — confirm] He was writing in a context where that scaffolding had collapsed catastrophically, where the church that had depended on it had largely capitulated to the power that destroyed it. He was asking what the gospel looked like when stripped of every cultural advantage — when being a Christian cost something real, when the institutional supports were gone, when the faith had to live on its own resources.

That question is not yet as urgent in contemporary America as it was in Bonhoeffer's Germany. But the direction of travel is clarifying. The cultural conditions that once made Christian faith easy are changing. The question of what remains when the easy conditions are gone is becoming less hypothetical.

---

What the church is called to in this moment is not primarily a cultural strategy. It is faithfulness to a set of practices that form people into the image of Christ — practices that were always countercultural to some degree and are now more visibly so.

Gathering. Not for a performance to attract the spiritually curious but for the formation of people who know how to be in covenant with one another across disagreement and difference. Prayer. Not the public display of spiritual confidence but the honest bringing of the actual condition of one's soul before the God who does not need the performance. Scripture. Not as a cultural artifact or a sourcebook for values but as the living word of the God who is working toward a kingdom that no cultural arrangement can capture or contain. Table. The Eucharist's insistence that the body broken and given is the center of reality, not any political or cultural project. Hospitality. The practice that enacts the theological claim that every person who arrives is made in the image of God and therefore belongs at the table. Reconciliation. The refusal to accept the culture's account of who is irredeemably other.

These practices are not dependent on cultural establishment for their efficacy. They worked in the Roman Empire before Constantine. They work in the underground church today. They will work in post-Christendom America if we are willing to pursue them with the seriousness the moment requires rather than mourning the cultural conditions that once made them easier.

---

Not every generation gets to be culturally at home. The tradition is older than any single culture. It has survived arrangements far more hostile than the one we currently inhabit.

The early church did not have Christian assumptions in the broader culture. It did not have favorable legal status. It did not have cultural prestige or political access. It had the practices and the witness and the Spirit, and it changed the world.

The question is not whether the culture will accommodate us. The culture has never been the ultimate audience.

The question is whether we are willing to be the church anyway — whether we will practice the faith with the seriousness that the moment requires, whether we will hold the costly things at cost, whether we will be strange enough to be worth explaining and honest enough to be believed when we do.

That is not a cultural strategy. It is a decision about what we love and whether we love it enough to carry it through conditions that do not favor it.

The tradition has been carried through worse. The question is whether we are willing to carry it now.
