When pastors think about the dangerous people on a church staff, they usually picture the obvious candidates: the person whose theology has drifted, the one whose moral failure is waiting to emerge, the employee whose ambition is outrunning their integrity. These are real dangers, and they deserve genuine leadership attention.

But the most consistently damaging person on many church staffs is none of these. It is the high performer with a toxic relational pattern. The person who delivers extraordinary results, whose gifts are undeniable, whose contribution to the ministry is visible and significant — and who creates relational wreckage wherever they work, treating subordinates with contempt, refusing to operate as part of a team, or generating a slow drain of morale that the leadership keeps overlooking because the performance numbers are too good to disturb.

## Why the Church Hasn't Named This

The church has a longer history with the gifted-but-destructive person than its leadership culture typically acknowledges. The biblical record itself contains examples: Saul, whose military gifts and royal bearing concealed a relational destructiveness toward David that eventually undid his reign (1 Samuel 18:6-16). Diotrephes, who "loves to be first" in 3 John 9 and uses his position to exclude rather than include, apparently with considerable institutional effectiveness. The false apostles in 2 Corinthians, who clearly had rhetorical gifts impressive enough to attract a following, combined with a relational style that Paul describes as enslaving and exploitative (2 Corinthians 11:20).

These figures appear in the biblical record not as theological failures primarily — though they are that — but as organizational leaders whose gifts functioned as cover for their relational destruction. The church has been encountering this pattern since the beginning. It has rarely developed adequate language for confronting it.

Part of the reason is the conflation of spiritual gifting with spiritual character that runs deep in evangelical leadership culture. The assumption, rarely made explicit but consistently operative, is that significant spiritual gifts are evidence of spiritual formation — that the person who can preach with genuine power, or lead with genuine charisma, or attract followers with genuine effectiveness, must therefore have the character that the church holds up as its standard. This assumption is demonstrably wrong. The biblical record demonstrates its wrongness. The experience of ministry demonstrates it weekly somewhere in the country. And yet it persists, because the alternative — acknowledging that gifts and character can be dramatically misaligned — requires the church to hold its gifted people to a standard that challenges them in ways the institution finds uncomfortable.

## Why This Person Is Especially Dangerous

The high-performing toxic team member is dangerous precisely because of the performance. The gaps they create are relational and cultural — things that are harder to measure and slower to produce visible consequences than ministry metrics. Meanwhile, the things they are good at — programming, attendance, production quality, whatever their domain — are generating the visible outcomes that the organization rewards.

The calculation that pastors and leadership teams make, often unconsciously, is: the cost of this person's relational toxicity is worth the benefit of their performance. This calculation is almost always wrong, and its wrongness becomes undeniable over time. The people this person manages leave. The team's morale gradually erodes. The culture of the staff shifts toward fear or cynicism. And eventually, the productivity itself declines because the team has been depleted by the conditions the high performer created.

Patrick Lencioni's organizational research has consistently documented the relationship between team health and organizational performance — specifically, the finding that dysfunction at the leadership level predictably produces dysfunction at every level below it, and that the dysfunction spreads faster than the performance benefits of the individual causing it [cite — confirm]. The church is not exempt from this dynamic. Its theological commitments actually intensify it — because the staff team that is enduring a toxic high performer is not just demoralized professionally. They are watching their organization fail to practice what it preaches, and the spiritual dimension of that failure compounds the organizational damage.

## Why Ministry Settings Produce This Pattern

Churches are particularly vulnerable to this dynamic for several reasons.

First, the high performer's gifts are often spiritual gifts — preaching, leadership, evangelism — and the conflation of spiritual gifting with spiritual character makes it harder to name the relational problems clearly. To say "this person's preaching is extraordinary but their relational behavior is damaging the staff" is to make a distinction that the church's cultural framework does not readily support.

Second, the pastoral instinct toward mercy and patience with human weakness — which is, in its proper application, a genuine virtue — can make leaders slow to confront patterns that would be addressed more directly in other organizational contexts. "I'm giving them grace" is sometimes the right response to a struggling staff member. It is sometimes a pastoral rationalization for conflict avoidance.

Third, the specific output that the high performer produces is often highly visible to the congregation — who benefit from it and whose positive feedback creates pressure to maintain the status quo. The congregation that loves the youth pastor who fills rooms every Wednesday night is not seeing the youth interns he has reduced to tears by the end of every planning meeting. Their feedback — genuine, enthusiastic, real — functions as institutional protection for the pattern that the staff is enduring.

I have been slow to address a version of this in my own ministry. The rationalization I used was pastoral patience. The actual dynamic was that I did not want to have the conversation, and I used a virtue to dress up a failure of nerve. The staff members who were bearing the cost of my patience were watching to see whether I would do what I believed about people mattering. I was not passing that test.

## The Steel-Manned Defense

The strongest objection to addressing the high-performing toxic employee is that organizational health frameworks may be importing a corporate standard into a context that should be governed by different principles — that the church's call to bear with one another, to pursue restoration, to extend mercy beyond what any secular organization would extend, means that the toxic high performer deserves more patience than the analysis suggests.

This objection has genuine theological content. The restoration imperative in Galatians 6:1 is real. The call to bear one another's burdens is not a metaphor. The church is not an HR department, and a framework that produces swift terminations for performance issues rather than genuine pastoral investment in the person's growth has misunderstood what the church is.

But the objection collapses under examination when it is applied specifically to a pattern rather than an episode. The pastoral obligation to pursue restoration presupposes honest naming of the problem, direct engagement with the person about the specific behavior, and a genuine process of accountability. The church that is tolerating a toxic high performer in the name of grace has usually done none of these things. It has neither extended genuine pastoral care nor held genuine organizational accountability. It has simply avoided the conversation.

Addressing this situation requires the pastor to be clear about a non-negotiable: results without integrity of character and relational health are not acceptable in a church context, regardless of the size of the results. This is not just a management principle. It is a theological one. The church is supposed to be a community that models the character of Christ, and a staff culture in which relational toxicity is tolerated for the sake of performance is not that community.

## What to Do

Confronting the high performer with specific, honest, documented feedback about their relational patterns — and making clear that sustained change is required for continued employment — is among the hardest things a pastor does. It is also among the most necessary.

The conversation requires specificity about behavior, not character. Not "you have a toxic personality" but "in the last three months, I have observed these specific interactions with these specific team members, and the pattern is this." The documentation matters — not as legal protection, though that is worth having, but because it prevents the conversation from becoming a contest of perceptions and forces it to engage with specific events.

The conversation also requires honesty about consequences. The high performer who has been consistently affirmed for their results and only mildly cautioned about their relational patterns has received a message about what the organization actually values. That message must be clearly corrected: the relational pattern is not a secondary matter. It is a primary matter. It is non-negotiable. The timeline for change is specific.

And the conversation requires follow-through. The pastor who names the problem, receives a promising response, and then quietly lets the accountability dissolve has confirmed the message he was trying to correct. The follow-up conversation, six weeks later, asking specifically about the specific changes that were committed to, is as important as the initial confrontation.

The team members who are quietly enduring the toxic dynamic are watching to see whether the pastor will do what he says he believes about people mattering. This conversation is not just about one staff member. It is about the culture of the whole organization.

Let your leadership of this situation be consistent with what you preach. The most dangerous person on your staff may already be the person you have been calling your best asset. Name it. Address it. The team you have is watching.
