Thom Rainer has long argued that the metrics most churches track — primarily attendance and giving — are the least useful indicators of genuine church health, and the most useful indicators are almost entirely unmeasured [cite — confirm]. This is not a peripheral observation. It has profound implications for how pastors and church leaders understand success, make decisions, and evaluate the fruit of their ministry.

The problem with tracking only attendance and giving is not that these things do not matter — they do. It is that they are lagging indicators. By the time they show a problem, the problem has usually been present for a long time. And they tell you almost nothing about the quality of what is happening in the congregation — whether people are actually becoming more like Jesus, whether genuine community is forming, whether the church is having meaningful impact on its surrounding community, or whether the congregation is spiritually healthy or merely numerically stable.

## The Deeper Problem with Church Metrics

There is a prior theological problem beneath the measurement question. The church that has adopted the business world's primary metric — growth — as its measure of success has, without necessarily intending to, accepted the business world's definition of success. And growth is not the New Testament's primary metric for a healthy congregation.

Paul's letter to the Philippians describes a church that is not numerically large, that is facing external persecution, and whose internal conflicts were significant enough to require public address (Philippians 4:2-3). His evaluation of their health is not attendance-based. It is based on their partnership in the gospel, their joy despite suffering, their generosity toward him in prison, and the specific evidence of lives being transformed — Epaphroditus nearly dying in service (Philippians 2:30), the Praetorian Guard hearing the gospel (Philippians 1:13), slaves and free people and a Roman jailer's household all receiving the same Lord. That is a health portrait that no attendance chart would capture.

Paul's prayer for the Ephesians is that they would know "what is the immeasurable greatness of his power toward us who believe" (Ephesians 1:19). That power is not measured by average weekend attendance. The question is whether we are developing metrics that at least gesture in the direction of what Scripture says a healthy church looks like — or whether we are measuring what is easy to count and calling it health because counting is what we know how to do.

I have been guilty of this. The weeks when the numbers were up, I felt better than I should have. The weeks when the numbers were down, I carried a weight I had no theological reason to carry. The metrics were forming me in directions I had not examined.

## Metric 1: Baptism and Conversion Rate

The ratio of baptisms or public professions of faith to overall attendance is one of the most revealing health metrics available to a local church. A congregation that is growing numerically through transfer growth — Christians moving from other churches — may be providing a valuable service to the mobile Christian population, but it is not fulfilling the Great Commission in the same way as a congregation whose growth is primarily driven by conversion.

Tracking this ratio over time reveals whether the church is genuinely reaching people outside the faith or primarily serving the already-convinced. Both have value, but only one represents the primary mission Jesus assigned.

Churches in contexts with high population mobility will need to interpret this metric differently than stable rural congregations. But the question it forces — are people genuinely coming to faith here, and at what rate relative to our overall presence — is a question that every congregation that takes the Great Commission seriously needs to be asking regularly and honestly.

## Metric 2: First-Time to Regular Attender Conversion Rate

How many people who visit your church once come back? And how many of those who come back regularly become connected to community within the congregation? This metric reveals the hospitality culture and connection infrastructure of the church — whether new people are genuinely welcomed and integrated or whether the existing community is functionally closed to newcomers even when it is theologically committed to openness.

The gap between stated welcome and experienced welcome is one of the most common and most damaging disconnects in local church life. A congregation that believes itself to be welcoming because it has a welcome team and a guest follow-up process, but whose culture is formed primarily around the relationships of existing members, will lose most first-time attenders before they ever form a meaningful connection. The metric does not lie. If the first-time to regular attender conversion rate is low, the problem is culture, not process — and no amount of process improvement will fix a culture problem.

## Metric 3: Ministry Participation Rate

What percentage of your congregation is actively involved in serving — in the church or in the community through the church? A congregation where twenty percent of the people do eighty percent of the ministry has a serious discipleship problem, regardless of how good the twenty percent are. The engaged minority cannot compensate for the passive majority when it comes to genuine community formation and mission engagement.

This metric cuts against the attractional church model's tendency to optimize for the experience of passive attenders. The attractional model has produced churches that are excellent at drawing people in and significantly less excellent at deploying them outward. The congregation that has fifteen hundred people on a Sunday morning and one hundred and fifty of them involved in genuine service is not fifteen times healthier than the congregation of one hundred where ninety people serve — it is less healthy, measured by this metric, by a significant margin.

Ephesians 4:11-12 describes the leadership of the church not as the people who do the ministry but as the people who "equip the saints for the work of ministry." The goal of the pastor, elder, and teacher is not to do ministry for the congregation. It is to develop the congregation into a community of ministers. The participation rate measures how successfully that equipping is happening.

## Metric 4: Discipleship Depth Indicators

These are harder to measure but not impossible to assess: Are people in your congregation having spiritual conversations outside of Sunday? Are they engaged with Scripture in their daily life, not just in church programs? Are they growing in the fruits of the Spirit — love, patience, generosity, humility — in ways that the people closest to them can observe? Small group leaders, regular pastoral conversations, and periodic congregational surveys can surface this information, though it requires more work than counting chairs.

Dallas Willard's argument — that the church has largely failed at its central task of producing genuine Christlikeness in its people — is one that every honest pastor feels in the specific, particular faces of the congregation they lead [cite — confirm]. We know the people who have been in our churches for twenty years and are not more patient, more generous, or more forgiving than they were when they arrived. We know the marriages that are no more Christ-shaped after a decade of church attendance than they were before. The discipleship depth indicators, honestly assessed, force the question of whether the programming we are running is producing what we say we are trying to produce.

## Metric 5: Community Impact

What does the neighborhood around your church building know about your congregation? What does it experience from you? The church that is genuinely healthy for its community tends to be known by that community — by the local school, the social services agencies, the businesses and families in the surrounding area — as a presence that makes the neighborhood better. If your neighbors would not notice if you closed, something is worth examining.

Jeremiah's command to the exiles in Babylon — "seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the Lord on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare" (Jeremiah 29:7) — is a metric disguised as a command. Is the city better because the church is there? Are specific people served, specific needs met, specific gaps filled? The answer to that question is measurable, even if messily, and it tells more about a congregation's health than the parking lot count on a Sunday morning.

## The Steel-Man Against Metrics

There is a serious argument against all of this. It goes: the Holy Spirit moves where it will, the fruit of genuine discipleship often takes decades to become visible, and the church that spends its pastoral energy on measurement and evaluation is diverting attention from the actual work of preaching and prayer and presence. Augustine's Confessions describes a spiritual formation that no metrics would have predicted during most of his early life. Paul was converted in a moment that no recruitment pipeline would have generated.

This is true. The church is not a business, and the pastor who relates to the congregation primarily as a metrics problem to be optimized has already made a category error. The metrics are not the mission. They are diagnostic tools — instruments for asking whether the mission is being pursued honestly or whether the institution has substituted its own survival for the actual purposes of God.

None of these metrics are perfect. All of them require judgment in interpretation. But used together, honestly, over time, they paint a picture of church health that attendance and giving simply cannot provide.

## What the Measurement Costs

There is a cost to measuring what matters. The metrics that reveal genuine health are also the metrics that reveal genuine failure — that force the pastor to look at the gap between the church they believe they are leading and the church that the data describes. Most pastors resist this not because they are lazy but because the data is painful. The discipleship depth indicators that reveal a congregation that has been attending for years without being genuinely formed are not easy numbers to sit with.

But the alternative is worse. The pastor who measures only what is easy to count and then makes decisions based on those numbers is building strategy on a foundation that will not bear the weight of the mission. The church needs leaders who are willing to measure what matters — even when what matters reveals how much remains to be done.

Track what matters. The conversation it generates is worth the work. And the honesty it requires is itself a form of the health you are trying to build.
