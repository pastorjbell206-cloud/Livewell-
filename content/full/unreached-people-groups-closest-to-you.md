Drive past your church building at eleven on a Tuesday morning and count the people within a mile radius who will have no meaningful contact with the gospel this week. Count the apartment complex where recent immigrants from Somalia live in quiet and considerable isolation. Count the truck stop twenty minutes outside town where men sleep in their cabs because they cannot afford a room. Count the nursing facility with eighty residents, three of whom have a visitor more than once a month. Count the county jail.

Then tell me where the unreached people groups are.

---

The missionary tradition in American Christianity has a specific geography: far away and difficult to get to. The maps with the red circles over the 10/40 Window [cite — confirm] — the band of latitude between ten and forty degrees north encompassing the most densely non-Christian populations on earth — form the visual imagination of global mission, and they are not wrong. Those populations deserve the church's attention and resources and prayer. The 7,000-plus people groups worldwide with no indigenous evangelical church, the communities that have no access to Scripture in their language, the places where naming the name of Christ requires a willingness to die for it — these represent genuine missionary urgency, and the church's commitment to them is not a mistake.

But the geography of mission in Scripture is more complicated than that. When Paul writes in Romans 15:20 that he makes it his ambition to preach the gospel where Christ has not already been named, he is not describing a continent. He is describing a posture. The ambition is to go where the good news has not landed — not to define that location from a conference in Colorado or to assume that "not yet reached" means "not yet reached by someone from somewhere far away."

There are populations within your city — possibly within your zip code — where the gospel has not, in any meaningful sense, landed. Not because there are no church buildings nearby. But because the church buildings are not inhabited by those populations, do not speak their languages, do not operate on their schedules, do not exist in their social world, and have never seriously tried to.

---

The immigrant community is the most obvious and most neglected example. American cities have experienced significant demographic change from immigration in the past three decades [cite — confirm], and the communities that formed in strip-mall mosques and apartment complexes and informal networks of mutual aid are not, by and large, communities in conversation with the local evangelical church. They are not in conversation with us partly because we have not sought them, partly because the cultural and linguistic distance requires significant investment to cross, and partly because the crossing will cost us something and we have quietly decided the cost is not worth bearing.

That last part deserves honesty. The cultural distance is real, and navigating it requires more than good intentions. It requires learning. Language learning, or at minimum the humility of entering a linguistic environment where you are the one without competence — where the tables are turned and the person who arrived last is the most capable in the room. It requires the willingness to be a guest in someone else's social world before extending an invitation to your own. It requires the renegotiation of what "church" looks like when the congregation is primarily first-generation immigrants with different assumptions about time, space, authority, community, and the relationship between faith and ethnicity than the church you built for people who look like you.

The congregations doing this well are not the ones with the best cross-cultural training programs. They are the ones whose pastors went first — who walked into the apartment complex uninvited and began meeting people, who showed up at the cultural festival and stayed long enough to have an actual conversation, who found the community leader and asked what the community needed rather than announcing what the church planned to offer.

---

The incarcerated population is another unreached group that the church walks past daily. The United States incarcerates people at a rate that is extraordinary by global standards [cite — confirm] — roughly two million people at any given time, with millions more cycling through pre-trial detention each year. The county jail in your community holds men and women who are, in many cases, more genuinely open to the gospel than anyone sitting in your Sunday morning congregation — because the conditions of their lives have stripped away the comfortable alternatives. They have time. They have stripped-down conditions that make the fundamental questions unavoidable. They are often cut off from the social networks that make it possible to avoid direct engagement with the state of their own souls.

The church that has a jail ministry carried by one or two volunteers who visit on Thursday evenings has done something. The church that has integrated the formerly incarcerated into its actual community — that has understood reentry as a mission field requiring the same sustained investment as a church plant in another country, that has people from the congregation serving as mentors and employers and friends to people coming out of incarceration — that church is rare. The gap between what the church offers and what the Scripture requires of it on this question is among the widest in American Christianity. Matthew 25:36 is not ambiguous: "I was in prison and you came to visit me." The Matthean logic is not "I was in prison and you started a Thursday evening program." It is "you came."

---

The elderly isolated are not usually discussed in missionary terms at all. They are understood as a pastoral care challenge rather than a mission field. But the eighty-year-old in the nursing facility who has never been asked what she believes about God — who was raised in a nominally religious household, who attended church at Christmas for a few decades, who has never made any kind of personal faith commitment, and who has now been alone with her thoughts for three years — she is unreached. The pastoral imagination that sees her only as someone to visit, rather than someone to whom the gospel has not yet come, has missed what the text requires.

The same is true of the homebound, the deeply depressed, the people whose social circumstances — poverty, disability, mental illness, histories that make them difficult to be around — have resulted in a practical exclusion from the social world in which Christianity primarily operates in America. The gospel was announced first to shepherds — Luke 2:8 places the first proclamation not in the temple or among the respectable but in a field at night among workers whose occupation rendered them ritually impure and socially marginal by the standards of first-century Judaism. The first recipients of the announcement were not in a position to spread it effectively through respectable social networks. They were exactly the kind of people who are nowhere near your Sunday morning attendance count.

---

Jesus sent the seventy-two out in Luke 10 and told them to go to every town and place where he was about to go. Every town. Every place. Not the strategically significant ones. Not the ones with high potential for multiplication or favorable demographics or existing relationships with the sending church. Every place.

The missiology that limits the missionary imagination to the far away is a distortion that serves the institutional church's preference for distance. Distance is comfortable in a specific way: it allows the church to feel missional without requiring it to cross the social and cultural boundaries that are genuinely proximate. The neighbor who is a different ethnicity, who is behind bars, who is dying in a facility with no visitors — they require a different kind of crossing than the church member who buys mission calendars and prays for distant people groups. The crossing required is personal and costly and cannot be delegated to a professional missionary who is working somewhere else.

The unreached people nearest to you are not a consolation prize for the churches that cannot afford to go far. They are the mission. They have always been the mission.

---

Let me make this concrete. In Fenton, Michigan — the small city where I pastor — there are populations within walking distance of our building who have had no meaningful engagement with the gospel in their lifetimes. Recent immigrants. The incarcerated at the county level. The elderly in facilities nearby. The families whose economic circumstances have placed them outside the social world the church inhabits.

These are not populations I have fully reached. I have not invested in all of them with the seriousness the text requires. I have had the convenient thought that the farther mission was more urgent, that the global commitment justified a degree of local neglect, that starting closer to home would have to wait until the infrastructure was better positioned.

That is not faithfulness. That is a deferral dressed as strategy.

The commitment to global mission and the commitment to the unreached at your gate are not in competition. The tradition of the church has understood since Acts 1:8 — "Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" — that the geography of witness is concentric, not exclusive. The word "and" appears twice. Not "or." Not "eventually." The Jerusalem and the ends of the earth are simultaneous callings, not sequential ones.

The distance that matters is not geographic.

It is the distance between the church you have built and the people God has placed in its shadow. Whether you close that distance is a theological question, not a logistical one. The logistics are solvable. The will is the question.

Who is within a mile of your building right now, waiting for something the church claims to carry?
