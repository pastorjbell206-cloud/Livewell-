There is a city that the prophet Jeremiah addressed in a letter to the exiles, and the instruction he gave them was this: "Seek the shalom of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its shalom you will find your shalom" (Jeremiah 29:7). He did not say: seek the shalom of your particular congregation. He said the city. The whole city.

Most of us preach that text and then go back to competing with the church three blocks over for the same pool of transfer Christians.

---

The fragmentation of the church was not inevitable. The church described in the opening chapters of Acts was a single community that met across multiple households, held goods in common, and was understood by its own members and by outside observers as a coherent body rather than a collection of competing congregations (Acts 2:42-47). The development of distinct local congregations, each with its own leadership structure and gathering, emerged gradually as the movement grew and spread — and Paul's letters are largely an extended argument against the tendency of those congregations to regard their particular expression as primary and the larger body as secondary.

The Corinthian church had already begun fragmenting in Paul's lifetime: "I follow Paul," "I follow Apollos," "I follow Cephas" (1 Corinthians 1:12). His response was not to adjudicate which teacher was better but to insist that the division itself was the problem. "Is Christ divided?" (1 Corinthians 1:13). The question was rhetorical. The answer was supposed to be obvious. That the answer has become increasingly less obvious in the fifteen centuries of Western Christian history since the Constantinian settlement is one of the most significant failures of the movement.

We have, in many American cities, a situation that would have been unrecognizable to the Corinthian community: dozens or hundreds of congregations within a few square miles, most of them unknown to each other, almost none of them sharing resources, many of them quietly regarding each other as rivals. The language we use to describe this — the "local church model," the "autonomy of the congregation," the "sovereignty of the individual expression" — has a theological vocabulary that obscures what it often actually is: institutional tribalism.

---

I have done this. The competition is real, and it is almost never acknowledged honestly. Pastors will talk about the kingdom while keeping meticulous track of who left their church to attend the newer one with the better sound system. We dress it in the language of stewardship — we are responsible for our own flock — and there is truth in that. But the stewardship argument has a way of becoming cover for the thing it is covering: the provincialism of the local church at the expense of the body of Christ in a given place.

The congregation does not belong to the pastor. The city does not belong to any congregation. The church in a given place is one thing — one body, one Spirit, one Lord, one baptism, one God and Father of all (Ephesians 4:4-6) — expressed in multiple locations, multiple traditions, multiple worship styles, multiple languages and ethnicities. Until pastors believe that at the level of their calendars, it remains a pious idea.

---

At minimum, genuine city-wide ecclesiology looks like the pastors of that city knowing each other. Not performing unity at a ministerial breakfast while quietly regarding each other as competitors. Actually knowing each other — the struggles, the failures, the things that keep them awake at three in the morning. The referral network, the resource-sharing, the collaborative response to the neighborhood's needs — all of that depends on relationships that go deeper than the professional courtesy we typically extend to each other at association meetings.

But this is not primarily strategic. It is theological. The pastor who regards himself as a co-laborer in a single city-wide mission will make different decisions than the pastor who regards himself as the CEO of a rival enterprise. The former will celebrate when the church down the street baptizes thirty people. The latter will wonder what that does to the attendance projections for September.

At least once a year, the theological conviction should be made visible to the congregation.

A city-wide gathering — churches from different traditions, different demographics, different theological emphases, meeting in a park or an arena or the largest sanctuary that will hold them — does something that no individual congregation can do alone. It makes visible what is otherwise invisible: that the church in this city is one thing, larger than any of its expressions, more durable than any single pastor's tenure, accountable to a story that began before any of us arrived and will continue after all of us have gone.

The formation that happens in that gathering is not easily replicated by preaching. When the people in your congregation see their pastor standing alongside the pastor of the historically Black church across town and the pastor of the Latino congregation meeting in the strip mall and the pastor of the Korean congregation in the office park, and they worship together to music that is not what any of them expected, something shifts in how they understand the body of Christ. They see that the Spirit is not confined to their worship style. That the kingdom extends beyond the zip code they drive to on Sunday morning. That the church they belong to is considerably larger than the building they attend.

---

The practical objections are real. Theology divides — Reformed and Pentecostal, Baptist and Methodist, churches that practice closed communion and churches with open tables. Logistics are difficult. Egos are involved. Somebody will not want to share a stage with somebody else, and the reasons will be dressed in doctrinal language while being mostly personal.

The theological objection deserves a direct response: genuine unity does not require doctrinal uniformity. It requires the acknowledgment of a common Lord. Paul's argument in Ephesians 4 is precisely that the gifts of the Spirit are distributed across the body, which means that no single congregation possesses all of them, and a church that operates as though it does is impoverishing itself. The Reformed congregation has things to learn from the Pentecostal congregation, and vice versa. The historically Black church has been formed by centuries of suffering and survival that the white suburban church has not experienced, and that formation produces a depth of faith that the comfortable church desperately needs. The gathering is not an exercise in theological compromise. It is an exercise in recognizing what the body of Christ actually looks like when it is not divided by institutional convenience.

The logistical objection is real, and the answer is: do it anyway. The difficulty of the logistics is not an argument against the gathering; it is part of the work. Every good thing requires effort, and the congregation that sees its pastor do hard work for the sake of the city rather than the sake of the brand learns something about ministry that sermons cannot teach.

I understand all the objections. I have been the pastor who found reasons not to show up. I have protected the Sunday morning program at the expense of the city-wide relationship. I have told myself it was stewardship and known, somewhere below the rationalizations, that it was something smaller.

---

The Reformation, for all its genuine theological recovery, fractured the Western church into pieces that have been multiplying ever since. There are an estimated 45,000 Protestant denominations worldwide [cite — confirm]. In any given mid-sized American city, you can find congregations that share the same confession of faith and have not shared a pulpit in decades because of a church split in 1987 over carpet color or staff compensation or a particular interpretation of a secondary doctrine. The divisions that divide the church in a given city are almost never about the things that matter most. They are about the things that matter least, and they have calcified into institutional identities that we now treat as load-bearing walls.

They are not load-bearing walls. They are partitions.

Jeremiah sent his letter to people who wanted to go home — who regarded exile as an interruption of their real life in their real city. He told them instead to plant gardens, build houses, raise families, and pray for the city they were in (Jeremiah 29:5-7). The instruction presupposes that they would be there long enough for the planting to matter.

We have been planted in this city. The question is whether we are praying for it together, or whether we are each praying for our own corner of it and calling that faithfulness.

---

What does a city-wide gathering produce in the long run? Not unity as the absence of disagreement — that is not a New Testament vision of anything. What it produces is visibility. The watching city sees a church that is larger and more diverse than any single congregation. The watching city sees people who cross ethnic, economic, and denominational lines because they believe they serve a Lord whose body does not observe those lines. The watching city — which has been, in many cases, watching the church argue about itself for a generation — sees something that it cannot fully account for.

That is witness. Not the programmatic kind. The kind that requires something to actually be true.

The early church was known for it. Tertullian's observation — "see how they love one another" [cite — confirm] — was not a compliment about their worship programming. It was an observation about a community that defied the social logic of the ancient world, a community in which slave and free, Jew and Greek, male and female sat at the same table and called each other family.

That is the church the city needs. It is assembled not building by building but together, at least once a year, long enough for the people in the seats to see what they belong to.

Long enough to remember that it is bigger than what they can see from where they usually sit.
