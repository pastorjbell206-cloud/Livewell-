# LiveWell Usability & Comprehension Testing Kit (L4A)

> Gap-closing program, Prompt L4A. A ready-to-run kit for testing the site with
> real readers. Claude cannot *be* a representative reader, so this prepares the
> study; a person runs it (L4B synthesizes the results). Consolidated into one
> doc. Nothing here changes the site.
>
> **The goal:** find where the site's assumptions differ from how real readers
> understand it — comprehension, orientation, reading continuity, subscription
> expectations, and whether the skeptic feels *met or handled*.

## Who to recruit (5–8 is enough to find the big problems)

Aim for a spread, not quotas: at least one from each, and at least one who is
mobile-only and one who uses a screen reader or magnification if reachable.
1. An existing LiveWell reader.
2. A thoughtful Christian who has never heard of James Bell.
3. A pastor or ministry leader.
4. A church-wounded or politically-weary reader still open to serious faith.
5. A newsletter-reader who has not used this site.
6. A skeptic / non-believer willing to read seriously.

Screener questions (keep it short, collect no unnecessary sensitive detail):
"How often do you read long-form writing online?" · "Do you subscribe to any
email newsletters?" · "What device will you use for this?" · "Any accessibility
tools you use to browse?" Record a participant **ID**, segment, and device — no
names or emails in any shared notes.

## Consent script (read aloud, get a yes)

"This is a study of a website, not a test of you — there are no wrong answers,
and anything confusing is the site's fault, not yours. I'll ask you to do a few
tasks and think out loud. I'd like to record the screen and audio to review
later; it stays private to the team and is deleted after we write up findings.
You can stop anytime and skip anything. Is that okay?" (Offer a no-recording
option. Never pull a pastoral-care relationship into research.)

## The session (about 30 minutes)

**A. Five-second comprehension.** Show the homepage for 5 seconds, hide it, ask:
"What is this site? Who is it for? What could you do here?" *(Looking for: do
they grasp "serious theological writing for people wrestling with faith and
life," or do they think blog/church/store?)*

**B. First-click, no scrolling help.** For each, note where they click *first*:
1. "You're new here. Where would you begin?"
2. "Find something to read about a struggle you actually have."
3. "You've read one essay and want another — do that without the Back button."
4. "What would subscribing give you, and where would the email come from?"
5. "Find out who James Bell is — without losing your place in the reading."
6. "Find a book or a resource, and tell me what you can do with it."
7. "Is this the same as James's church? How can you tell?"

**C. Think-aloud reading.** "Open a long essay and read as you normally would."
Watch: do they finish? use headings? follow links? hit anything that breaks
trust? Then: "If you wanted to go deeper, what would you do?"

**D. Subscription.** "Subscribe with this test address, or tell me what would
stop you." Watch the form, the error path if any, and whether the Substack
handoff confuses them.

**E. Recovery.** Send them to a made-up URL (a 404) and an empty search: "Get
yourself back to something useful."

Ask *"what do you think this means?"* and *"what would you do next?"* — never
*"do you like this?"* Do not point to answers.

## Findings template (for L4B — the human-run synthesis)

For each finding: observed behavior · participants affected · frequency ·
severity (P0 blocks a task / creates misleading consent / a11y barrier → P1
repeated confusion → P2 clarity → DEFER preference) · confidence · the task it
came from · recommended action. Group by: comprehension · orientation ·
navigation · reading continuity · subscription · books/resources · cross-property
· mobile · accessibility · error recovery.

**Then run L4B** (`docs/CORPUS-DEPTH-AUDIT.md`-style discipline: implement only
findings with converging evidence, severe failure, or accessibility necessity;
never redesign around taste; retest the changed task).

## The one test that governs the rest
Would the skeptic — intelligent, injured, allergic to being handled — finish a
session feeling *met*, or *handled*? If handled, that is the finding, whatever
the task metrics say.
