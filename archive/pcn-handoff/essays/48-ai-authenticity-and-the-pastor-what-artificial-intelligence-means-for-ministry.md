On November 30, 2022, a research lab released a chatbot, and within a few months pastors I know (good men, serious men) were quietly typing sermon texts into it to see what would come back. I know because I did it too. I gave the machine a passage and a congregation-shaped prompt, and it returned something in seconds that was structured, orthodox on its surface, and deliverable. I sat looking at a screen that had just done in eight seconds what takes me fifteen hours, and I understood that the question had changed. The old question was whether a machine could write a sermon. It can. The question now is what a sermon is, and whether we still know.

## The Temptation Is Not New. The Speed Is.

Every generation of preachers has met a technology that promised to carry part of the load. Gutenberg's press put the text into the hands of the people and ended the preacher's monopoly on it. Radio and television detached the preaching voice from the preaching body and gave us the celebrity pulpit, the audience of millions who received the Word without a community to receive it in. The searchable sermon database gave a tired pastor on a Thursday night ten thousand other men's outlines and a fresh temptation to preach one of them. The press did not preach. The radio did not preach. The database did not preach. Neither does the machine.

But it is not honest to stop there, because this technology differs from those in one way that matters. The press and the radio distributed what a human being had already wrestled into existence. This one produces the wrestling's product without the wrestling. It hands you the artifact of an encounter that never happened. That is not a faster horse. That is a new thing under the pulpit, and pastors who wave it off with the Gutenberg comparison have not sat with it long enough to feel the difference.

## The Cause Is Older Than the Machine

Jacques Ellul saw this coming before the transistor was in every home. In The Technological Society, published in French in 1954, he argued that the defining force of the modern world is not any particular machine but what he called technique, the relentless drive to find, in every field of human activity, the one most efficient method, and then to let efficiency become the standard that judges everything else. Technique does not ask whether a thing should be done the slow way. It cannot ask that. It only asks what the slow way costs, and the slow way always loses the audit. Ellul's darkest claim was that technique does not stay in the factory; it colonizes the human interior, until we evaluate prayer, friendship, and worship by the same metric we use for supply chains.

Read that against the pastor's Thursday and the diagnosis lands. The machine did not create the temptation to treat sermon preparation as an inefficiency. Technique had been preparing that verdict for a century, the ministry rebuilt around throughput, the pastor as content producer, the sermon as weekly deliverable, the church calendar as a production schedule. Neil Postman, in Technopoly in 1992, added the warning we needed and ignored: every new technology is a bargain, and it never announces its price; it gives us something we can see and takes something we will not notice until it is gone. We adopted each tool asking only what it gave. Nobody asked what the sermon database took. Now the machine arrives at the end of that long surrender and makes the final offer: the entire process of preparation, outsourced, and nobody in the pews will know.

That last clause is the tell. Nobody will know. Any use of a tool that depends on nobody knowing has already been judged, and not by me.

## What the Hours Were Actually For

Here is what the efficiency audit cannot see. The fifteen hours were never the price of the sermon. They were the sermon happening to the preacher first.

I have five sons, and I have watched what happens when the struggle is optional. A boy who can generate the essay does not become the man the essay-writing was quietly building. The assignment was never really the essay; the essay was the byproduct. The assignment was the formation of a mind that can hold an argument, and there is no efficient path to that, because the inefficiency is the mechanism. I say this to my sons about their homework, and then I sit down on a Tuesday with a text that will not open and a machine one browser tab away, and I discover that the sermon on temptation was for me. The hours a pastor spends being defeated by a passage, reading it until it reads him, carrying it into the hospital room and the counseling session and his own three-in-the-morning doubts, those hours are not research. They are the encounter the congregation will actually receive on Sunday. Take them away and something will still be preached. It will be about the text. It will no longer be from it.

Paul told the Corinthians what the office is: "For what we proclaim is not ourselves, but Jesus Christ as Lord, with ourselves as your servants for Jesus' sake" (2 Corinthians 4:5 ESV). Notice the strange grammar of that sentence, not ourselves, and yet ourselves are in it, handed over, servants for Jesus' sake. The preacher is not the message, and the preacher cannot be subtracted from the message. The Psalms are the permanent proof. Psalm 88 ends in the dark, without resolution: "my companions have become darkness" (Psalm 88:18 ESV). No system produced that line by sampling human expressions of grief. A man was in that dark, and the line has weight because he was, and three thousand years of sufferers have prayed it because the weight is real. The congregation can tell the difference between testimony and content. Not always consciously. But they can tell.

## The Strongest Case for the Machine

Now the other side, in the form its defenders would sign, because there is a serious one and it is not made by lazy men. The bivocational pastor works forty hours at the shop and preaches Sunday. He has no research assistant, no protected study day, no shelf of commentaries. If the machine can compress ten hours of background work (the historical setting, the state of the scholarly argument, the range of readings on a disputed verse) into one, then the actual encounter with the text gets the hours he has left instead of none of them. The church does not exist to protect my preparation ritual. It exists to feed people. If a tool feeds them better, the burden of proof sits on the man forbidding the tool, not the man using it. And the pastors making this argument are frequently the ones serving the congregations nobody else would take, at a cost the full-time critic has never paid.

Almost all of that is right. The error is one inch wide and a mile deep: it treats research and encounter as one phase, when the whole question is the line between them. Let the machine gather; it gathers well. What it must not do is conclude, because the conclusion, the moment the text breaks open against the specific lives in your specific pews, is the part of the work that is the work. The danger was never that the machine writes bad sermons. The danger is that it writes passable ones, and that passable, fluent, doctrinally unobjectionable content will be delivered by a man it never happened to, to people who needed a witness and received a summary.

## The Sermon That Cost Nothing

A pastor told me about the Sunday he preached what the machine had drafted. He was exhausted, a family crisis had eaten the week, and the draft was sound, he checked it, he edited it, nothing in it was false. He said he could hear it while he preached: not an error, an absence. The place where his own life usually breaks open against the text was smooth. Afterward three members told him, separately, that something had felt different, and none of them could say what. He knew what. They had come for bread and been handed a photograph of bread, well lit, correctly composed, nourishing no one.

I have not preached the machine's sermon. I want that on the record, and I want the next sentence beside it: the only reason is that the fear got to me before the deadline did. The temptation is not theoretical to me, and any pastor who claims it is theoretical to him is either lying or has not yet had the right kind of bad week. This indictment has my name inside it. That is why I trust it.

## The Line, and Who Draws It

So where is the line. Let the machine do what an assistant could do without scandal: gather background, check the grammar of the newsletter, draft the administrative letter, list the questions a text has raised across the church's history. Refuse it the moment it starts doing what only the called man can do: deciding what this text says to these people this week, and generating the story, the application, the voice. And tell the truth about which side of the line you are on, to yourself first, then to the elders who hold you accountable. A congregation that discovers its pastor has been outsourcing the encounter will not primarily feel cheated of quality. They will feel what sheep feel when they learn the shepherd sent a recording of himself to the field.

Eugene Peterson spent his whole working life insisting that pastoral work is local and particular (this congregation, these names, this soil) and that the pastor who dreams of scale has already begun to leave. The machine is the apotheosis of scale: every sermon, for everyone, from no one. Which is exactly why this moment, honestly met, could be the best thing to happen to preaching in a generation. The world is about to be flooded with fluent religious content produced by nothing and costing nothing. In that flood, the one thing that cannot be generated is a man your people have watched suffer, repent, bury their dead, and stand up on Sunday still believing, telling them what he found in the text this week because he was actually there.

The machine will get better. The drafts will get smoother. The temptation will grow every year for the rest of your ministry, and no denominational policy will hold the line for you at eleven o'clock on a Thursday night. What your people need from you was never content, and after this, content is free. What they need is a witness. Witnesses cannot be generated. They can only be formed, slowly, in the hours the machine was built to save you.
