A pastor stands at the door after the third service, and forty people tell him it was exactly what they needed, and he drives home and cannot think of a single person he could call to say he is not okay. He leads a staff of twelve. He has elders who pray for him by name. He is surrounded all week by people who depend on him, who admire him, who would say without hesitation that they love him. And he is alone in a way that none of them can see, because the seat he sits in has put a pane of glass between him and everyone who reports to him, and he installed the glass himself and forgot he did it.

## The Loneliness We Do Not Examine

The loneliness of the senior leader is one of the most documented and least discussed facts of ministry. Pastors leave the work in great numbers, and when Barna asked those who had considered quitting what weighed on them, more than two in five named loneliness and isolation among the reasons (Barna 2021). We acknowledge this loneliness with a kind of sympathy that does nothing, the way we acknowledge that the work is hard. What we rarely do is look at where the loneliness comes from, because the source is uncomfortable. The loneliness of the senior seat is not only something done to the leader. It is largely something the leader does to himself, through power, and the cure he reaches for makes it worse.

> The loneliness of the senior leader is one of the most documented and least discussed facts of ministry.

Let me steel-man the leader who keeps his distance, because his reasons are not foolish and I have used every one of them. He will tell you, correctly, that he cannot be fully transparent with people he supervises, that there are confidences he must keep, that a leader who falls apart in front of his team destabilizes the whole team. He will tell you that authority requires a measure of separateness, that the captain cannot drink with the crew, that someone has to carry what cannot be shared. And he is right about all of it. The seat does require a real and necessary solitude. Some of the distance is the cost of the office, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of dishonesty.

## Necessary Solitude and Self-Protection

But he has done something with that legitimate truth that turns it into a prison. He has taken the necessary solitude of the role and quietly expanded it into a total isolation that serves his power rather than his people. There is a difference between the loneliness that comes from carrying confidences and the loneliness that comes from never letting anyone see you need anything. The first is the cost of leadership. The second is the fruit of pride dressed as duty. He has confused them on purpose, because the second kind protects an image, and the image has become more precious to him than the relationships, and he calls the whole thing the loneliness of command when half of it is the loneliness of self-protection.

## The Long Sad Arc of Saul

The text I want to carry is the long, sad arc of Saul, the first king of Israel, because no story in Scripture shows more plainly what power does to a man who uses it to protect himself. Saul began among the people, hiding among the baggage on the day he was chosen, a head taller than everyone and somehow still shy of the seat (1 Samuel 10:22-23 ESV). But once the crown was on him the seat began to close around him. When the women sang that David had struck down his ten thousands and Saul his thousands, the king eyed David from that day on (1 Samuel 18:9 ESV), and the eyeing never stopped. He grew suspicious of his own son, of his own servants, of the one man at court who loved him most, until he sat in his hall with a spear in his hand and no friend left in the room. Read what isolated him. Not the office. The office did not have to do this to him. What isolated Saul was a power he used to guard his standing instead of to serve his people, and the more he guarded it the more alone he became, until the lonely seat had swallowed him whole. That is the medicine taken in reverse, the warning written across a king's whole reign.

Because here is what power does to most of us, and the social science is as old as the church's amnesia about it. Edwin Friedman observed that leaders become isolated not primarily because others push them away but because they stop being honestly engaged, hiding behind the role, managing impressions, surrounding themselves with people who will not contradict them (Friedman 2007). The lonely leader has often, without admitting it, selected for agreement. He has promoted the people who affirm him and grown cool toward the ones who push, and over years he has built a court rather than a team, and a court is the loneliest place on earth because everyone in it is performing for the king. The isolation he laments is the harvest of choices he made one staffing decision at a time.

## The Cult of the Singular Visionary

The history here runs deep and it indicts our whole model. The cult of the singular visionary pastor, the leader as charismatic founder whose voice cannot be questioned, took its modern shape in the church growth movement of the nineteen seventies and eighties and hardened into the celebrity pastor era that followed. We built churches around one magnetic personality and then watched, decade after decade, as those personalities fell, in moral collapse and in burnout and in the slow corrosion of men who had no peers because they had been elevated above everyone they knew. The architecture that made them powerful is the same architecture that made them alone. We keep building it. We keep being surprised when it produces the same wreckage it has always produced.

## Vulnerability From Within the Power

Henri Nouwen saw the spiritual mechanism under all of it, and he named the temptation with a precision that still stings. He wrote that the great seduction of Christian leadership is the temptation to power, to be the one in control, and that this temptation is so dangerous precisely because it is so easy to confuse the desire to rule with the desire to serve (Nouwen 1989). The antidote he prescribed was not better self-protection or more self-care. It was confession and community, the deliberate placing of oneself among people to whom one is accountable and before whom one is known, the refusal to lead alone. The lonely pastor has often medicated his loneliness with more control, and control is the drug that deepens the disease it pretends to treat.

> The solution to the loneliness of power is not more power and it is not less responsibility.

Here is the reversal, and it is the hinge of everything. The solution to the loneliness of power is not more power and it is not less responsibility. It is vulnerability exercised from within the power, the choice to let someone see you while you still hold the authority. Where Saul guarded the throne until it isolated him, David, for all his sins, kept the throne and kept being known, wept openly, bound his soul to Jonathan, let the prophet Nathan walk in and tell him to his face that he was the man. This is terrifying, because we believe that to be seen as needy is to forfeit the right to lead. The cross says the reverse. The One with all authority in heaven and earth knelt and was stripped and let himself be seen at his most exposed, and he did not lose his authority by descending. He revealed it. Authority that cannot be seen needing is not authority, it is insecurity holding a title, and everyone on the staff can feel the difference even when they cannot name it.

## Being Known by a Few

What this asks of us is concrete and costly. It asks the senior leader to build, on purpose, a set of relationships outside his chain of command where he is not the pastor but simply a man who needs God, peers who can tell him the truth because their livelihood does not depend on his mood. It asks him to find a spiritual director or a soul friend, what Ruth Haley Barton calls the company of others who will help him discern his own deceptions, because the leader cannot see his own blind spots by definition and will go down with them if no one is permitted close enough to point (Barton 2008). And it asks him to stop curating a staff of admirers and start keeping the people who push back, treating their resistance as a gift instead of a threat.

> We choose the glass.

I have lived on both sides of the glass, and the difference is not subtle once you have felt it. There were years when I told myself the distance between me and my staff was the dignity of the office, and what it actually was, underneath, was fear of being found out, the dread that if anyone got close enough they would discover I was less than the man who stood on the platform. So I kept everyone at the range of admiration, which is a safe and lonely range, and I mistook the safety for wisdom. What broke it was not a strategy. It was a few men outside my church who refused to be impressed by me, who asked how my marriage was and would not accept the sermon-version of the answer, and who slowly taught me that being known by a few is the only thing that makes being admired by many survivable. Bonhoeffer wrote that the person who cannot bear to be alone should beware of community, and that the person who cannot bear community should beware of being alone, because we need both and we will be destroyed by whichever one we use to flee the other (Bonhoeffer 1954). The lonely leader has used the crowd to flee the few, and it is killing him by the inch.

The lonely seat will not be cured by sympathy and it will not be cured by stepping down. It will be cured, if it is cured at all, by the slow and humbling work of letting yourself be known by people you cannot control, which is the only kind of being known that counts. Most of us would rather stay lonely than do that, because the loneliness, for all its pain, lets us keep the image intact. We choose the glass. We sit behind it and ache and call it the price of leadership, when half the time it is the price of pride.

Saul died on a mountain with no one left who could reach him, a king who had spent his whole reign making sure of it. Put down the power you are hiding behind, and you might not end there.
