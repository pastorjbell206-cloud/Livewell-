The numbers came to me on a Tuesday. Average attendance down a third over four years, the giving following it like a shadow, three funerals and no baptisms, and a roof that would not last another Michigan winter. I had the spreadsheet open on the kitchen table when one of my sons asked what was wrong, and I could not answer him, because the honest answer was that I did not know how to make a dying thing live. That is where every revitalization actually begins. Not with a plan. With a man who has run out of plans.

## The Wrong First Question

We reach for the wrong question first. We ask how to grow the church, as if growth were the disease working backward, as if the absence of people were the whole of the sickness. But a church can grow numerically and be dead, and a church can shrink and be more alive than it has been in twenty years. The real question is not how to reverse the decline. The real question is whether the body in front of us still wants to be a church at all, or whether it wants to be a club with a steeple, a place where the familiar faces gather to manage their own slow ending with dignity. You cannot answer that question with a survey. You answer it by preaching the gospel and watching who flinches.

## Answering the Charge at Full Strength

> Not with a plan. With a man who has run out of plans.

There is a strong case against pastors like me even using the word revitalization, and it deserves to be heard at full strength. The critics say that most revitalization talk is church-growth machinery in vestments, that we have taken the language of the New Testament and welded it to attendance metrics borrowed from retail, and that the result is a generation of pastors who measure the Spirit by the parking lot. They are right about more than we would like. Eugene Peterson spent a career warning that the pastoral vocation had been colonized by the shopkeeper's mentality, that we had become managers of religious consumers, fussing over the displays (Peterson 1993, 1). When the critics say our revitalization is often just a more spiritual word for marketing, they have caught us. So let me answer the strongest version of the charge, not the weak one. The strong version is this. A church is not a business and cannot be saved by business means, because its life was never its own to begin with. That is true. And it is precisely why revitalization is possible. A thing that lives by its own strength dies when that strength runs out. A thing that lives by resurrection has a future its accountants cannot see.

Which is why I keep returning to one text, the one I cannot preach without my voice catching. The prophet is set down in the middle of a valley, and the valley is full of bones, and the bones are very many and they are very dry (Ezekiel 37:2 ESV). This is not a church with a budget problem. This is a graveyard with no expectation of a morning. And the Lord asks Ezekiel the only question that matters in a dying church. Son of man, can these bones live. Notice that God does not ask whether Ezekiel can make them live. He asks whether Ezekiel believes they can. And Ezekiel, who is wiser than every consultant I have ever hired, refuses to lie in either direction. He will not say yes, because the evidence is bone-dry against him. He will not say no, because he has met this God before. O Lord God, you know (Ezekiel 37:3 ESV). That is the posture revitalization requires. Not optimism. Not despair. Surrender of the verdict to the only One who has ever raised anything.

## The Valley of Dry Bones

We forget how we got here, and the forgetting keeps us reaching for shallow cures. The decline of the inherited church in the West is not a failure of one denomination or one decade. Lesslie Newbigin came home to England in 1974 after thirty-eight years as a missionary in India and found a country that had become, in his words, a pagan society whose public life was governed by assumptions no longer Christian, and harder to reach than any non-Christian culture he had served abroad (Newbigin 1986, 20). He saw what most pastors still refuse to see. The dying church is not a patient with a treatable infection. It is the visible edge of a civilization that quietly relocated its faith from the church to the self somewhere between the Enlightenment and the shopping mall. Charles Taylor traced the architecture of it, the long migration from a world where belief was effortless to a world where belief is one option among many and not the easiest one (Taylor 2007, 3). If you do not see the size of the problem, you will treat a five-hundred-year shift with a six-week sermon series, and you will fail, and you will blame the people in the pews who are simply the last to feel the cold.

## A Five-Hundred-Year Cold

> That is the posture revitalization requires. Not optimism. Not despair. Surrender of the verdict to the only One who has ever raised anything.

So what do we actually do. Here is the reversal that took me years to accept. The pastor does not revitalize the church. The pastor is the first one who has to die. I came into a struggling congregation convinced my energy was the cure, that if I prayed harder and worked longer and programmed smarter the bones would rattle. What I learned is that my frantic competence was part of the death. I was offering them a healthier version of self-reliance, and a church cannot be saved from self-reliance by a more spiritual self-reliance. The dry bones do not come together because the prophet is impressive. They come together because the prophet speaks a word he did not invent over a situation he cannot fix, and then waits for a wind he cannot summon. Prophesy to the breath, the Lord says, and Ezekiel does, and the breath comes (Ezekiel 37:9 ESV). The pastor's whole authority in revitalization is the authority of a man with nothing in his hands but a borrowed word.

## The Pastor Must Die First

This is where the cross does its work, and it does it on the leader before it does it on the congregation. The law comes first, and the law in a dying church is brutal in its honesty. It says your decline is not merely demographic. It says you have loved your own comfort more than the lost neighbor, that you have made the sanctuary a museum of your own memories, that the empty pews are partly the fruit of doors you were happy to keep shut. I have to preach that to myself before I preach it to anyone, because I have done it. I have protected my own schedule from the very people Christ died to reach. The law leaves us in the valley with the bones, and that is exactly where it is supposed to leave us, because grace cannot raise a church that still believes it is only sleeping. Only when we have agreed with God that we are dead can the next sentence land. And you shall know that I am the Lord, when I open your graves (Ezekiel 37:13 ESV). Not when you fix your graves. When I open them.

> The pastor does not revitalize the church. The pastor is the first one who has to die.

The practical work follows from this and never precedes it. You will need to preach the whole counsel of God again, not management wisdom in a clerical collar. You will need to know the names and the wounds of the people actually in the room, because Newbigin was right that the only hermeneutic of the gospel a watching world will accept is a congregation that believes it (Newbigin 1989, 227). You will need to raise up others rather than hoard the work, to be patient across years rather than quarters, to bury programs that died long ago and stop holding their funerals every Sunday. None of that is the source of the life. All of it is the riverbed the life runs through when it comes.

## The Riverbed the Life Runs Through

My congregation is not what it was. Some of that is grace I can point to, baptisms I did not expect, a young family in the third pew who found Christ from outside everything. Some of it is still bone and still dry, and I have made my peace with not knowing the ending. The question God asked Ezekiel He has never once let me answer with statistics. He asks it of every pastor standing in a valley with a spreadsheet and a roof that will not last the winter. Can these bones live. The only faithful answer is the prophet's answer, the answer that refuses both the lie of optimism and the lie of despair. O Lord God, you know. Then we open our mouths, and we speak the word we did not write, and we wait for a wind that has raised the dead before.

The church is not yours to save. It never was.
