Two staff members have not directly spoken to each other in four months, and everyone knows it, and nobody says it. They are professionals. They pass each other in the hall. They sit on the same row in the all-staff meeting and route every actual message through a third person who has become an unwilling diplomat between two people who used to be friends. The team is functioning. The calendar fills, the services happen, the bulletin gets printed. And underneath the function a low current of avoidance is running through everything, and the longer it runs the more the whole team organizes itself around not setting it off.

## How the Church Mislearned Conflict

We have a strange relationship with conflict in the church. We treat it as a failure of sanctification, as if a mature team would simply never disagree, as if the presence of friction proves the absence of the Spirit. So when conflict shows up, and it always shows up, we feel ashamed of it and we hide it. We swallow the disagreement and call the swallowing humility. We let the resentment build and call the building patience. And the conflict does not go anywhere. It just moves down, out of the meeting and into the body, into the headaches and the clipped emails and the Sunday smiles that do not reach the eyes.

> We swallow the disagreement and call the swallowing humility.

Let me steel-man the peacemaker, because he is quoting Jesus and he is not wrong about much. Blessed are the peacemakers. A quarrelsome church is a poor witness, and the world is watching how we treat each other, and Paul begged the Philippians to be of the same mind. The peacemaker has seen what unchecked conflict does, has watched a church split over a building program and never recover, has buried friendships that died in a business meeting. His instinct to protect the unity of the team is not cowardice. It is the scar tissue of someone who has seen the alternative. He is guarding something he has watched bleed out before.

## Peacemaking Is Not Peacekeeping

But he has misread the text he is standing on, and the misreading is costing him the very thing he wants. Peacemaking is not peacekeeping. The peacemaker in the Sermon on the Mount is an active figure who goes into the broken place and makes something, the way a steelworker makes a beam, through heat and pressure and joining. The peacekeeper is a different creature entirely. He keeps the peace by keeping the lid on, and the lid is not peace, it is a delay. What he calls unity is two people not speaking for four months while a third carries their mail. Jesus did not bless that. He blessed the harder and braver thing, the one that walks into the conflict on purpose and stays until something is actually mended.

## The Most Disobeyed Verse in Staff Life

The spine text is plainer than we admit, and it is from Jesus' own mouth. 'If your brother sins against you, go and tell him his fault, between you and him alone' (Matthew 18:15 ESV). Go. The movement is toward the person, not away. Alone, which means not to the third party, not to the prayer chain disguised as concern, not to the spouse over dinner, not to the loose coalition you are quietly building in the hallway. The most disobeyed verse in church staff life is not about money or sex or doctrine. It is this one, the simple command to take the problem to the person and nobody else, and our entire architecture of avoidance exists to get around it.

Watch what triangulation actually does to a team, because it is the disease underneath most church conflict. Edwin Friedman built his whole understanding of congregations on the way anxiety moves through relationships, and he showed that when two people cannot tolerate the tension between them, they will reliably pull in a third to stabilize it (Friedman 2007). The third person feels useful, even chosen, carrying messages and absorbing complaints. But the triangle does not resolve the conflict. It preserves it. It lets two people stay angry indefinitely because the pressure that would have forced them to deal with each other is being bled off through someone else. Every staff has these triangles. Most pastors are sitting inside several of them and feeling needed instead of feeling alarmed.

## The Therapeutic Turn We Inherited

We did not invent this avoidance. We inherited it, and the history clarifies the grip it has. The therapeutic turn in American culture, which the sociologist Philip Rieff diagnosed as early as nineteen sixty-six in his account of the triumph of the therapeutic, taught us to read every relational difficulty as a matter of feelings to be managed and validated rather than truths to be told (Rieff 1966). The church drank deeply from that stream. We learned to prize emotional comfort above honest correction, to fear hurting someone more than we feared leaving them in sin, to mistake niceness for love. A whole generation of church teams now treats the avoidance of hard words as the highest relational virtue, and we cannot find the verse that taught us that, because there is no such verse.

> The conflict you are avoiding is not the threat to the team. The avoidance is the threat.

Here is the reversal. The conflict you are avoiding is not the threat to the team. The avoidance is the threat. Lencioni mapped this with uncomfortable precision when he showed that teams which cannot engage in honest conflict do not become more unified, they become less committed, because people will not buy into a decision they were never allowed to argue with (Lencioni 2002). The silence you are protecting is producing the disengagement you are about to complain about. The meeting where nobody pushes back is not a sign of alignment. It is the sound of people who have stopped believing their voice changes anything, saving their real opinions for the parking lot where the decisions get quietly unmade.

## Leading Into the Conflict

So lead into it. When you sense the four-month silence, name it out loud and decline to be the third corner of the triangle, sending the two people back to each other with your blessing and your expectation. When someone comes to complain about a colleague, the most pastoral question you can ask is whether they have said this to the person directly, and the most pastoral answer to no is to walk with them until they do. Set the table so that conflict in the meeting is normal and conflict in the hallway is not. The team that fights well in the open is the team that does not need to fight in the dark.

And remember that the alternative to honest conflict is not peace but a burden no single person can carry. In the wilderness Moses came apart under exactly this weight. The people quarreled and complained and routed every grievance through him, and he cried out to God that he was not able to carry all this people alone, that the load was too heavy, that he would rather die than keep bearing it by himself (Numbers 11:14 ESV). The Lord's answer was not to make the conflict disappear. It was to spread the weight, to take of the Spirit on Moses and put it on seventy elders so that they would bear the burden of the people with him and Moses would not bear it alone (Numbers 11:17 ESV). Read what God refuses to do. He does not promise Moses a quiet camp. He gives him shared leadership, a body of people willing to stand inside the tension with him rather than route it around him. The leader who absorbs every conflict alone, who lets himself become the third corner of every triangle, is not being strong. He is doing the very thing God told Moses to stop doing, and it will crush him the same way. Nouwen called the alternative the downward movement of Christian leadership, the refusal to escape conflict through power, the choice to remain present and bear the weight together where it would be easier to dominate or disappear (Nouwen 1989).

## What I Mistook for Unity

I have to name my own part in this, because I led a team for years that I was proud of for never fighting, and I have since come to understand that I was proud of the wrong thing. The reason nobody fought in my meetings was not that we had achieved some rare maturity. It was that I had made disagreement expensive, in a hundred small ways I never noticed, and my people had done the arithmetic and decided silence was cheaper. The peace I prized was the peace of a room that had given up on changing my mind. It took a staff member leaving, and telling me on the way out the things she had never felt safe to say while she stayed, before I understood that the quiet I had treated as a trophy was actually the bill coming due. I had mistaken the absence of conflict for the presence of unity, and the two had never been further apart.

A team that has learned to fight honestly and stay is more unified than a team that has never fought at all, the way a bone that healed is stronger than a bone that was never tested. We keep protecting our teams from the one thing that would actually bind them together. We keep choosing the quiet, and the quiet keeps costing us the thing the quiet was supposed to save.

> Peace is not the absence of conflict. It is conflict carried all the way through.
