No one walks out in the middle of a sermon. That is not how a room empties. It empties slowly, over months, through a thousand small decisions made in cars on the way home, the decision that next week can wait, that the children have a game, that nothing will be lost by missing it. The preacher rarely sees the leak because the leak is silent and because he is standing at the exact spot where it begins. I have emptied rooms. Not with scandal. With habits I could not see in myself because I was the one doing them.

Before the list, the strongest objection, and it is one I half believe. A faithful preacher should not be managing the room at all. The numbers are the Spirit's business. Noah preached for decades and saved eight. Jeremiah cleared the building every week and was right every week. If you start preaching to keep people, you will stop preaching the truth, because the truth empties rooms on purpose, and the cross was the emptiest room in history, down to a few women and a borrowed tomb. There is real steel here. The preacher who counts heads will eventually trim the gospel to fit the heads he wants to keep, and a trimmed gospel is no gospel. I hold that with both hands.

## The Offense of the Cross and the Offense of the Preacher

> When they leave because the man in front of them mistook his habits for the gospel, that is not a cross.

But the objection proves less than it claims, because there is a difference between a room emptied by the offense of the cross and a room emptied by the offense of the preacher. Jesus said the word would divide households and that men would hate his disciples for his name (Matthew 10:22 ESV). He did not promise they would hate us for being boring. When people leave because the gospel cut them, that is the cost of faithfulness. When they leave because the man in front of them mistook his habits for the gospel, that is not a cross. That is a waste, and we have learned to baptize our waste by calling it persecution. So examine the habits, and be honest about which kind of emptying you are causing.

The self-deception here is comfortable, which is why it lasts. A man whose preaching is dull can read the parable of the sower and decide his hearers are simply the path, the rocky soil, the thorns, and that the seed was fine all along. The parable gives him cover, because it is true that some hearts are hard. But the same parable says the sower scatters, and a sower who has fallen asleep against the fence post is not faithfully losing seed to bad soil. He is just asleep. I have used the doctrine of the hardened heart to explain away rooms that I myself bored into the parking lot. The doctrine is real. My use of it was a lie. Discernment begins with refusing to let a true doctrine cover a false comfort, and the five habits below are five comforts I have hidden behind.

## Preaching to No One

The first habit is preaching to no one. The sermon is built for a congregation that does not exist, the general Christian, the person in general, and so it lands on no actual chest in the room. Haddon Robinson warned that a sermon must aim its single idea at the real lives present, not at humanity in the abstract (Robinson 1980). The abstract sermon is safe, because no one feels named, and that safety is the leak. The woman whose son will not speak to her did not come to hear about reconciliation in general. She came in case God had a word for that, the specific grave she is carrying, and the general sermon tells her, week after gentle week, that he does not.

The second habit is explaining when you should be proclaiming. The preacher becomes a lecturer, accumulating information about the text, the Greek, the historical setting, the cross-references, until the sermon is a seminar with a prayer at each end. The people leave knowing more and feeling nothing, and a person who feels nothing will eventually decide that nothing is at stake. Fred Craddock noticed that the church had trained preachers to explain the gospel so thoroughly that they forgot to announce it (Craddock 1971). Information is not proclamation. You can teach a man the chemistry of bread and leave him starving.

## Explaining Instead of Proclaiming

The third habit is the sermon with no spine. It has parts, and the parts are each true, but they are stapled together rather than grown from one root, and the listener cannot carry any of it out because there was no single thing to carry. The mind does not retain a list. It retains a thrust. When a sermon has three good points and no governing idea, the people remember that the preacher was earnest and forget what he was earnest about, and a thing forgotten by Tuesday is a thing that will not bring them back by Sunday.

The fourth habit is the soft landing, and this one I committed most. The sermon builds, the law presses, the text has the listener cornered, and then at the last moment the preacher loses his nerve and lets everyone off easy. A comfortable closing. A reassurance that softens the very thing the text spent its force to say. Walter Brueggemann argued that the prophetic sermon must not resolve the tension it raises but leave the hearer standing in it, because a too-quick comfort robs the word of its power to change anything (Brueggemann 1989). The soft landing is the preacher protecting the room from the text and the text from the room, and the room can feel the protection, and the protection is condescension wearing the mask of grace. People do not return to a place that keeps telling them nothing is wrong.

The fifth habit is the borrowed self. The preacher steps into the pulpit and becomes someone else, a pulpit voice, a pulpit cadence, a borrowed certainty that belongs to no one in his actual life. Phillips Brooks defined the whole enterprise as truth through personality, and he meant that the truth has to pass through a real person who has been changed by it, not through a performance of a person (Brooks 1877). When the self in the pulpit is borrowed, the people sense the gap without naming it. They feel handled. They cannot say why they distrust the man, only that they do, and distrust is a slow exit. The cross was the most personal thing God ever did, a body, named, on a Friday, and we announce it through a mask.

## One Fear Beneath the Five Habits

> Five habits, one fear, and the fear is the fear of being seen failing at the most exposed task a person can attempt.

There is a sixth thing under the five, and it is worth naming because it explains why the habits cluster. A man does not usually pick up these habits one at a time, like bad swings in golf. He picks them up together, all at once, the first time the pulpit frightens him. The fear is reasonable. To preach is to stand up and claim that God is speaking, and that claim invites a judgment no other public speech invites. So the frightened man reaches, almost without deciding, for every available shelter. He generalizes so no one is named. He explains so he can hide behind data. He multiplies points so no single one can be wrong. He softens the ending so the room will not turn on him. He puts on a voice so that if they reject the sermon they are only rejecting the voice and not the man. Five habits, one fear, and the fear is the fear of being seen failing at the most exposed task a person can attempt.

Notice what all five share. Each is a way of avoiding contact. The abstract sermon avoids the particular person. The lecture avoids the demand to proclaim. The spineless sermon avoids the one hard thing. The soft landing avoids the wound. The borrowed self avoids the cost of being known. Every emptying habit is the preacher protecting himself, and the room leaves because it can smell the self-protection, even from the back row, even through the music.

## The Cure of the Shared Self

> The room does not empty around a preacher who is willing to be known.

So carry one text out of this. Paul tells the Thessalonians that he was ready to share not only the gospel but his own self, because they had become dear to him (1 Thessalonians 2:8 ESV). That is the cure for all five at once. The shared self is particular, so it cannot preach to no one. It proclaims, because it is not hiding behind data. It has a spine, because a person under conviction speaks of one thing. It does not soften the landing, because a man who loves you will tell you the truth that costs. And it is not borrowed, because it is his own self he is handing over. The room does not empty around a preacher who is willing to be known.

The habits that empty a room are all the same habit. Hiding.
