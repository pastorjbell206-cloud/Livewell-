A man I trained for the ministry called me last spring to tell me he was done. Not falling, not in scandal, not in doubt about the existence of God. Done. He said the sentence the way a man says it when he has already cleaned out the office and is only calling to be heard once before he stops being a pastor. He had given eleven years to a congregation that loved him and exhausted him in roughly equal measure, and he could not name the thing that broke. That is the part that should frighten us. He could not name it.

I have learned to distrust the easy explanations, because I have used all of them on myself. We say a man left because of conflict, because of money, because his wife was unhappy, because the elders were cruel or the building was failing or the denomination was dying. Sometimes those things are true. But they are rarely the wound. They are the place where an older wound finally tears open. The Barna Group has been tracking this for years, and in their 2021 research thirty-eight percent of Protestant pastors said they had seriously considered quitting full-time ministry within the previous year (Barna 2021). Two years earlier that number had been considerably lower. Something accelerated. The question is whether we understand what.

## Distrusting the Easy Explanations

The strongest case against my alarm comes from men I respect. They say the exodus is a correction, not a crisis. The men leaving were never built for it, or the culture has simply grown hostile to the faith and is shaking out the casual. There is truth in this, and I will not pretend otherwise. Not every man ordained should have been. Some of us mistook a gift for a call, and a faithful exit is better than a faithless tenure. Eugene Peterson said that the vocation of pastor has been replaced in many men by the profession of running a religious business, and a man can be very good at the business and never have been a pastor at all (Peterson 2011). If those men are leaving, that is not loss. That is honesty.

> The correction theory works on the periphery and fails at the center, because the men at the center are the ones bleeding out, and they are bleeding out from a wound the theory cannot see.

But that explanation does not account for the men I am actually losing. It does not account for the man who could not name what broke. He was not casual. He was not in it for the platform. He prayed, he visited the dying, he preached texts that cost him. The correction theory works on the periphery and fails at the center, because the men at the center are the ones bleeding out, and they are bleeding out from a wound the theory cannot see.

## Elijah Under the Broom Tree

Let me carry one text through this, because I do not trust my own diagnosis without it. Elijah, after the fire fell on Carmel, after the prophets of Baal were routed, after the single greatest public victory of his ministry, runs into the wilderness and sits down under a broom tree and asks God to kill him. It is enough, he says. Take away my life, for I am no better than my fathers (1 Kings 19:4 ESV). Read that slowly. This is not a man who failed. This is a man who won, and the winning emptied him, and in the emptiness the only prayer he could find was the prayer to die. The text does not treat him as a coward. God does not rebuke him. God feeds him. God lets him sleep. Then God asks him a question, and the question is the whole sermon. What are you doing here, Elijah.

Notice what God does not do. He does not give Elijah a strategy. He does not give him a church growth plan or a five-point recovery framework. He gives him bread, then sleep, then a question, then his own voice in a silence so thin you could miss it. And then, only then, He gives him a successor and a companion, Elisha, because part of what broke Elijah was the lie that he stood alone. I only am left, Elijah says, twice, and both times it is not true. Seven thousand had not bowed. The isolation was a story he told himself, and the story nearly killed him.

I know that story from the inside. There were months in my own ministry when I preached resurrection on Sunday and could not feel my own pulse by Wednesday. I came to faith out of atheism, and one of the strange griefs of conversion was discovering that the church could exhaust the very God it preached, that a man could handle the holy things every day and grow numb to them the way a surgeon grows numb to blood. I was raised without a father, and I think part of why I poured myself into the congregation without limit was that no one had ever taught me a man could be loved without earning it. So I earned. I earned until I was hollow. That is not a confession I enjoy making. It is the truest sentence I can write about why I almost left.

## The Machine We Built and Called Shepherding

The history here is not ancient, and the dates matter. The modern American pastorate was reshaped in the second half of the twentieth century by the church growth movement, by the rise of the senior pastor as chief executive, by the megachurch model that taught a generation of us to measure faithfulness in attendance and budget. Peter Drucker, the management theorist, became an unlikely patron saint of the large church in the 1980s and 1990s, advising pastors as though the congregation were a firm. Something was gained. Much was lost. We trained men to grow organizations and called it shepherding, and then we wondered why the shepherds were dying of a loneliness no organization can touch. Henri Nouwen saw it early. He left Harvard to live among the disabled at L'Arche precisely because he had become a teacher of intimacy with God who no longer had it (Nouwen 1989). He named the temptation of the minister as the temptation to be relevant, to be spectacular, to be powerful, and he said the way back was downward, toward weakness, toward the place where you are known by God and not by the crowd.

> The casual survive. They were never invested enough to be consumed.

Here is the reversal. The men who are leaving are often the men who took the job most seriously, because the job as we have built it cannot be taken seriously without it eating you. The casual survive. They were never invested enough to be consumed. It is the devoted who burn, because they fed the machine with everything they had, and the machine does not return love. So when we say the strong are surviving and the weak are leaving, we may have it exactly backward. Some of the men leaving are leaving because they were too faithful to a thing that was never worthy of that faithfulness, and they could not separate the unworthy structure from the worthy Lord.

## Where the Cross Does Its Work

That is where the cross does its work, and it does it as law before it does it as grace. The law first. If your identity is built on the congregation's approval, the congregation is your god, and that god is jealous and cruel and will demand everything and bless nothing. You have broken the first commandment in the holiest-sounding way available to a man. I have. The grace second, and only second. The cross says your worth was settled before you preached a single sermon, settled by a Father who ran toward a son who had earned nothing, settled in blood you did not spill. You cannot be exhausted out of that. The crowd can leave and the building can close and the denomination can die, and the verdict over you holds, because it was never the crowd's verdict to give.

My friend who quit, the one who could not name what broke, called me again months later. He had not gone back to the church. He had gone back to God. The two had become so fused in his mind that losing one felt like losing both, and the great mercy of his leaving was discovering they were never the same. I do not know if he will pastor again. I am not sure that is the question. The question is the one under the broom tree.

## Naming the Master

We are not losing pastors because the work is hard. We are losing them because we built a work that requires a man to die for a master that is not Christ. Tear that down, and some of them come back. Leave it standing, and we will keep burying the faithful and calling it attrition.

Name the master. Then feed the prophet bread.
