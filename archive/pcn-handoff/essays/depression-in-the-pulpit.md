I have preached a sermon on the goodness of God on a morning when I could not feel it. I want to begin there, because I am tired of articles about pastoral depression written as though the writer observed it from a clean distance. I did not observe it. I stood in a pulpit with my chest full of lead and told three hundred people about a joy I was, in that hour, unable to locate in myself. Some of you reading this know exactly the weight I mean. You have done it too. We do not talk about it, and the not talking is part of the sickness.

> There is a sentence pastors learn to say without believing it. I am fine.

There is a sentence pastors learn to say without believing it. I am fine. We say it in the narthex with a smile that took years to perfect. We say it to elders who would panic if we said anything else. We say it to our wives until our wives stop believing us and start grieving in silence beside us. The smile is a skill, and like every skill it can be practiced until it runs without the soul behind it. A man can be the most fluent liar in his own life, and the lie he tells best is the one about his own condition.

## The Theological Objection, Given Its Weight

The strongest objection to taking this seriously is theological, and it comes from sincere men, so I will give it its full weight. They say depression in a believer, and especially in a shepherd, is finally a failure of faith. The joy of the Lord is your strength. Rejoice in the Lord always. If a man truly believed the gospel he preaches, the argument goes, he would not be crushed, and to treat his despair as a medical condition is to excuse a spiritual one. I understand the instinct. There is a kind of modern therapy culture that dissolves all sin into syndrome, that lets a man off every hook by renaming every failure a wound. That culture is real and it is wrong, and I will not defend it.

But the argument fails on its own ground, because it cannot survive contact with the Bible it claims to honor. The Psalms are full of men of profound faith in profound despair. Why are you cast down, O my soul, the psalmist asks himself, and the very next breath he is still cast down (Psalm 42:5 ESV). Jeremiah curses the day he was born. Job, whom God Himself calls blameless, longs for death across thirty chapters. And there, again, is Elijah under the broom tree, a man who had just called down fire from heaven, asking God to take his life (1 Kings 19:4 ESV). If despair were always faithlessness, then the most faithful men in Scripture were the most faithless, and that is an absurdity the argument cannot escape. The God of the Bible does not treat Elijah's collapse as sin to be rebuked. He treats it as a body and a soul to be fed.

## Bread Before the Sermon

Let me carry Elijah through, because his story is the truest map I know for this. Read what God does. He does not preach to Elijah. He sends an angel who bakes bread and touches him and says, twice, arise and eat (1 Kings 19:5-7). The first prescription for the despairing prophet is food and sleep. Let that land on the men who think the cure for a depressed pastor is more Scripture memory and a sterner devotional life. Sometimes the holiest thing a crushed man can do is eat a meal and sleep through the night. The body is not a distraction from the soul. The body is where the soul lives, and a body that has not slept in three weeks will lie to a man about God, about hope, about whether the morning is worth rising for.

Then, after the bread and the sleep, God brings Elijah to the mountain and is not in the wind, not in the earthquake, not in the fire, but in a sound of thin silence (1 Kings 19:11-12). I find that almost unbearable in its tenderness. The God who answered Baal's prophets with fire answers His broken servant with quiet. He does not shout at a man who is already on the floor. We could learn to pastor each other that way. We could learn to pastor ourselves that way.

> depression is the bill that comes due for a man who has never let anyone carry him

I said I would be inside this and not above it, so here is the inside. There was a winter in my ministry when I sat in my study every Saturday night and could not finish the sermon, not because the text was hard but because I could not assemble a reason to care. I am a man who came to faith out of atheism, and the cruelty of that season was the return of the old silence, the suspicion that the universe was empty after all, arriving now in the very office where I prepared to preach it full. I was raised without a father, and I had spent my life proving I could carry things alone, and depression is the bill that comes due for a man who has never let anyone carry him. I told no one for far too long. I am a pastor. I am supposed to be the one who carries. The pride of that is its own confession.

## How the Church Manufactured the Shame

The history of how the church got here is worth naming, because shame this specific is usually manufactured. For much of the modern era the church inherited a folk theology that read all suffering as either punishment or insufficient faith, and the holiness movements and parts of the revival tradition sharpened it. Then the twentieth century added the cult of the victorious Christian life, the smiling-pastor-as-brand, the platform that punishes any visible weakness with declining attention. Charles Spurgeon, who knew this country well, preached through depression so heavy he sometimes could not leave his bed, and he told his students plainly that fits of depression come over the most of us, that the strong are not always vigorous, the wise not always ready, the brave not always courageous, and the joyous not always happy (Spurgeon 1875). The man Victorian England called the prince of preachers was describing his own broom tree. We forgot him, and built a culture that would have shamed him into silence.

The research confirms what the silence hides. Lifeway Research found that nearly a quarter of pastors have personally struggled with some kind of mental illness, and about half of those said the illness had been diagnosed (Lifeway 2014). A quarter. Look out across the men at your next denominational gathering and do the arithmetic, then remember that the number is almost certainly low, because the instrument was a survey, and pastors have a trained reflex for telling surveys that we are fine.

## The Wound That Becomes Usefulness

Here is the reversal that the smiling theology cannot hold. The pastor's despair is not always his disqualification. It is sometimes the very ground of his usefulness, because a man who has been to the floor and found God still there preaches resurrection with a weight that the untouched cannot fake. Henri Nouwen called this the wounded healer, the minister whose own wounds become a source of healing for others, not when they are hidden but when they are made available (Nouwen 1972). The point is not to perform the wound. The point is that a man who has suffered and been met by God in it can sit with a suffering parishioner and not flinch, because he has been in that room and knows the way the light eventually comes in.

## The Cross in the Silence

Now the cross, and the law before the grace. The law first. If you are hiding your despair to protect your reputation, your reputation has become your idol, and you are willing to drown privately rather than let the body of Christ see that you bleed. That is not humility. That is pride wearing humility's coat, and I have worn it. The grace second. The cross is the place where God Himself entered the dark and cried out that He was forsaken, and that cry means there is no basement of human despair where Christ has not already been. My God, my God, why have you forsaken me (Matthew 27:46 ESV). The man in the pulpit who feels forsaken is not outside the gospel. He is standing exactly where the gospel happened. The Savior went into the silence so that the silence would never again be the final word over a single one of His shepherds.

So tell someone. Not the congregation from the platform, necessarily, but someone. A brother, a physician, a counselor, a friend who will bake the bread and not preach the sermon. The God who fed Elijah is not ashamed of your weakness. The only one ashamed of it is you, and that shame is a liar.

The pulpit is not a place you have to be well to enter. It is a place a wounded man tells the truth about a God who meets the wounded. Be that man. Then go eat something and sleep.
