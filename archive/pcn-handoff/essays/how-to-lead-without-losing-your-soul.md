Jesus asked a question once that every ambitious pastor should have tattooed somewhere he reads it daily. What does it profit a man to gain the whole world and forfeit his soul (Mark 8:36 ESV). He was not speaking to stockbrokers. He was speaking to disciples, to men who wanted to be great, and the warning lands hardest on those of us whose whole world is the work of God. A pastor can forfeit his soul building a kingdom and call the forfeiture sacrifice.

I have watched it happen, and I have felt it happening in myself, which is the only reason I am allowed to write about it. There is a way of leading a church that slowly hollows out the man leading it, and the tragedy is that it often looks like success from the outside. The attendance climbs. The staff grows. The man is busy, sought after, effective. And inside, the well that fed all of it is running dry, and he is preaching from memory of a God he used to know rather than communion with the God he has.

## Steel-Manning the Cost of Ministry

Let me steel-man the resistance, because there is a real and good objection to a whole genre of writing about pastoral self-tending. The objection says that ministry is meant to cost. The shepherd lays down his life for the sheep. Paul poured himself out as a drink offering and counted it joy. There is a modern obsession with the leader's wellness that can become a thin gospel of comfort, a way of dressing up self-protection as wisdom, and a pastor who is forever guarding his peace will never go to the hospital at two in the morning, never absorb the abuse a flock sometimes inflicts on the one who loves them. Sacrifice is not the disease. Sacrifice is the calling. I believe that, and I will not retreat from it.

> There is a difference between a candle and a battery.

But the objection mistakes the thing being protected. The argument I am making is not that a pastor should spend himself less. It is that he must be filled from a source that the spending cannot empty, or the spending becomes suicide rather than sacrifice. There is a difference between a candle and a battery. A candle gives light by consuming itself, and when it is gone it is gone. The pastor is not called to be a candle. He is called to be a branch on a vine, abiding, drawing life from a root, giving fruit precisely because he himself is being fed. Cut the branch off and tell it to bear fruit through sheer sacrificial will, and you get a dead stick that produces nothing and calls its barrenness devotion.

## The Vine and the Severed Branch

So I will carry one text, the vine, through this whole piece. Abide in me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit by itself, unless it abides in the vine, neither can you, unless you abide in me (John 15:4 ESV). Read the next line, the one we skip. Apart from me you can do nothing (John 15:5). Nothing. Not less. Nothing. Jesus did not say apart from Him we can do a reduced amount of slightly weaker ministry. He said the works of a disconnected branch amount to zero, no matter how impressive the branch looks while it withers. The most frightening possibility in pastoral ministry is a man building something large and fruitless, a great visible nothing, because he severed himself from the vine in order to have more time to produce fruit.

I know the severing intimately. I came to faith out of atheism, and converts often carry a fear that the whole thing depends on their effort, because they remember a time when there was no grace holding them and they survived on will alone. I was raised without a father, so I learned early that love had to be earned and that rest was for men who could afford it. Put those two facts in a pastor and you get a machine. For years I treated abiding as a luxury I would get to once the work was under control, which is to say never, because the work is never under control. I was a branch trying to manufacture sap. I produced a great deal of activity and was, in the years I am least proud of, doing nothing.

## The Pastor as Shopkeeper

The history of how we got here has a shape worth tracing. The modern leadership-pastor is a relatively recent invention. Through the twentieth century, and especially from the 1970s forward, the American church absorbed the language and the instincts of corporate management. The pastor became the visionary, the leader, the CEO of a religious enterprise, and the seminaries followed, adding leadership tracks and strategic planning to curricula that once taught prayer and the cure of souls. Eugene Peterson watched this happen and named it as a betrayal of the office. He said pastors had become a company of shopkeepers, and the shops they kept were churches, busy with the trivia of religious commerce, asking how to keep the customers happy, how to lure customers away from competitors, how to package the goods (Peterson 1987). The work of attending to God in the depths got crowded out by the work of running the store. And a man can run a very good store and lose his soul behind the counter.

> To abide is not to visit.

I want to press on the word abide, because it is the verb the whole vine passage turns on, and it is the verb we are worst at. To abide is not to visit. A man does not abide in a place he stops by between appointments. He abides where he lives, where he returns at the end of every day, where his deepest sense of home is located. Jesus is describing a continuous dwelling, a settled communion that does not switch off when the sermon is finished and the office locks. And the test of whether a man is abiding is not how he performs on Sunday. The test is what happens in his soul on the ordinary Thursday when nothing is required of him, whether his instinct is to run back to the vine or to run to the next task. I have failed that test for whole seasons of my ministry, abiding in the work and merely visiting the Lord, and the fruit of those seasons looked alive on the branch and was already dying at the stem.

There is a cost to admitting this in front of other pastors, and the cost is the very thing that keeps us silent. We are supposed to be the men who have figured out the life with God, who teach others to abide. To confess that we ourselves have been severed branches running on stored sap feels like disqualification. It is not. Bonhoeffer, writing for a community of pastors under pressure, insisted that the man who cannot be alone with God should beware of community, and the man who cannot bear community should beware of being alone, that the two are bound together and a failure in one corrupts the other (Bonhoeffer 1954). The pastor who has no interior solitude with God brings only his anxious, performing self into the life of the church, and the church feels it, even when it cannot name it.

## The Usefulness of the Invisible

Here is the reversal, and it cuts against everything the leadership industry sells. The most useful thing a pastor does for his people is often the thing that produces nothing visible at all. The hour of prayer that fills no seats. The day of rest that the calendar resents. Edwin Friedman, a rabbi and family therapist, argued that the single most important variable in a leader's effectiveness is not technique but the leader's own differentiated, non-anxious presence, his capacity to stay connected to people while not being absorbed and driven by their anxiety (Friedman 1999). A pastor who has not tended his own interior life becomes a tuning fork for every fear in the congregation, vibrating with the room, unable to lead because he has no ground to lead from. The interior work is not retreat from leadership. It is the precondition of leadership that does not destroy the leader.

## The Insomnia That Is a Doctrine

Now the cross, law first. The law exposes the lie under the workaholism, and the lie is this. You do not actually believe the work is God's. If you believed God builds the house, you would be able to sleep while He builds it, and your inability to rest is a sermon you are preaching to yourself every day about who you really think is in charge. Unless the Lord builds the house, those who build it labor in vain (Psalm 127:1 ESV). The verse goes on to say He gives to his beloved sleep. Your insomnia is a doctrine. I have preached that false doctrine to myself for years, and it is a denial of the gospel dressed as diligence.

The grace second. The cross means the decisive work is already finished, accomplished by Another, signed in blood you did not spill. It is finished, He said, and He meant it, and your frantic addition to a completed work is not piety, it is unbelief (John 19:30 ESV). The gospel frees a man to labor hard precisely because his identity does not ride on the labor. He can pour himself out at the hospital bed at two in the morning and then sleep without guilt, because the sleep is not laziness, it is faith that the vine keeps feeding the branch while the branch rests.

The soul is not a resource you spend on the church. It is the place where you meet the God who sends you to the church, and if you spend it, you have nothing left to send.

Guard the root. Let the fruit take care of itself.
