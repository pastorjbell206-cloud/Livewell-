A woman sits in the fourth pew on a Sunday in February, and she has heard this passage before. She has heard it from you. She has heard it from the man who stood there before you. She heard it at the camp she went to in 1994 and at the funeral of her mother and on a podcast last Tuesday while she folded laundry. You announce the text and her face does a small, courteous thing. It closes. Not in anger. In recognition. She knows where this is going, and she is already three steps ahead of you, waiting at the application like a commuter who has memorized the route.

This is the real difficulty of preaching in a country soaked in two centuries of sermons. Not unbelief. Familiarity. The skeptic at least arrives curious about whether the thing is true. The veteran believer arrives certain that he already knows what you are about to say, and his certainty is the wall. I have stood in front of that wall most Sundays of my life. I built part of it myself, every week I told people what they expected to hear in the order they expected to hear it.

## The Wall of Familiarity

The strongest case against worrying about this comes from the deepest part of the tradition, and it deserves a fair hearing. The Reformed instinct says the preacher is not in the entertainment business. The Word does its own work. Faith comes by hearing, and the hearing is of the Word of Christ (Romans 10:17 ESV). If the gospel feels familiar, that is no defect of the gospel. It is the patient repetition by which the Spirit wears a groove into the soul. The manna fell every morning and it was always manna. To chase novelty is to insult the bread. There is real truth here, and I will not pretend otherwise. The preacher who only wants to surprise people has already left the pulpit for the stage. Repetition is not the enemy. Familiarity is not the enemy.

But answer the strongest version of the objection honestly, and it splits. The manna was the same substance, yes. It was never the same morning. The problem in the fourth pew is not that the woman has heard the truth too often. The problem is that she has heard it explained too often and encountered it too rarely. We have handed people summaries of the text instead of the text. We have told them what the passage means so efficiently that they have never once been made to stand inside it and feel the floor give way.

## The Road to Emmaus and the Long Way

> The familiar listener is not the obstacle to good preaching.

Carry one text. Luke gives us a road. Two men walking away from Jerusalem on the afternoon of the resurrection, talking about the very events that had just shattered them, and the risen Christ falls into step beside them, and they do not know him (Luke 24:13-16 ESV). These are not skeptics. They are insiders. They had hoped he was the one to redeem Israel. They knew the prophecies. They had heard it all. And the Lord himself, walking beside them, does not begin by announcing who he is. He asks them a question. What are you discussing as you walk along. He makes them say the familiar thing out loud until they hear how it sounds in their own grief, and only then, beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, does he open the Scriptures they had known since childhood. Their hearts burned. The text had not changed. The road had been walked before. Something else happened.

What happened was that the familiar material was taken back from them and given again as if for the first time. This is the preacher's task with the over-catechized. Not new information. Re-encounter. Fred Craddock saw this clearly when he argued that the old deductive sermon, which announces its conclusion and then defends it, hands the hearer a verdict before the hearer has lived the case (Craddock 1971). The woman in the fourth pew has the verdict already. She does not need it restated. She needs to be walked back through the evidence until the verdict becomes hers again, earned in the body, not received in the ear.

Consider how the Lord does it on that road, because the method is the message. He withholds. He could have ended the conversation in one sentence at the first mile, named himself, settled the grief, and the two men would have gone home with a fact and a fading wonder. Instead he chooses the long way. He lets them grieve out loud. He lets them recite the very hope they had buried, that they had hoped he was the one to redeem Israel, and he lets the past tense of that sentence hang in the air. He even reproaches them, calling them slow of heart to believe all that the prophets had spoken (Luke 24:25 ESV), which is a harder word than we would dare give the wounded. And only after the reproach, after the recitation, after the slow reopening of texts they had memorized as children, does he break the bread and let their eyes come open. He preached the long sermon when the short answer was available, because the short answer would have been survivable and the long one was not.

## How Efficiency Hardened the Sermon

Here the history of preaching has a warning for us, and it is specific. By the late nineteenth century, much of American Protestant preaching had become what Phillips Brooks, lecturing at Yale in 1877, feared most. He defined preaching as truth through personality, and he meant that the truth had to pass through a living person who had been altered by it (Brooks 1877). A generation later the alteration was optional. The sermon became a delivery system for points. Three of them, alliterated, with an illustration apiece. The form was so predictable that the congregation could fill in the blanks. We taught people to anticipate us, and then we blamed them for being bored.

The machinery that made the sermon predictable was not laziness. It was efficiency, and efficiency is the enemy here. A revival culture that needed to move crowds quickly learned to compress the gospel into a portable formula, a few verses, a decision, a closing hymn, and the formula worked so well at scale that it hardened into the only shape a sermon was allowed to take. By the time I came up in the church, the shape was invisible to the people inside it, the way water is invisible to a fish. We did not choose to make the gospel familiar. We inherited a machine for making it familiar, and we kept feeding it, Sunday after Sunday, because it ran and because Saturday was short and because no one in the building could remember the bread tasting like anything else.

So the reversal is sharper than it first appears. The familiar listener is not the obstacle to good preaching. The familiar listener is the judgment on bad preaching. When people have heard it all, they are telling you that you have only ever given them the all they could hear coming. They are a mirror. I have looked into that mirror and not liked the preacher staring back, the man who reached for the predictable phrase because it was Saturday night and the predictable phrase was paid for already.

## Recovering the Buried Scandal

How then do you preach to such people. You go underneath the summary to the strangeness that the summary was built to manage. Every passage they have heard a hundred times was once scandalous, and the scandal is still in there, buried under our domestications. The prodigal's father did not merely forgive. He ran, and in that culture a grown man did not run, because running meant lifting the robe and showing the legs, and the father took the shame of the boy onto his own body before the boy could say a word (Luke 15:20 ESV). Tell it that way and the woman who has heard the prodigal forty times leans forward, because she has never been allowed to see the father's legs. The information was always there. We sanded it smooth.

> A reheated sermon has a particular smell, and the room learns it, and the room stops coming hungry because it has learned what is on the stove.

It costs more to preach this way, and the cost is the reason most of us do not. To recover the strangeness of a text you have to sit in it long enough to be surprised yourself, and being surprised by a passage you have preached four times requires admitting you did not exhaust it the first three. That admission is a small death. It is far easier to retrieve last year's outline, which already knew where it was going, than to stand again in front of the burning bush and take the sandals off a second time. I have chosen the outline. I have chosen it on a Friday when the week had been cruel and the text felt like a stranger and the old notes were warm and waiting. The people could tell. They always can. A reheated sermon has a particular smell, and the room learns it, and the room stops coming hungry because it has learned what is on the stove.

## Particular Grace, Not Grace in the Abstract

This is why the cross has to do its work inside the room and not above it. The man who has heard the gospel his whole life is not in danger of forgetting that Christ died. He is in danger of remembering it the way he remembers his own phone number, a true fact that costs nothing to recall. The law has to come first and find him where he actually lives, in the specific contempt he feels for his brother, in the money he will not let go, in the apology he has rehearsed and never given. Grace that answers no particular guilt is grace in the abstract, and the over-familiar have made their peace with grace in the abstract. They have not made their peace with grace for that, named, on a Tuesday. Haddon Robinson insisted that the sermon's idea must press on the actual lives in front of you, not on people in general (Robinson 1980). General grace is the most familiar thing in the room. Particular grace is news.

> General grace is the most familiar thing in the room.

So do not aim to tell them something they have never heard. Aim to make them feel what they have always known. The woman in the fourth pew is not waiting for a fact. She is waiting to be found. The Word is sharper than any two-edged sword, and it has not gone dull. We have.

Preach the strangeness back into the familiar.
