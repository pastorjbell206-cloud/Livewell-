Every September the same announcement goes up on the screen and into the bulletin and across the platform. We need volunteers. The nursery is short, the parking team is thin, the connections desk has two people doing the work of five, and so the ask goes out, and a few tired saints raise their hands again because they always do. The church fills its slots. The machine runs another year. And if you stood at the back of that room and asked who among these faithful people is being formed into a leader who could one day carry weight, the honest answer would be almost none of them, because nobody is trying to.

## Recruiting Versus Developing

There is a difference between recruiting and developing, and most churches have collapsed it without noticing. Recruiting fills a need that the church has. Developing meets a need that the person has, the need to grow into the fullness of what God made them to carry. Recruiting asks what the volunteer can do for the ministry. Developing asks what the ministry can do for the volunteer, what God might be building in a human being through the assignment. We say we want to develop leaders. What we usually want is to fill the schedule, and the schedule, once filled, has no reason to develop anyone.

> Recruiting fills a need that the church has.

Let me make the recruiter's case honestly, because the work is real and the recruiter is not lazy. The services do have to happen. The children do have to be safe. Someone does have to count the offering and run the slides and stack the chairs, and a church that romanticizes development while the practical work falls apart is not deep, it is just disorganized. The recruiter is keeping the lights on. He looks at the gap between the work and the workers and he closes it the only way he knows how, week after week, and the church owes him more thanks than it gives. His instinct to staff the need is not wrong.

## The Quiet Exchange That Hollows a Church

But he has accepted a quiet exchange that is hollowing his church out from the inside. By treating people as labor to be deployed, he gets the slot filled this week and loses the leader who would have filled a hundred slots over twenty years. He is harvesting and never planting. And there is a deeper cost, because people can tell when they are being used rather than formed. The volunteer who senses that the church only wants his hands and not his growth eventually burns out, not from the labor but from the meaninglessness of labor that leads nowhere. We wonder why we cannot keep volunteers. It is because we never made them into anything. We just spent them.

## With Him Before Sent Out

The text I want to carry is the way Jesus made leaders, because it is almost the opposite of how we do it. 'And he appointed twelve, so that they might be with him and he might send them out to preach' (Mark 3:14 ESV). Read the order. With him first. Sent out second. The being with came before the doing for, and the doing flowed out of the being with. Jesus did not recruit twelve volunteers to staff a movement. He drew twelve men into his life, let them watch him pray and weep and argue and rest, and only out of that shared life did he send them to do anything. The formation was relational before it was functional. We have inverted the order, and the inversion is the whole problem.

> The kingdom does not scale the way a corporation scales.

The history of how we lost this is the history of the church learning to think like a business, and the year markers are not subtle. When the church growth movement took hold across the nineteen seventies and eighties, and Peter Drucker, the most influential management thinker of the century, began consulting with megachurch pastors and teaching them to run congregations as organizations to be scaled, we gained efficiency and lost apprenticeship. The org chart replaced the rabbi. We built systems to process volunteers at scale, intake forms and ministry placement matrices and the language of human capital, and a system that processes people efficiently is structurally incapable of doing the slow, inefficient, deeply personal work of forming one leader at a time. The factory cannot raise a child.

## The Cost of Proximity

Here is the reversal that costs the most to accept. Developing leaders will always look less efficient than recruiting volunteers, and it will always produce more in the end. Pouring eighteen months into one person who might lead a ministry someday is a worse use of a Tuesday than filling six open slots, by every metric your dashboard tracks. But the six slots are filled by people who will leave, and the one person becomes someone who fills slots you will never see, who develops others, who is still leading when you are gone. The kingdom does not scale the way a corporation scales. It scales the way a vineyard scales, slowly, through roots, one planting at a time, and you will not see the harvest in the quarter you planted it.

What does developing actually require of us, and why do we avoid it. It requires proximity, the willingness to let people close enough to watch you lead and to be coached inside the real work rather than in a classroom. Ruth Haley Barton argues that leaders can only give away what they themselves have received, that an unformed leader produces unformed followers, that the inner life of the one doing the developing is the ceiling on everyone he develops (Barton 2008). This is why we avoid it. Developing a leader means being watched up close, and being watched up close means being known, and being known means our own gaps come into view. Recruiting keeps people at arm's length. Developing brings them inside, where they can see us, and that is precisely the cost we do not want to pay.

## Jethro's Cure for the Overfunctioning Leader

It also requires that we hand over real authority before the person is fully ready, which terrifies the recruiter, because real authority handed over means real mistakes made in public. But there is no other way it has ever worked. Jesus sent the twelve out while they still misunderstood almost everything, and they came back having done the work badly and well, and the sending was the school. We hoard the meaningful decisions and then wonder why nobody around us has grown. You cannot develop a leader by letting him stack chairs. You develop him by letting him lead, and then standing close enough to debrief the wreckage and the wins, week after week, until the day he is leading better than you.

The deepest part is the part we are most tempted to skip, the formation of the person beneath the function. Bonhoeffer reminded us that Christian community is not an ideal we impose but a reality we receive, that the goal is never to build the impressive thing but to serve the actual person God has placed in front of us (Bonhoeffer 1954). Developing a leader is finally an act of love for that specific human being, not a strategy for staffing the church. The moment it becomes a strategy, the person feels it, and the formation dies. We must want the volunteer to grow even if it means he grows right out of our church and into a calling we never get the credit for.

## The Cross as Law Before Grace

The oldest picture of this is not Jesus and the twelve. It is Moses in the wilderness and the day his father-in-law watched him work. Jethro found Moses sitting alone from morning to evening while the people stood around him waiting, judging every dispute himself, and he said the thing every overfunctioning leader needs to hear, what you are doing is not good, you and the people will wear yourselves out, for the thing is too heavy for you and you are not able to do it alone (Exodus 18:17-18 ESV). Then he told Moses to develop leaders, to look for able men out of the people and place them over thousands and hundreds and fifties and tens, to teach them the statutes and let them carry the lighter cases so that they would bear the burden with him (Exodus 18:21-22 ESV). Read what the cure for Moses was. Not a better schedule. Not a more efficient docket. Other leaders, formed and trusted and handed real weight, because the work was never meant to live or die on one man. You will not develop leaders from behind a desk handing out assignments. You develop them the way Jethro told Moses to, by raising up able people, teaching them, and giving them the cases that are too small for your ego and exactly the right size for their growth.

There is a final cost most of us never count, and it is the cost to our own ego. The leader who develops other leaders is signing up to be eclipsed. The people you raise well will, if you have done it faithfully, eventually preach better than you preached at their age, lead larger than you led, see things you cannot see. And a great deal of what passes for a shortage of leaders in our churches is actually a senior leader's quiet unwillingness to be surpassed, a hoarding of the spotlight dressed up as a concern for readiness. We keep people in the volunteer pool because a developed leader is a threat to the man at the top in a way a chair-stacker never will be. Nouwen warned that the descending way of Jesus runs directly against the climbing instinct that lives in every leader, that we are forever tempted to grasp at relevance and visibility instead of giving them away (Nouwen 1989). You cannot develop a leader and protect your own prominence at the same time. You have to choose, and most of us choose ourselves and call it prudence.

> Stop filling slots. Start raising leaders.

This is where the cross has to do its work, and it does it as law before it does it as grace. The law first. The reason you will not raise leaders who might surpass you is that you need to be the indispensable one, the source the church cannot do without, and that need is not humility wearing thin, it is idolatry, the determination to be a small savior to people who already have a real one. You have made your own irreplaceability into a god, and like every idol it demands that everyone around you stay smaller than you so that you can keep feeling large. That is sin, and naming it as sin is the only thing that loosens its grip. The grace second. The cross says the one indispensable work was already finished by the only indispensable Man, accomplished in blood you did not spill while you contributed nothing but the sin he carried, and your standing was settled there before you developed a single leader. You do not have to be the source. You were never the source. The man who has heard that law and received that grace can pour himself into people who will eclipse him and rejoice when they do, because his worth was never riding on being the largest one in the room. He is free to give his power away, because the verdict over him was never his to earn and is no longer his to lose.

A church that only recruits will always be short of volunteers. A church that develops will one day have more leaders than it knows what to do with. Almost all of us are running the first kind and praying for the results of the second.

Stop filling slots. Start raising leaders.
