A man stands up with notes and speaks for thirty minutes about God, and at the end you cannot tell whether what just happened was a sermon or a talk. The room cannot tell either. They clap or they do not, they take notes or they do not, and they go home essentially unchanged, having heard something true and useful and survivable. That last word is the whole problem. A talk is survivable. You can hear a good talk and remain exactly who you were. The question of what makes a good sermon is, at bottom, the question of why a sermon is not supposed to be survivable.

The two can look identical from the back row. Both can have a clear point and a memorable line and an honest delivery. Both can quote Scripture. Both can be moving. The difference is not visible in the technique, which is why so much preaching instruction misses it, drilling the mechanics of a thing whose essence is not mechanical. You can teach a man everything about how to give a talk and have taught him nothing about how to preach, because you have trained the hands and never touched the question of who is speaking.

## The Case That There Is No Difference

Let me build the case that there is no real difference, because intelligent people make it and it deserves answering. A sermon, the argument runs, is just a religious talk. It uses a Bible instead of a business case, it ends in prayer instead of applause, but the underlying act is the same, a prepared person communicating ideas to a listening group. The talk that helps a man forgive his father has done more good than a sermon that bored him. Communication is communication, and the spiritual gloss we put on preaching is mostly the profession protecting its mystique. There is a real point buried here, and I will grant it before I answer it. A sermon that is badly communicated is not made holy by being a sermon. Clarity is not optional. Mumbling is not anointing. Many sermons would be improved by learning what a good talk knows.

> A talk is something a man decides to give. A sermon is something a man is sent to deliver.

But take the argument at its strongest, that communication is communication, and it collapses on one verse. Paul reminds the Romans that faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ (Romans 10:17 ESV), and then he asks how they will hear without someone preaching, and how anyone will preach unless he is sent (Romans 10:14-15 ESV). That word sent is the whole difference. A talk is something a man decides to give. A sermon is something a man is sent to deliver. The speaker of a talk is the source. The preacher of a sermon is a courier, and a courier who edits the message is a thief. The act is not the same, because the authority behind the act is not the same, and the listener, even one who could not explain it, feels the difference between a man offering his opinion and a man under orders.

## Augustine and the Sent Man

Carry that sent-ness as the spine, because it changes everything downstream. Augustine, who had been a professional teacher of rhetoric before his conversion, wrestled with exactly this in the fourth book of his treatise on Christian teaching, written near the year 426. He knew every device of the talk, every trick of persuasion the empire could teach. And he concluded that the Christian speaker must subordinate all of it to a higher aim, that he speaks not to be admired but so that the truth may be loved and obeyed, and that his own life must preach alongside his tongue or the tongue is a clanging thing (Augustine 426). The rhetorician gives a talk to win the room. The preacher, Augustine saw, is trying to lose the room to God. Those are not two grades of the same act. They are opposite acts that happen to use the same lungs.

> The talker owns his subject and bends it to his gift. The preacher is owned by his subject and bends his gift to it.

What makes Augustine's argument matter is that he was not a man suspicious of skill. He had eaten his bread by skill. He had taught young men in Milan how to move a crowd toward whatever the crowd needed to be moved toward, and he never once pretended that eloquence was worthless. He kept it. He told preachers to use the whole armory of the rhetoricians, the clear style for teaching, the moderate style for delight, the grand style for bending the will. But he reversed the master. In the schools, the cause served the eloquence, because a paid speaker would defend any cause that paid. In the church, the eloquence serves the cause, and the cause is fixed, and the speaker did not choose it and may not amend it. That single reversal is the whole boundary line between a talk and a sermon. The talker owns his subject and bends it to his gift. The preacher is owned by his subject and bends his gift to it. A man can have the identical skill in both rooms and be doing two different things, because in one room he is the lord of the message and in the other he is its servant.

## Why the Cross Belongs in a Sermon

This is why the cross has to be in a sermon in a way it never has to be in a talk. A talk about generosity can stand on its own legs, can move a man to write a check by the sheer force of the case. A sermon about generosity that does not run, sooner or later, into the one who though he was rich became poor so that we through his poverty might become rich (2 Corinthians 8:9 ESV), is a talk wearing a stole. The cross is the watershed. A talk persuades you to do better. A sermon announces that you cannot do better, kills the man who thought he could, and raises a new one who gives because he has been given to. The first leaves you with a task. The second leaves you with a grave and a resurrection. Tim Keller pressed this point hard, that preaching to the heart must always show how the text drives to Christ and his work, or it becomes mere moral advice with a religious accent (Keller 2015). Moral advice is a fine talk. It is a failed sermon.

And here is the reversal, the thing that humbles the preacher who thinks he can manufacture the difference. The man cannot make his talk into a sermon by his own effort. He cannot add enough Scripture or enough passion or enough cross to cross the line by technique, because the line is not technical. A sermon happens when God speaks through the speaking, and that is not in the preacher's power to guarantee. He can only prepare the kindling. The fire is given. This should terrify us out of our professionalism and free us from it at the same time, because it means the worst-delivered sermon, given by a sent man who has been broken by his text, may do what the most polished talk can never do, while the preacher who trusts his polish may give a flawless talk every Sunday for thirty years and never once preach.

## Standing Over the Text or Under It

> A talk is what comes out of a man who has mastered his material. A sermon is what comes out of a man whose material has mastered him, and the difference is felt in the chest before it is understood in the mind.

I know which of those preachers I have most often been, and the confession is not modesty. There were years I gave excellent talks. They were clear, they were warm, they had a line people quoted in the lobby, and they cost me almost nothing, because I had not let the text get its hands on me before I stood up. I had studied the passage the way a lawyer studies a brief, mastering it, standing over it, never once standing under it. A talk is what comes out of a man who has mastered his material. A sermon is what comes out of a man whose material has mastered him, and the difference is felt in the chest before it is understood in the mind. My polished years were my emptiest years. The people were entertained and unchanged, and so was I, and I mistook the lobby compliments for fruit until a season came that broke me open and I learned, late, what it costs to actually be sent.

So what makes a good sermon. Not the skill, though skill serves it. Not the subject, though it must be the right subject. A good sermon is one through which a man who was sent says the thing he was sent to say, having been changed by it first, so that the cross does its killing and its raising work inside the room, and the people leave unable to remain who they were. Phillips Brooks called it truth through personality, and the personality matters, but the truth is the master and the man is the window (Brooks 1877). A talk shows you the man. A sermon shows you through him to the thing that has hold of him.

## The Test of the Morning After

The test is the morning after. A talk you admired. A sermon admired you back, found you, named you, and would not let you stay. If you remember the speaker, it may have been a fine talk. If you cannot stop thinking about your own life, your father, your money, your unforgiveness, the grave you have been avoiding, it was probably a sermon, and the speaker has already done the one thing a preacher is for. He got out of the way.

A talk you survive. A sermon survives you.
