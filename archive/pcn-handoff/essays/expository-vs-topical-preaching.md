Two preachers prepare for the same Sunday. The first opens his Bible to where he left off last week, the next paragraph of Romans, and his sermon is decided for him before he has had a single idea, because the text decided it. The second begins with a question that has been pressing on his people, anxiety, money, a marriage falling apart, and he goes looking for the passages that speak to it. By Saturday night both will have a sermon. The question the conferences keep arguing about is which of them did it right. The question underneath, the one that actually matters, is which of them let the Word be in charge.

That is the real fault line, and it does not run cleanly between the two methods. There are expositors who march verse by verse through a book and never once let the text correct them, because they have already decided what each verse will say. And there are topical preachers who chase a theme and end up more captive to Scripture than the expositor, because the theme drove them back into six passages they could not control. Method is not the same as submission. We have spent a generation treating it as though it were.

## The Strength of the Consecutive Text

> Method is not the same as submission.

Let me give the case for expository preaching its full strength, because it is strong and I believe most of it. When a preacher commits to working through a book of the Bible, paragraph by paragraph, he surrenders his right to avoid. He cannot skip the verse about money this autumn because the building campaign is on. He cannot dodge the hard saying about divorce because three couples in the room are in the middle of one. The discipline of the next verse drags him into territory he would never have chosen, and that is precisely its gift. Haddon Robinson built his whole case on this, that the expositor lets the biblical idea, not the preacher's idea, govern the sermon, so that the authority in the room is the text and not the man (Robinson 1980). John Stott went further and said that all true Christian preaching is expository in spirit, because all of it exists to open up the meaning of a text and let it speak (Stott 1982). I will not argue against that. The discipline of the consecutive text has saved more sermons from the preacher's vanity than any other practice I know.

But the strongest form of the expository case has a blind spot, and naming it is not an attack on the method. It is this. The Bible itself does not always preach expositorily. When Peter stands up on the day of Pentecost, he does not open a scroll and work through it verse by verse. He has a topic, and the topic is the thing that just happened in the street, and he reaches for Joel and for two Psalms and for the promise to David, pulling texts from across the canon to answer one question the crowd is screaming (Acts 2:14-36 ESV). That is a topical sermon. It is in the Bible. It converted three thousand people. Whatever rule we are about to make against topical preaching, the apostle broke it on the first day of the church, and the Spirit blessed the breaking.

## Peter at Pentecost

Carry that Pentecost sermon as the spine, because it teaches the actual rule. Peter's sermon is topical in arrangement and ruthlessly expository in submission. He does not bend the texts to his theme. He lets the texts dictate what the theme is allowed to mean. He quotes Joel and then insists that the strange thing in the street is the very pouring out of the Spirit that Joel saw in the last days (Acts 2:17 ESV). He quotes the Psalm and then refuses to let David claim it for himself, because David died and was buried and his tomb is with us to this day, so the incorruption the Psalm promised must belong to another (Acts 2:29-31 ESV). The topic gave Peter his starting point. The text gave Peter his authority. He did not choose between the two. He ordered them rightly.

This is the distinction the whole argument keeps missing. The danger of topical preaching is real, but it is not the topic. The danger is the proof-text, the verse yanked out of its paragraph to nod along with a point the preacher already owned. The danger of expository preaching is also real, and it is not the consecutive text. The danger is the running commentary, the verse-by-verse march that explains everything and lands nowhere, that mistakes coverage for proclamation. I have committed both sins. I have used a Psalm to prove a thing the Psalm was not saying. I have also walked a congregation through fourteen verses of Leviticus and left them more informed and less pierced than when they came in, and called it faithfulness because it was thorough.

## The Proof-Text and the Running Commentary

> The danger of topical preaching is real, but it is not the topic.

Watch how Peter's submission shows itself, because it is the part we cannot fake. When the crowd, cut to the heart, cries out asking what they should do, Peter does not soften the topic to keep them. He has just told them they crucified the Lord of glory, and now that the wound is open he pours grace into it, repent and be baptized, every one of you (Acts 2:38 ESV). The law came first and it came hard, the plain charge that this Jesus, whom you crucified, God has made both Lord and Christ (Acts 2:36 ESV). The topic did not let Peter dodge that sentence. The topic was the resurrection of a man the crowd had killed, and you cannot preach that topic faithfully without the indictment that makes the grace necessary. So the test of a topical sermon is not whether it began with a theme. The test is whether the theme was allowed to wound before it healed, or whether the preacher reached for a comfortable topic precisely so he would never have to.

The history clarifies why we are so anxious about this. The verse-by-verse expository sermon, the kind associated with the consecutive exposition of a whole book, has deep roots, but its modern American dominance owes much to the twentieth-century reaction against the topical sermon's collapse. By the mid-century the liberal pulpit had so often used a verse as a springboard into the preacher's own opinions that conservatives recoiled toward the text and would not let go. The recoil was justified. The springboard sermon deserved to die. But a recoil is not a doctrine. We turned a healthy correction into an absolute, and we began to speak as though Spurgeon, who almost never preached consecutively through a book and took a single text wherever it served the wounded soul in front of him, had somehow been doing it wrong (Spurgeon 1875). Spurgeon was not doing it wrong.

## When to Preach Each

So when each. Preach the consecutive book when your people need to be confronted with the whole counsel of God and cannot be allowed to live on the preacher's favorite forty verses. Preach the consecutive book when you yourself are the danger, when your instinct to avoid the hard text needs a stronger master than your instinct. That is most seasons. But preach topically when a death has torn through the congregation and the next paragraph of your series has nothing to say to it, because to ignore the open grave in the room in order to protect your schedule is to love the method more than the people. Preach topically when the culture has put a question to the church that the church is fumbling, and the answer is scattered across the canon and has to be gathered. Phillips Brooks said the truth must come through a personality that has been changed by it (Brooks 1877). A method cannot be changed by anything. Only a man can.

> The question is never which method is faithful.

There is also an honesty the method debate lets us avoid, and it is about why we choose what we choose. I have hidden inside the expository series for reasons that had nothing to do with the whole counsel of God. A long series is a long permission to coast. The decisions are made for the year, the trajectory is set, and a tired man can lean on the structure the way a tired man leans on a wall. I have likewise reached for a topical sermon not because a grave had opened but because the next paragraph of Romans frightened me and a softer subject was available. The method was a costume in both cases. Underneath the costume was the same man making the same choice, which was the choice to spare himself. No method will save you from that choice. The consecutive text can become an alibi as easily as the topic can become an escape. The question is never which method is faithful. The question is which one, this Saturday, you are using to hide.

## The Method as Costume

And in both, the cross has to do its work, and the law has to come before the grace. The expositor can hide from this behind his careful verbs and his Greek, ending the sermon in the lexicon instead of at the foot of the cross. The topical preacher can hide from it behind relevance, scratching the felt need so gently that no one is ever cut. Both evasions leave the room comfortable. Neither method protects you from the one failure that matters, which is preaching about a text instead of letting the text preach to you first and then through you to them.

Do not ask which method is holy. Ask which one, this week, makes you submit.
