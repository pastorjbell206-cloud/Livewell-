A pastor asked me recently whether it was wrong to use artificial intelligence to write his sermons. I did not answer immediately. Not because the question was complicated technically, but because I was trying to locate what it was actually asking underneath the surface, and what I found there was older than any algorithm.

The question was really this: what does it mean to be called to preach?

---

## The Double Claim Beneath the Ministry of the Word

The ministry of the word has always rested on a double claim. First, that the Scripture is living and active, able to divide and discern in ways that no technique can replicate: "For the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing to the division of soul and of spirit, of joints and of marrow, and discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart" (Hebrews 4:12 ESV). Second, that the preacher who stands before a congregation to open that Scripture has been formed (by study, by suffering, by encounter with the text over years) in ways that make his voice particular and irreplaceable. The congregation does not gather on Sunday to receive a competent communication of correct theological content. They gather to hear a human being, wounded and restored like them, who has wrestled with this text in the dark and come back to tell them what he found.

This is not a romantic notion. It is an ecclesiological claim rooted in the specific way the New Testament describes the task of proclamation. When Paul writes, "But we have this treasure in jars of clay, to show that the surpassing power belongs to God and not to us" (2 Corinthians 4:7 ESV), he is making a deliberate point about the medium through which the gospel travels: the weakness of the vessel is not a problem to be engineered away but the very condition under which the power of God becomes visible. The same apostle tells the Thessalonians what his preaching had actually consisted of: "So, being affectionately desirous of you, we were ready to share with you not only the gospel of God but also our own selves, because you had become very dear to us" (1 Thessalonians 2:8 ESV). The gospel and the self, delivered together, because in preaching they cannot be delivered separately. Phillips Brooks compressed the whole theology into three words at Yale in 1877: preaching is truth through personality. Not truth beside personality, not truth despite it. Through it. Remove either term and whatever remains is no longer preaching. The preacher who has been through the valley of the shadow of death and who speaks about Psalm 23 from inside that experience communicates something that no algorithmically optimized content can carry. The weight is in the clay, not the gold.

Artificial intelligence can produce content that is theologically accurate. It can organize an argument, identify relevant texts, and construct prose that reads smoothly. A capable model can produce, in minutes, a sermon outline that covers the exegetical bases, names the right commentators, and moves through application with the formal logic that homiletics textbooks describe. It can write better, in the narrow technical sense of grammatical correctness and logical coherence, than many preachers who have been in the pulpit for thirty years.

> The machine has no Wednesday afternoon.

What it cannot do is spend Wednesday afternoon with a dying deacon and then bring the weight of that room into Sunday's exposition of John 11. What it cannot do is carry the specific grief and specific hope of a specific community into the particular encounter with Scripture that preaching, at its best, is. The machine has no Wednesday afternoon. It has no dying deacon. It has never stood at a graveside in February in Michigan and tried to say something true.

## The Difference Between a Tool and a Substitute

I am not arguing that pastors should not use these tools at all. I have used them. I have asked AI tools to summarize a commentary, to suggest parallel passages, to help me organize a complicated theological argument, to identify where a line of reasoning might be challenged. These are research tasks. They are not preaching. The distinction is analogous to the difference between using a concordance and having the concordance write your sermon. The concordance is a tool for finding what exists. The sermon is what you do with what you found, after you have lived with it.

The distinction matters because what erodes in AI-assisted preaching is not immediately obvious. The pastor who begins outsourcing the generative labor of sermon preparation (the sustained wrestle with the text, the search for the right word, the willingness to sit with what the passage demands before rushing to what it offers) does not suddenly become a fraud. He becomes, over time, something subtler and more dangerous: a communicator who no longer has anything of his own to say. The congregation eventually feels this, even if they cannot name it. They receive information. They do not receive witness.

The temptation is strongest in the week the schedule has already failed, and that is itself diagnostic. The week the sermon has to be outsourced because there was no time to prepare it is a week in which the pastor was not able to do the central work of his calling. The problem is not solved by outsourcing. The problem is a schedule that has crowded out the most important thing with things that seemed more urgent.

## The Oldest New Question

Every technology of the word has been met at the door with this same anxiety, and the anxiety has never been simply wrong. In Plato's Phaedrus, Socrates tells the story of the god Theuth presenting the invention of writing to King Thamus and promising it will make the Egyptians wiser. Thamus refuses the gift's own advertisement. Writing, he says, will produce forgetfulness, because men will trust the marks instead of their memories, and it will give them the appearance of wisdom without the reality of it. That was roughly twenty-four centuries ago, and the king was right about the cost even though the world rightly took the deal. Writing did relocate memory. Print did the same thing at scale after the 1450s, and it flooded pulpits with borrowed words almost immediately.

The church has been here too, and its best men handled the question with more honesty than we tend to. Augustine, in On Christian Teaching, allowed the preacher who could not compose to memorize and deliver what a wiser man had written, and counseled him to pray before he spoke. Luther went further and industrialized the help: in the 1520s he published the postils, model sermons keyed to the church year, precisely so that a parish priest who could not build a sermon would at least read a faithful one aloud to his people. Notice the honesty of that arrangement. The postil was openly another man's work. It was bread carried into a starving pulpit, and nobody pretended the man reading it had wrestled it out of the text himself. The congregation knew what it was receiving, and what it was receiving was true.

That is the line the new tools invite us to cross without noticing. The trouble with the machine-drafted sermon is not that the help is borrowed. Preachers have always borrowed, and the honest ones have said so. The trouble is the pretense: borrowed wrestling presented as the pastor's own, a witness costume worn over a summary, the appearance of wisdom without the night at the desk that wisdom costs. Thamus named the danger before the church existed. The danger was never the tool. It was the appearance.

## The Scribe and the Prophet

This is not new technology producing a new problem. The problem is as old as the difference between a scribe and a prophet. The scribe copies what has been written. The prophet has stood in the council of God and speaks from that encounter. Jeremiah did not dictate from a manual. When he resolved to quit the calling, the calling refused him: "If I say, 'I will not mention him, or speak any more in his name,' there is in my heart as it were a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I am weary with holding it in, and I cannot" (Jeremiah 20:9 ESV). The speaking cost him everything, and the not-speaking cost him more. That fire is not generated. It is given, given through the kind of slow, costly, personal formation that cannot be automated.

Bonhoeffer, in the homiletics lectures he gave his seminarians at the illegal Confessing Church seminary in Finkenwalde in the mid-1930s, with the Gestapo two years from closing the doors, insisted that the preacher's primary qualification was not rhetorical skill or theological knowledge but the willingness to speak as one who had heard. The sermon that the congregation needed was not a tour through biblical scholarship but a word: a specific word, for this community, at this moment, addressed by a person who had stood before God on behalf of the people and returned with something to say. That account of preaching is not compatible with a process in which the first draft comes from a language model.

## When the Better Sermon Is the Lesser One

There is a counter-argument here, and it is made by serious pastors, not lazy ones: the bi-vocational man with very limited hours can use these tools to give his congregation a more careful, more clearly built sermon than his time allows him to produce alone. Is a good sermon not better than a poorly prepared one? The argument has force. I want to answer it honestly rather than dismiss it.

> It will not necessarily be more alive.

The answer turns on what we mean by "better." A sermon that is more theologically polished but less personally witnessed is not obviously better. It may be, in a narrow technical sense, more correct. It will not necessarily be more alive. The congregation that needs to hear about the death and resurrection of hope does not primarily need theological accuracy about eschatology. It needs a person who has lost hope and found it again to stand up and say so. That kind of sermon is not improved by artificial intelligence. It is undermined by it, because the personal witness is the whole point.

The bi-vocational pastor working sixty hours a week at his day job and trying to pastor a congregation on the side is not helped by better AI output. He is helped by a theological recalibration that tells him: you are allowed to preach a shorter, less polished, more honest sermon. The congregation that gets a fifteen-minute word from a man who actually lives among them and has thought about this text for thirty concentrated minutes will receive something more valuable than a forty-minute sermon optimized by a language model that has never met any of them. And if the week has truly devoured the hours, the old honesty is still available: preach a faithful man's sermon and say so. Luther built the postils for exactly that week. What he did not build, and what no faithful century has built, is a way to present unwrestled words as wrestled ones.

---

## The Question AI Actually Raises

The pastoral question AI raises is not primarily "how do we use this tool responsibly?" That is a legitimate question, and it has workable answers.

The deeper question is: am I still doing the things that form a preacher, or have I begun substituting efficient output for the irreplaceable labor of actual encounter with God and with the people I serve?

Paul's charge to Timothy assumed that labor and named its temper: "preach the word; be ready in season and out of season; reprove, rebuke, and exhort, with complete patience and teaching" (2 Timothy 4:2 ESV). In season and out of season describes a man's years, not his output. Complete patience describes a formation, not a workflow. The charge is unexecutable by anything that does not live among the people it addresses, because readiness in and out of season is a quality of a life, and only a life can have it.

That question does not have an AI answer. It has a Thursday afternoon answer: the hours given to the text before the sermon, the prayer before the study, the deacon's bedside before the pulpit. The formation that makes preaching something other than content delivery is not a product of any technology. It is a product of the slow, mostly invisible work of a life genuinely given to the calling.

The congregation that receives a sermon a human being actually wrestled through, even if it is rougher, even if the transitions are less polished, even if it leaves a few exegetical questions unresolved, receives something the most sophisticated language model cannot produce: a witness. A person who went somewhere hard and came back changed.

The tools are new. The calling is not.

And the calling, finally, cannot be outsourced without ceasing to be what it is.
