The argument is over. The evidence ended it.

The center of global Christianity has moved, south into Africa and Latin America, east into Asia, and the fastest gospel advance on earth is now led by national pastors reaching their own peoples with a depth of language, trust, and cultural understanding no outsider can carry in. The church in Africa grew from under ten million Christians in 1900 to hundreds of millions today. The historian Andrew Walls spent his career documenting the shift and refused to treat it as a surprise: the center of gravity of the Christian world has moved before, he showed, from Jerusalem to the Greek cities to the barbarian north, because Christianity advances by crossing frontiers and has no permanent homeland. The church in China grew for decades after the expulsion of Western missionaries that began in 1949; there were perhaps a million Protestants in China when the missionaries left, and there are tens of millions now by the most conservative counts, and the growth came fastest in the years when no one from outside could reach it. William Carey asked the English church in 1792, in a pamphlet with the unglamorous title An Enquiry into the Obligations of Christians to Use Means for the Conversion of the Heathens, to take the nations seriously; the answer to his prayer turned out to be, in large part, men and women whose names the Western church has never learned.

There is a reason the advance runs deepest in local hands, and it is built into the faith itself. Lamin Sanneh, the Gambian scholar who taught at Yale, argued that Christianity is at bottom a translation movement. It has no sacred language the way Islam has Arabic; from the first Pentecost it has insisted on speaking every mother tongue on earth, and the crowd's astonishment is still the pattern: "we hear them telling in our own tongues the mighty works of God" (Acts 2:11 ESV). Translate the Scriptures into a people's language, Sanneh showed, and you have handed the faith to that people. The missionary who translates has signed the deed over, whether he meant to or not. The gospel in Yoruba belongs to the Yoruba. The New Testament in Mandarin does not need a visa.

What remains is the harder and humbler question. If the national pastor is the primary agent of the mission, what is left for the Western church to do? Not in principle. In practice. In budget lines, in relationships, in the next missions-committee meeting.

## The Answer the Church Wrote Down and Ignored

We have known the answer for a hundred and seventy years. In the 1850s, Henry Venn in London and Rufus Anderson in Boston, the two most influential mission executives of their age, arrived independently at the same formula: the goal of every mission is a church that is self-governing, self-supporting, and self-propagating, and the mission's proper ambition is its own disappearance. Venn gave that ambition an unsettling name. He wrote of the euthanasia of a mission, the good death that comes when the planted church has come of age and the scaffolding comes down. He also acted on it: under his hand Samuel Ajayi Crowther, taken from a slave ship as a boy, translated the Scriptures into his native Yoruba and in 1864 became the first African bishop of the Anglican communion. After Venn died, younger English missionaries spent Crowther's last years stripping his authority, because the principle was easier to write than to obey. It has been easier to write than to obey ever since.

In 1912 Roland Allen, an Anglican who had served in North China, pressed the point to its biblical root in Missionary Methods: St. Paul's or Ours? Paul, he observed, stayed in a city for months, at most a few years; he appointed local elders, entrusted them to God, and left. "And when they had appointed elders for them in every church, with prayer and fasting they committed them to the Lord in whom they had believed" (Acts 14:23 ESV). No subsidy from Antioch. No resident supervisor from the sending church. Allen's contemporaries ignored him almost completely; he told his son the church would not take the argument seriously until about 1960, and he was nearly exactly right. Where the formula was actually tried, the results ended the debate. The Presbyterian missionaries in Korea adopted a self-support plan after John Nevius visited them in 1890, and the Korean church that grew from it now sends missionaries by the thousand, for years more than any nation on earth except the United States.

So the question in the missions-committee meeting is not whether national leadership works. That argument is over. The question is why our structures keep behaving as if it were still open.

## Ask First, Then Believe the Answer

When national pastors in underresourced regions are actually asked what they need (asked, not told), the answers are consistent, and they are not what our structures assume.

They do not ask for our church planters. They ask for theological training that engages their actual context: their religious neighbors, their political pressures, the questions their congregations carry, rather than a transplant of a Western seminary curriculum built for questions no one in their village is asking. They ask for peer community: other pastors who understand the specific weight of their work, the companionship a Western pastor takes for granted and a rural Ethiopian or north Indian pastor may lack entirely. They ask for funding structured to build capacity rather than dependence: scholarships and training and infrastructure, not salaries that vanish the year the Western church's attention moves on. And beneath all of it, sometimes in words and sometimes only in the fatigue under the words, they ask for the dignity of being treated as partners in the mission rather than beneficiaries of our generosity.

Scripture has a name for that arrangement, and it is older than the modern mission. When John commended a church for supporting traveling workers it did not control, he defined the relationship in one phrase: "You will do well to send them on their journey in a manner worthy of God... Therefore we ought to support people like these, that we may be fellow workers for the truth" (3 John 6, 8 ESV). Fellow workers. Not staff. Not beneficiaries. Not evidence for the annual report. The churches of the New Testament funded work they did not direct, carried by people they had never met, and counted it fellowship.

None of that is exotic. All of it is harder than sending a team.

## Four Questions for the Missions Budget

Take the missions budget, the actual document, and ask four questions of every line.

Who set the direction? If the strategy was written in an American conference room and the national partner was recruited to execute it, the relationship is staffing, not partnership. The test is simple and uncomfortable: when the national pastor and the sending church disagree about priorities, whose judgment governs? There is a second version of the same test, quieter and more revealing. When did the sending church last change its mind because the national partner told it something it did not want to hear?

What does this dollar build? A salary paid from abroad creates a dependence that ends when the giving ends, and it ends; it nearly always ends. A scholarship, a training program, a peer network, a translated library: these remain. The question is not whether the money is generous. It is whether the money leaves anything behind that does not require more money to survive.

What happens when we leave? Roland Allen pressed this test on the church in 1912, and it has not aged. Paul planted churches, entrusted them to the Spirit and to local leaders, and left. If the honest answer is that the work collapses when we withdraw, we have not built a church's capacity. We have built our own necessity.

Would we accept this arrangement in reverse? If a wealthy foreign church funded our ministry on the condition that it approve our plans, receive our reports, and host our leaders for photographs, we would recognize the arrangement immediately. We would call it patronage. The national pastor recognizes it too. He is usually too gracious, and too much in need, to say so.

## The Failure Modes

The failures here rarely look like failures. They look like warm relationships.

There is the salaried partner, who cannot say a true hard thing to the people who fund his family's food, and whose reports therefore grow sunnier as his situation grows worse. There is the junior partner, present at the strategy meeting but not weighted in it, whose local knowledge is treated as color rather than as expertise, consulted about logistics and overruled about direction. There is the showcase partner, flown in to Western conferences to stand as evidence of the church's global reach, spending his scarce time validating our generosity. And there is the trip-shaped partnership, which exists at full strength for one week a year and as a line item the other fifty-one, and which quietly bends the national church's calendar around the visitors' itinerary.

I have helped build at least one of these. The warmth of the relationship concealed the asymmetry of it, and the asymmetry was the point I could not see, because I was standing on the higher end.

The deep failure under all four is theological before it is organizational. Money in these relationships behaves like gravity. It bends decisions toward its source without anyone announcing the bend, and both sides learn to call the bending gratitude. The New Testament knew this about wealth and said it without blinking; James watched the man with the gold ring get the good seat while the poor man stood in the back, and called the seating chart what it was (James 2:2-4). A partnership in which one side holds all the money, and both sides pretend the money is not in the room, has not escaped that chapter. It has moved it overseas.

## What It Looks Like When It Works

It looks like training designed around the questions the national pastor actually faces, delivered in ways he can access without leaving the people he serves. It looks like peer networks that connect him to other pastors across his region and beyond it, the model the Pastors Connection Network is built on: theological training in context, companionship across borders, funding aimed at capacity, and direction set by the pastor who lives there. It looks like the Mekane Yesus church in Ethiopia, which counts its members in the millions, more than any Lutheran body in Europe, and plants congregations in regions no Western agency has entered. Nothing about that work waits on our permission.

The partner's question is never "what should they do?" It is "what do they need to keep doing it?" Asked, answered, and believed.

The Great Commission does not name the senders' nationality. It names the scope and the authority: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:18-19 ESV). The authority is his, the nations are the field, and the Spirit has been filling that scope without waiting for our organizational charts. John saw where the whole enterprise ends: "a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne" (Revelation 7:9 ESV). No sending agency is named in the vision. No one in that crowd is anyone's project.

Paul watched the church at Corinth sort itself into parties behind its founders and answered with the only organizational chart the mission has ever had: "What then is Apollos? What is Paul? Servants through whom you believed, as the Lord assigned to each. I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth" (1 Corinthians 3:5-6 ESV).

We were never the center of this work. We were servants of it. The budget is where a church admits that, or does not.
