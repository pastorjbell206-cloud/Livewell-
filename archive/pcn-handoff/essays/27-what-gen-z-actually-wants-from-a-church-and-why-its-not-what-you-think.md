They are coming back.

After a decade of obituaries for young adult faith, the surveys keep finding Gen Z in the pews, at rates that meet or exceed the Millennials just ahead of them. Pew Research Center's largest survey of American religion, released in early 2025, found that the long decline in Christian identification had leveled off, and that the youngest adults in the survey were about as religious as the cohort just ahead of them, which had not been true in decades of measurement. In England, where the baseline was lower and the collapse further along, a 2025 Bible Society survey found that monthly church attendance among eighteen- to twenty-four-year-olds had roughly quadrupled since 2018, with young men leading the return. The researchers called it a quiet revival. Why they are returning is its own story. This piece is about the harder thing: what happens after they walk in. A generation can arrive and be lost again in eighteen months, and whether they stay has almost nothing to do with the welcome video.

The question is not how to attract them. Many of them are already in the parking lot. The question is whether the church they enter can receive what they bring: the questions, the wounds from previous church experience, and a trained eye for the gap between what is performed and what is real. They grew up inside the curated feed. They know staging when they see it. The church that wants to keep them will have to change things that are more expensive than the music.

## Worship That Tells the Truth

Start with the service itself, because that is where they take the first measurement.

The most consistent finding about this generation and institutions is that they do not expect perfection. They expect honesty, including honesty about failure, uncertainty, and the parts of faith that do not resolve on schedule. A church receives them by worshiping honestly: by letting the full texture of human experience into the room instead of sanding it off in rehearsal.

Scripture is already there, waiting. Roughly a third of the psalter is lament, the genre Walter Brueggemann calls the psalms of disorientation, and most of our services have quietly deleted it. Psalm 88 descends for eighteen verses and never comes up; its last line is "my companions have become darkness" (Psalm 88:18 ESV), and the Spirit saw fit to leave it there, unresolved, in the church's songbook. Job's friends, with their tidy explanations of his suffering, are the ones the book indicts; when God finally speaks about the debate, he says to Eliphaz, "you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (Job 42:7 ESV). The man who raged got the commendation. The men with the clean answers needed a sacrifice. And Matthew records that when the risen Jesus met the eleven on the mountain, "they worshiped him, but some doubted" (Matthew 28:17 ESV). Worship and doubt in the same verse, the same room, the same people, and Jesus commissions all of them anyway. The canon contains more lament and confusion than most Sunday services permit. Restoring it is not a concession to a demographic. It is a recovery of what we cut.

A generation raised on performances can smell one. What they have almost never seen is a room where grief is allowed to stand in the open without being hurried toward its resolution verse. Give them that room and you have given them something the feed cannot counterfeit.

## Doubt With a Spine

The anxious counterargument deserves its hearing: if the church becomes a community defined by uncertainty, it has stopped being the church. Peter did not stand up at Pentecost and hedge. He ended the first Christian sermon with a verdict: "God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified" (Acts 2:36 ESV). A congregation that will never make the specific claims of Christianity, that Jesus is Lord, that the resurrection happened, that the gospel is true, for fear of discomforting its young adults is not being hospitable. It is being cowardly. And a generation that has watched every other institution dissolve its own convictions on contact with criticism is not asking the church to do the same trick. They have seen the trick. They can get the trick anywhere.

The concern is right, and it does not require choosing. What this generation trusts is not certainty and not uncertainty. It is integration: the leader who can say "I believe this with my whole life" and also "I have had nights when I could not feel the truth of it and kept praying anyway." Conviction, with the questions allowed in the room. That is not a compromise between faith and doubt. It is what mature faith has always looked like up close, and the New Testament institutionalizes it. Jude's instruction to the church is three words long: "have mercy on those who doubt" (Jude 22 ESV). Mercy, not remediation. And the risen Christ himself set the pattern with Thomas, who had announced his terms like a modern skeptic. Jesus did not lecture him. He offered him the evidence of his own wounds: "Put your finger here, and see my hands; and put out your hand, and place it in my side. Do not disbelieve, but believe" (John 20:27 ESV). The doubter met wounds, not scolding, and out of that meeting came the highest confession in the Gospels: "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28 ESV). A church that wants Thomas's confession has to make room for Thomas's week of doubting first. The church that shames the week never hears the confession.

## Build the Table Before the Stage

Gen Z has access to more preaching, more worship music, and more theological content than any generation in the history of the church. No one drives forty-five minutes for a sermon they could hear in bed. What only physical presence provides is being known: belonging to a people, having a seat that is noticed when it is empty.

So the receiving church spends on community infrastructure before production quality. The small group that actually functions. The dinner table. The friendship that exists on Tuesday without a program requiring it. This is not a new church-growth technique; it is the oldest description of the church we have. "And they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers" (Acts 2:42 ESV), and then, a few verses later, the texture of it: "day by day, attending the temple together and breaking bread in their homes, they received their food with glad and generous hearts" (Acts 2:46 ESV). Homes. Food. Daily. The first church had no stage to invest in, and it grew in a culture at least as suspicious of it as this one is of us.

The diagnosis behind the hunger is now documented. Robert Putnam charted the collapse of American association in Bowling Alone (2000): the leagues, lodges, unions, and congregations that once wove strangers into neighbors, thinning out decade by decade. Jonathan Haidt's account of the great rewiring of childhood in The Anxious Generation (2024) names what happened next: the first generation to pass through adolescence on the smartphone, trading embodied play and presence for the feed, and reporting, by its own account, more anxiety and loneliness than any cohort in memory. Both books are worth a pastor's time, and both see the symptom truly. Neither can see all the way down, because the need is older than the phone. "It is not good that the man should be alone" (Genesis 2:18 ESV) is on the second page of the Bible; the diagnosis precedes the technology by the whole length of human history. The phone did not create the hunger for communion. It sold a counterfeit cheap enough that a generation gorged on the counterfeit and is now starving. The church that offers genuine, demanding community is not providing something nice. It is providing something they are starving for and cannot find at any price elsewhere.

## Trust Them With Real Things

They are impatient with the consumer model, come, receive, leave, in a way their parents were not. Robert Bellah and his co-authors named the underlying habit forty years ago in Habits of the Heart (1985): expressive individualism, the American instinct to treat every institution as a vendor of experiences for the self to select. The attractional church accepted that framing and built a better vendor. Gen Z is the first generation visibly bored by it. They want to contribute. The churches that keep them find ways to hand over genuine ministry: not token youth committees, not watered-down volunteer slots, but real responsibility with real stakes.

That means tolerating the messiness of inexperience. It means leadership pathways that move a young adult from attending to contributing to leading on a timeframe that respects both their capacity and their unwillingness to wait a decade for a seat at the table. It has apostolic precedent. Jesus sent the seventy-two out ahead of him (Luke 10:1) before they were anything like ready, and processed the failures with them when they came back. Paul told Timothy, a young man holding authority in a church full of his elders, "Let no one despise you for your youth, but set the believers an example in speech, in conduct, in love, in faith, in purity" (1 Timothy 4:12 ESV). The early church took formation seriously enough to be demanding about it; the third-century practice described in the Apostolic Tradition put catechumens through roughly three years of instruction before baptism. Note what that combination refuses: it refuses both the modern shortcuts. It neither entertained the young for a decade before trusting them, nor handed them a lanyard without forming them. High demand, real work. The church that trusts them with real things earns their real investment. The church that asks them only to attend has told them what it thinks they are.

## What It Looks Like

A woman in her mid-twenties started attending our church after two years of attending nowhere. She had grown up in a church she described as "clean": theologically tidy, emotionally polished, efficient. "I never saw anyone bleed there," she told me.

She stayed because of a small-group conversation in which a man ten years older said, out loud, that he had no idea how to pray in a season of depression and was doing it anyway. She had never heard a Christian say that before. It was, she said, the most credible thing she had ever heard in a church building.

She now leads that group.

## The Data Point Is Not a Destiny

The rise in Gen Z attendance is an opening, not an outcome. The same generation that came will leave again if what it finds is a presentation of community rather than a community, if the honesty stops at the branding, if the doubt is managed rather than met, if the responsibility on offer is decorative. The survey that found them arriving cannot promise they will remain, and the history of revivals is a history of openings the church answered and openings the church staged its way through and lost.

Receiving them well requires the church to be what it claims to be. That is not a communications challenge. It is a discipleship challenge, and it was ours before they arrived. Every weakness this generation exposes, the sanded-down worship, the managed doubt, the spectator seating, was already a weakness. They did not create the gap between what we perform and what we are. They were simply raised with the training to see it.

Gen Z is not looking for a better product. They are looking for a real community. The church that has built one, honestly, slowly, imperfectly, at cost, has something to offer. The church that has built a presentation of one does not.

The difference is visible from the parking lot. And Gen Z, more than any generation in recent memory, knows how to look.
