The conversation everyone is dreading has already happened a hundred times. It has happened in the car on the way home, when the executive pastor's wife asked how it went and he said nothing and stared at the road. It has happened in the elder meeting, in the long pause after someone says his name. It has happened in the worship leader's own gut at two in the morning. The only place it has not happened is the one place it needs to happen, which is across a desk, in daylight, with the words said plainly. We will keep almost anyone for almost any length of time to avoid that one room.

## Why It Is Harder Here

Firing someone in ministry is harder than firing someone anywhere else, and we should say why instead of pretending otherwise. In a normal job, the relationship is contractual. In a church, the relationship is covenantal, or it feels that way. You prayed together. You buried his father. His kids call you by a nickname. You stood in front of the congregation and said God had called this person to this work, and now you are sitting across from him preparing to say it is over. The language of calling and the language of employment are tangled together, and we use the tangle as an excuse to do nothing.

> Grace is the center of what we preach.

Let me give the case for patience its full strength, because it is a strong case and I have hidden behind it. Grace is the center of what we preach. We believe people can change. We believe the Spirit is not finished with anyone. We have all known a leader who was struggling in month eight and thriving in month twenty, and we are right to fear the kind of pastor who fires fast and prays slow. Scripture is full of restoration, of Peter rebuilt on a beach after he had failed at the worst possible moment. A church that cuts its people loose at the first sign of weakness is not a church, it is a firm with a steeple. The patient pastor is protecting something real.

## When Patience Becomes a Costume

But grace and clarity are not enemies, and we treat them as if they were. The patient pastor has performed a sleight of hand on himself. He has decided that keeping the failing staff member is the gracious choice, when in fact it is often the cowardly one wearing grace as a costume. Grace toward the underperforming worship leader is not the only grace in the room. There is also the grace owed to the volunteers he is exhausting, the grace owed to the congregation he is no longer serving, the grace owed to the man himself, who deserves to know the truth while he is still young enough to find the thing he is actually built for. By calling my avoidance grace, I was spending other people's grace to protect my own comfort.

## The Role Belongs to the Congregation

The text I want to carry is the appointing of the seven in Acts, because it shows that ministry roles exist for a function and not as a reward. The widows were being neglected in the daily distribution, and the apostles did not say the men currently failing at the task deserved patience. They said the work had to be done, and they sought out men full of the Spirit and of wisdom to do it (Acts 6:3 ESV). The role served the people. When the role stops serving the people, the role has to change hands, and the kindness is owed first to the people the role exists for. We have inverted this. We treat the staff position as belonging to the staff member, a possession we would be cruel to take away, when it was never his. It was the congregation's, on loan.

> The lie is the signal. When you are lying to keep the peace, the peace is already gone.

So when. Not for one bad season, and not for a personality you find tiring, and never for telling you the truth you did not want to hear. Fire for a sustained gap between the work and the person that prayer and coaching and time have not closed, when you have named it clearly more than once and given a real path and watched the path go unwalked. Fire for character, faster than you want to, because a gifted manipulator does more damage in a church than an honest underperformer ever will. Fire when keeping someone requires you to lie, to the elders about how it is going, to the congregation about why numbers are slipping, to the person himself about a future that is not coming. The lie is the signal. When you are lying to keep the peace, the peace is already gone.

## Corporate Tools and Covenantal Nerves

The history of how we got so timid is worth tracing. The professionalization of ministry, which gathered speed across the twentieth century and accelerated after the church growth movement of the nineteen seventies built the multi-staff church, gave us employees without giving us the stomach to manage them. We borrowed the human resources department's vocabulary, the performance plan and the documented warning, but we kept a theology of the pastorate that treats every staff member as an ordained brother whose removal is a kind of excommunication. So we freeze. We have the corporate tools and the covenantal nerves, and the two cancel out, and the failing leader stays for three years while everyone suffers and calls it love.

Edwin Friedman saw this paralysis everywhere he looked, and he named its real cause. He said the chronically anxious system will always pressure the leader to relieve its anxiety, and the most anxious members will set the agenda, so the leader who cannot tolerate someone else's pain will be ruled by whoever is most willing to inflict it on him (Friedman 2007). The pastor who cannot fire is not more loving. He is less differentiated. He has let the discomfort of one hard conversation hold an entire church hostage. The failure of nerve does not look like failure. It looks like patience. That is what makes it so durable.

## The Cross as Law Before Grace

Here is the reversal that took me too long to learn. The kindest thing is almost always the earlier thing. The longer you wait, the worse the eventual conversation gets for everyone, especially the person you are protecting. Keep a man two years past the point of fit and you have not given him grace, you have given him two years of failing publicly, two years of his confidence eroding, two years of a marriage straining under a job he cannot do, before you finally say what you could have said with dignity at month nine. Delay does not soften the blow. It compounds it and then delivers it anyway, with interest he had no idea he was accruing.

## When Discernment Becomes a Hiding Place

Before the day comes, the cross has to do its work on you, and it does it as law before it does it as grace. The law first. The reason you cannot have this conversation is that you need this man to walk out still thinking well of you, and that need is an idol, the same idol the apostles refused when they would not let the neglected widows go on being neglected to spare anyone's feelings. You have made your own reputation as a kind employer into a small god, and it is costing the very people the role exists to serve. That is sin, and naming it as sin is the only thing that breaks its grip. The grace second. The cross says your standing was settled by Another before you ever managed a single staff member, bought in blood you did not spill, so you do not have to purchase this man's approval with one more year of his slow failure. You are free to tell the truth precisely because the verdict over you is not his to give. The leader who has heard that law and received that grace can deliver hard news from a settled place and not from anger, because he is no longer secretly trying to make the other person agree that he is fair. Tell the truth plainly. Own your part in waiting too long. Be generous with severance and time and a real reference, because how you end a tenure preaches to everyone still on staff about what you actually believe. Ruth Haley Barton writes that leaders have to attend to their own souls precisely so that their decisions flow from God and not from their own unexamined fear (Barton 2008). The firing done from a settled soul is a different act than the firing done from panic, even when the words are the same.

There is one more place we hide, and it is the holiest-sounding excuse of all. We say we are waiting on the Lord. We say we have not yet received clarity, that we are praying for wisdom, that we do not want to run ahead of God. And sometimes that is true and good. But often the language of discernment is the language of paralysis with a stained-glass window in front of it. Ruth Haley Barton writes that we have to be honest about the difference between genuine waiting on God and the spiritualized procrastination that lets us avoid a decision we already know we have to make (Barton 2008). The Spirit who gave us wisdom also gave us the courage to act on it. When the clarity has been sitting in your chest for six months and you are still calling it discernment, the thing you are waiting on is not God. It is your own willingness to be disliked.

> Grace tells the truth in time. Cowardice waits for it to die.

The goal was never to be a man who can fire easily. The goal is to be a man who can tell the truth in love when love and truth both cost something. Most of us are not too quick to let people go. We are too slow, and we baptize the slowness, and the people we claim to be protecting pay for our comfort.

Grace tells the truth in time. Cowardice waits for it to die.
