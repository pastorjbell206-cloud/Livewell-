It is the second Sunday of November and the preacher has a decision to make before he opens his mouth. Half the room voted one way. Half voted the other. Both halves are watching his eyes for a sign. He could mention nothing, and the half that wanted him to speak will hear cowardice. He could mention something, and the other half will hear betrayal. He stands there with a text in his hand and a country in his pews, and there is no neutral sentence available to him. Even the weather report would be heard as a verdict.

This is the bind, and pretending it does not exist is the first failure. Karl Barth is widely paraphrased as telling young preachers to take the Bible in one hand and the newspaper in the other, and that compressed line gets quoted as permission to politicize everything. What he actually said, in a 1963 interview, was harder. Take your Bible and take your newspaper, he told them, and read both, but interpret the newspaper from your Bible and never once the Bible from the newspaper (Barth 1963). The moment the newspaper starts editing the Bible, you no longer have a preacher. You have a chaplain to a faction, blessing what the faction already decided to do.

## The Case for a Clean Pulpit

The strongest argument for keeping politics out of the pulpit is not cowardice, and it is dishonest to treat it as such. It runs like this. The church gathers people who disagree about tax policy and immigration and war, and it gathers them around a table that those disagreements cannot reach. When the preacher takes a side on the contested questions of the hour, he fences off the table. He tells the brother who voted differently that he is welcome to commune but not to belong. Worse, he binds the eternal gospel to a platform that will look foolish in twenty years, and when the platform falls the gospel is dragged down with it. Keep the pulpit clean, this argument says, so that the one thing the world cannot hear anywhere else stays audible. I find this argument serious. I have used it. There were Sundays I used it to protect myself, and called the protection wisdom.

But take it at its strongest and it still fails at one point, and the point is decisive. The argument assumes there is a category called politics that sits outside the gospel, a separate room the preacher can choose to enter or avoid. There is no such room. When Scripture says that the Lord executes justice for the oppressed and gives food to the hungry (Psalm 146:7 ESV), it has already entered the room the argument wanted to keep sealed. The question of whether the hungry eat is not adjacent to the gospel. It is downstream of the character of God. To refuse to speak it is not neutrality. It is a vote for the way things are, cast by a man who has convinced himself that not voting is an option.

## Amos at Bethel

> To refuse to speak it is not neutrality.

Carry the prophet Amos as the spine, because Amos is where this gets tested. He was a herdsman from Tekoa, not a court preacher, and around the year 760 before Christ he walked north into the prosperous kingdom of Israel under Jeroboam the Second and said the most political thing imaginable. He named the economics by name. They sell the righteous for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals (Amos 2:6 ESV). He named the women of the comfortable class as cows of Bashan who crush the needy and tell their husbands to bring drinks (Amos 4:1 ESV). This is not gentle. This is a man pointing at the policy of a nation and the dinner habits of its donors. And the priest Amaziah did exactly what the keep-it-clean argument would have him do. He told Amos to take his preaching back south, to earn his bread somewhere else, because the sanctuary at Bethel was the king's, a temple of the kingdom (Amos 7:13 ESV).

The timing of Amos matters, and it indicts us, because Jeroboam's reign was a boom. The borders were wide, the markets were full, the festivals were crowded, and the worship was, by every external measure, thriving. This is the detail we skip. Amos did not preach into a collapsing nation begging for a word. He preached into a confident, religious, prosperous one, a nation whose churches were full and whose conscience was asleep, and the thing the Lord could not stomach was precisely the worship. I hate, I despise your feasts, he says, and takes no delight in your solemn assemblies (Amos 5:21 ESV). The singing offended God because the singing covered the selling of the needy. That is the political sermon at its sharpest, the one that walks into the largest, happiest congregation in the land and says the songs are an offense because of what the singers do on Monday. No preacher gives that sermon by accident, and no preacher gives it to stay popular.

## Silence Has an Owner

There it is, the whole problem in one sentence from a priest. Bethel was the king's sanctuary. Amaziah had made the pulpit an arm of the palace, and so the only politics he could not hear was the politics of God. He thought he was keeping religion out of politics. He had it exactly backward. He had let politics swallow the religion so completely that the word of the Lord sounded to him like sedition. This is the warning for every preacher who imagines that silence keeps him clean. Silence has an owner. At Bethel the owner was the king.

So the danger runs in both directions, and honesty requires naming the second one as plainly as the first. The man who preaches the party from the pulpit has lost the gospel just as surely as Amaziah lost it, only from the opposite door. He has taken the cross, which judges every kingdom, and he has stapled it to one kingdom and called the staple faithfulness. John Stott described preaching as bridge-building between the ancient text and the modern world, and he warned that a bridge anchored only on the modern bank is no bridge at all, just a ramp into the prejudices of the age (Stott 1982). The party preacher has built a ramp. He flatters the half of the room that already agreed with him and calls their agreement revival.

## The Cross No Party Can Survive

> When you preach the cross with its full weight, you do not take a side in the room's argument.

Here is the line that holds, and it is the line of the cross itself. The cross is political, because it was a political execution carried out by an empire on a man it considered a threat to public order. But the cross is political in a way that no party can survive. It indicts the religious leaders who handed him over and the imperial governor who washed his hands and the crowd that chose Barabbas, and it leaves no faction standing as the hero. When you preach the cross with its full weight, you do not take a side in the room's argument. You announce a judgment that falls on both sides and a mercy offered to both. That is harder than partisanship and harder than silence, because both halves will be uncomfortable, and the preacher will not get the warm thing that comes from telling people they are right.

Law before grace, and here the law has to find the actual idol. For one half of the room the idol is order, the fear that the country is slipping and only power can hold it. For the other half the idol is progress, the certainty that history is theirs and the resisters are obstacles. The preacher who only ever convicts one of these idols has been captured by the other. I have stood in front of a room and known exactly which idol I was willing to name and which one I would let sleep, and the one I let sleep was always the one my own heart was guarding. The newspaper had gotten into my Bible after all.

There is a test for whether you have preached politics or preached the party, and it is brutal in its simplicity. After the sermon, which half of the room felt comforted. If the same half walks out warmed every single week, the half that already agreed with you on Friday, then you have not been preaching the cross. You have been preaching the platform, and the cross has become the decoration on the platform. The genuinely prophetic word leaves your own side uneasy too, because the kingdom of God does not map onto any ballot, and the man who has heard it rightly goes home unable to feel fully at home in the camp he arrived in. I came to faith out of atheism, which means I have no inherited tribe in the church to protect, and I still catch myself protecting one, the one I drifted into, the one whose approval feels like belonging. The pull is not theological. It is the simple hunger to be on a side that will have me, and that hunger has bent more sermons than bad exegesis ever did.

## The Test of the Comforted Half

> The genuinely prophetic word leaves your own side uneasy too, because the kingdom of God does not map onto any ballot, and the man who has heard it rightly goes home unable to feel fully at home in the camp he arrived in.

So preach the issue when the text raises it, and let the text decide which issue and which week, not the news cycle, not the donor, not the half of the room you are afraid of. Brueggemann argued that the prophetic word does its work not by scolding but by offering a counter-world so vivid that the official world loses its grip (Brueggemann 1989). That is the aim. Not to tell people how to vote. To make the kingdom of God so concrete in their imagination that every kingdom of this world, including the one they voted for, looks smaller than it did on Tuesday.

You will lose people either way. Lose them to the gospel, not to the party.
