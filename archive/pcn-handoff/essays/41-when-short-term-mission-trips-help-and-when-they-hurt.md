Somewhere on your church calendar there is probably a trip. The dates are set. The fundraising letters may already be drafted.

This is not the argument about whether short-term missions mostly serve the people they visit or the people they send. That argument matters, the honest version of it is uncomfortable, and every missions leader should sit with it. But a second, working question remains after the argument, and it is the one this piece takes up: the trip is happening. Whether it helps or hurts will be decided now, in the planning, months before anyone boards a plane.

## How New This Is

It helps to remember how young this practice is, because we plan as though the apostles invented it. When William Carey sailed for India in 1793, he was not planning a week of ministry and a return flight. There was no return. He worked at Serampore for forty-one years and died there in 1834 without seeing England again. Adoniram Judson sailed in 1812 and gave the rest of his life to Burma. For most of church history, crossing the world for the gospel meant staying, and the sending church's part was to hold the rope for decades, not to visit the well.

The short-term trip is a creature of the airplane. Youth With A Mission was founded in 1960 around the new possibility that ordinary young believers could go and come back; cheap airfare did the rest. By the time the Princeton sociologist Robert Wuthnow published *Boundless Faith* (2009), he could estimate that roughly 1.6 million American Christians were traveling abroad on short-term teams every year. A practice barely one generation old now absorbs an enormous share of American missions money and energy, and nothing that new, and that expensive, has earned an exemption from examination.

The New Testament does contain one carefully documented project of cross-cultural giving, and its shape is instructive. Paul spent years organizing a collection from the Gentile churches for the poor believers in Jerusalem. The relationships were long. The money traveled with men the churches themselves chose, and Paul insisted on open books: "for we aim at what is honorable not only in the Lord's sight but also in the sight of man" (2 Corinthians 8:21 ESV). And the aim was explicitly mutual, dignity running in both directions: "For I do not mean that others should be eased and you burdened, but that as a matter of fairness your abundance at this present time should supply their need, so that their abundance may supply your need, that there may be fairness" (2 Corinthians 8:13-14 ESV). Long relationship, chosen representatives, public accountability, mutual benefit. The first international relief effort in church history would pass every test that follows. Many of our trips would not.

The fairest thing that can be said for the short-term trip is that it stands in a tradition older than fundraising letters: pilgrimage. Christians have always traveled to be changed, to Jerusalem, to Iona, to Canterbury, and a person who crosses a border and meets the church of the poor will often come home converted in ways no sermon ever managed. That defense is real, and it deserves honor. But a pilgrim knows what he is. He travels to be changed, not to change the destination, and he does not present his itinerary as aid. Most of the damage described below flows from a single confusion: pilgrimage dressed as rescue, formation billed as service. Name the two honestly and much of the harm never happens.

## The Three Conditions Under Which Trips Help

The trips that genuinely serve their hosts share a small set of features, and the features are structural, not sentimental.

The first is a long-term partnership. The team builds on an ongoing relationship between sending and receiving churches, contributes to work the receiving community has itself identified as a priority, and operates under the leadership of national believers rather than assuming its own. The trip is one week inside a ten-year relationship, not a transaction with photographs. This is the Pauline shape: the week is an episode in a relationship that existed before the tickets were bought and will continue after the luggage is unpacked, and the receiving church holds the pen when the plans are written.

The second is genuinely scarce skill. A medical team providing care that is actually unavailable locally. Specialized training no one nearby can offer. The test is blunt: does this team supply what no local worker can, or does it displace a local worker who needed the wage? A crew of unskilled American teenagers building a wall that local masons could build better, faster, and for pay is not help. It is a cross-cultural experience purchased at the expense of the local economy. Steve Corbett and Brian Fikkert draw the underlying distinction plainly in *When Helping Hurts* (2009): relief is for crisis, development is for chronic conditions, and applying relief where development is needed does harm with a clean conscience. Most receiving communities are not in crisis the week the team arrives. They are in the long middle of development, and development is precisely the work a week cannot do.

The third condition is honesty about formation. Some trips exist primarily to form the participants: to put comfortable American Christians inside the global church and the poverty it works within, because that encounter permanently reshapes a person's understanding of the gospel. Such a trip can be legitimate. But only if it is named for what it is, the host community consents to it and is compensated for its time, and the visitors arrive as learners rather than rescuers. A pilgrimage that calls itself a rescue corrupts both things at once.

## How Trips Hurt

The harms are documented. Corbett and Fikkert catalogued them in *When Helping Hurts*, Robert Lupton did the same in *Toxic Charity* (2011), and they follow patterns any planning team can check itself against. Unskilled labor that displaces local wages. Predetermined programs implemented without reference to what the community asked for. Dependency: the community that learns to schedule its needs around the annual arrival of Americans, so that the yearly medical team becomes the standing argument against staffing the local clinic. And the subtlest harm: the trip as spiritual commodity, producing testimonies and social media for the senders while the hosts return to the structural poverty a week of enthusiasm has not altered.

Lupton, after four decades of urban ministry, traced the arc of repeated one-way giving in a single line of descent: the first gift produces appreciation, the second anticipation, the third expectation, then entitlement, then dependency. Nothing in that line requires a villain. It only requires generosity that never becomes partnership, arriving on schedule, year after year, until the receiving community's own capacity is the one thing no one has invested in.

The research behind those patterns is more specific than most planning teams know. After Hurricane Mitch devastated Honduras in 1998, North American teams poured south to build houses, and Kurt Ver Beek, a sociologist at Calvin College living in Honduras, studied what happened and published the results in the journal Missiology in 2006. Teams were spending on the order of thirty thousand dollars in travel and expenses to build houses that Honduran workers could build for about two thousand. Homes built by visiting teams produced no greater lasting benefit for the recipients than homes built by paid local crews. And the senders fared no better under measurement: the trips produced almost no lasting change in the participants' giving or missions involvement once the glow faded. The houses were real. The transformation was mostly a feeling, and the feeling faded on schedule.

Jesus left a warning that sits closer to all this than we would like. "Beware of practicing your righteousness before other people in order to be seen by them, for then you will have no reward from your Father who is in heaven" (Matthew 6:1 ESV). He was talking about almsgiving performed for an audience. The verse anticipated the mission-trip slideshow by two thousand years, and notice that it does not say the alms were fake. It says the audience was the point, and that the giver has already collected his full payment, in the currency he actually wanted.

I have been on some version of these trips. I came home with the photographs and an enlarged sense of my own generosity. I did not ask, while I was there, whether my presence was the best use of the money it cost to send me. I should have asked.

## Five Questions Before the Deposit Is Paid

What has the receiving community asked for, in its own voice, not filtered through an agency brochure? If no one on the planning team can answer this, the planning has started at the wrong end. Asked is the operative word. A need observed from the sending side is a guess. A request made by the people who live there is information, and the difference between the two is the difference between service and staging.

What would it cost to hire local people to do what the team plans to do? If the answer is "a fraction of the airfare," have the honest conversation about who the trip is for, and consider sending the difference. This is the question teams most reliably refuse to ask, because the arithmetic is almost never in the team's favor, and everyone quietly knows it before the calculator comes out.

Who holds authority on the ground? If the national leader directs the team's work, the trip serves the mission. If he merely hosts a plan built in an American church office, the trip serves the trip. Authority shows itself in small things: who sets the daily schedule, who is allowed to change it, whose judgment ends a disagreement when the team wants one thing and the hosts another.

What surrounds the week? A trip with no relationship before it and no engagement after it is almost certainly benefiting the participants more than the hosts. The strongest pattern practitioners recommend is simple: raise more than the trip costs, so that money remains in the community and continues the work after the team is gone.

How will it be evaluated? By the transformation of the participants, or by benefit the receiving community would itself name? If the honest answer is the first, say so out loud, and then design the trip so the hosts are served rather than used in the process. Churches evaluate what they value, and the evaluation form is the real mission statement, whatever the brochure says.

## Twelve Years In

A pastor I know led a mid-sized suburban church with an active short-term program. He took teams to the same Central American community for twelve consecutive years. He knew the national pastor's name, his wife's name, their children's names. He had stayed in their home.

He told me that for the first four or five years his teams came with plans and left with photographs and produced, by his own honest accounting, very little the community could not have done better and cheaper without them. What changed everything was a conversation in year six, when the national pastor said, with the mixture of grace and directness that only real friendship permits, "What we need from you is not your volunteers. We need your pastors' network. We need help getting our young leaders into theological training we cannot reach from here."

The program changed. The trips became smaller and were about connecting rather than building. The logistics money began funding scholarships for local seminary students. The church in Michigan became a partner instead of a periodic benefactor.

That is what a trip looks like when the receiving community is finally allowed to define it. Notice what the redefinition required: twelve years, a real friendship, and a national pastor who trusted his American brother enough to risk the sentence. Most programs never receive that sentence, not because their hosts have nothing to say but because nothing in the relationship has made it safe to say it. The absence of correction from the field is not evidence of a healthy program. It is usually evidence of a polite one.

The communities on the receiving end of American mission trips deserve that level of care, and the gospel deserves to be pursued with enough wisdom to genuinely serve the people it claims to love. Start with what they asked for. End with whether they were better served than you were formed. Let the distance between those two questions be the work of this year's planning.
