A man called me late on a Tuesday, ashamed in a way he could barely put into words. He pastored a small church in a small town, forty people on a good Sunday, and to feed his family he drove a delivery route four days a week. He had spent the afternoon at a denominational gathering listening to talk of growth strategies and full time placements and church health metrics, and he had come away convinced he was a failure, a man who could not make ministry work and had to prop it up with a real job. He used those words. A real job. As if the route were the embarrassment and the pulpit were the dream that had not quite come true.

I wanted to reach through the phone. Because I have stood near that shame, and because the verdict he had absorbed is not just discouraging, it is a lie with a long history, and the lie has done real damage to real men who deserved better from the church that formed them.

The term we use is bivocational, a pastor who holds paid work outside the church alongside his ministry. The word itself reveals the problem. We call it bivocational as if the second job were a separate vocation grudgingly added on, a compromise, a sign that the church could not afford to do the thing properly. The assumption buried in our language is that the normal, mature, successful pastor is fully funded by the congregation, free of secular labor, devoted entirely to the work of ministry, and that the man on the delivery route is a developmental stage on the way to that ideal, or worse, a man who never got there.

## The Case for Full Time Support

Let me state the strongest case for full time, salaried ministry, because it is a serious case and I am not interested in romanticizing poverty. The argument runs this way. The laborer deserves his wages. Paul himself wrote that those who preach the gospel should get their living by the gospel, that the elder who labors in preaching and teaching is worthy of double honor, including financial honor. A pastor pulled in two directions cannot give the church his full attention. Study suffers. Visitation suffers. The man is exhausted. A congregation that truly valued its shepherd would free him to shepherd, and the drive toward full time support is therefore not worldliness but love. There is genuine truth in this. Scripture does defend the right of ministers to be paid, and a church that could support its pastor and refuses to, out of stinginess, is sinning against him. I will not pretend otherwise.

So I grant the wages. And then I notice who wrote the verse about them.

## The Apostle Who Made Tents

> The dirt under his fingernails was not a failure of fundraising.

The text I want to carry is Paul describing his own practice, and it should end the shame on the delivery route forever. 'You yourselves know that these hands ministered to my necessities and to those who were with me. In all things I have shown you that by working hard in this way we must help the weak' (Acts 20:34-35 ESV). The man who wrote that ministers deserve their wages chose, repeatedly, not to take them. He made tents. He worked with his hands, by his own account often into exhaustion, so that the gospel would cost his hearers nothing and so that no one could accuse him of preaching for profit. The greatest missionary in the history of the church was, by our anxious modern definition, bivocational. The dirt under his fingernails was not a failure of fundraising. It was a deliberate strategy, and he held it up as an example to follow.

## The Historical Novelty Reversed

This is the reversal the ashamed pastor needs. Bivocational ministry is not a fallback from the New Testament model. In significant part it is the New Testament model. The fully salaried, professionally credentialed, vocationally segregated pastor is the historical novelty, not the bivocational tentmaker. For most of the church's life, in most of the world even now, the man who preaches on Sunday works alongside his people the rest of the week, and that arrangement was not a defect to be outgrown. It was a witness. It put the pastor inside the same economic weather as his congregation, subject to the same bosses and deadlines and money fears, and it made his preaching about work and money and ambition something other than theory.

The historical root here is worth naming with care, because the Reformation cuts both ways on this question. When Luther flattened the staircase between sacred and secular labor, he did something we have only half received. He declared that the cobbler and the preacher stood on level ground before God, that there was no holy class of work and no profane class. I treat this lightly, because the larger story has been told, but follow what it means for the man on the delivery route. If Luther was right, then the delivery route is not the unholy half of his week that subsidizes the holy half. The route is itself ministry, holy labor, the love of neighbor through useful work, no less sacred than the sermon. The bivocational pastor is not a man living half in the sacred and half in the secular. He is a man for whom Luther's insight has become his actual life, because he cannot afford the illusion that the two are separable.

## Luther on the Delivery Route

Lester DeKoster would push it further. Work, he argued, is the form love takes when it becomes useful to a neighbor, and the worker who serves people through honest labor is doing exactly what the worker was made to do (DeKoster 1982). The pastor delivering packages is loving the people on his route in the most concrete way available, putting bread on tables, keeping the small machinery of a community running, and then standing up on Sunday to preach to people who know he understands their week because he has lived it. That is not a diluted ministry. In certain ways it is a more credible one. The skeptic in the pew who suspects the clergy of living off other people's labor has no such suspicion of the man who clocks in beside him on Monday.

Now let the law fall, because it must fall on the church, not on the bivocational pastor. The shame that man carried was put on him. It was manufactured by a church culture that measures success by salary and staff size, that treats the small congregation as a problem to be solved rather than a flock to be loved, that has so absorbed the metrics of the corporation that it can no longer see the tentmaker as anything but a man who failed to scale. That is our sin, and I include myself in it, because I have admired the wrong things and praised the wrong men and made the faithful pastor of forty people feel like a footnote. We did this to him. The repentance belongs to us.

## The Shame the Church Manufactured

And here the grace, which is the same grace that funded Paul's tents. The pastor's worth was never going to come from his salary or his attendance or his place in the denominational hierarchy. It was secured at a cross, by a carpenter, a man who worked with his hands for thirty years before he preached a word, who knew the smell of sawdust and the ache of labor and chose to enter the world through a workshop rather than a palace. The Lord the bivocational pastor serves was himself, by every measure his denomination would apply, a working man. There is no shame in the route, because there was no shame in the carpenter's shop.

## The Grace That Funded the Tents

> There is no shame in the route, because there was no shame in the carpenter's shop.

So bivocational ministry done right is not full time ministry with an asterisk. It is ministry in the line of Paul and in the shadow of the carpenter, a pastor who has refused to let his calling float free of the economic weather his people live in, who preaches about work with his own hands still sore from it. Done right, it asks the congregation to honor the man and support him as they are able, because the laborer does deserve his wages. And done right, it refuses the lie that the man who cannot yet be paid in full is anything less than a real pastor with a real calling.

The man on the delivery route hung up that night a little steadier. He is still driving. He is still preaching. And the next time someone asks him when he is going to get a real job, I hope he remembers that the realest pastor who ever lived made tents, and the realest one of all made tables.
