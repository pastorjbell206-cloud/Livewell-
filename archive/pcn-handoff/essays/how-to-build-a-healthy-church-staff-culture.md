A staff meeting opens with prayer, and three of the five people praying are afraid of the person leading it. They are not afraid he will yell. They are afraid of something quieter than that. They are afraid of the look that crosses his face when someone disagrees, the way the temperature in the room drops two degrees, the email that comes at nine that night with the subject line that just says 'Quick thought.' So they pray, and they smile, and they keep the real conversation for the parking lot. The culture of that staff is not the printed values on the breakroom wall. The culture is what happens in the parking lot.

## Comfortable Is Not Healthy

Most pastors think they are building a healthy staff culture when what they are building is a comfortable one. The two are not the same. A comfortable culture is a place where nobody contradicts the leader and everyone leaves on time and the conflict goes underground where it ferments. A healthy culture is a place where the hardest thing on the table gets said by the person least afraid to say it, and nobody loses standing for saying it. I have run both kinds. I know the difference from the inside, because for years I mistook the silence in my own staff meetings for peace, and it was not peace. It was the absence of trust dressed up as agreement.

> Most pastors think they are building a healthy staff culture when what they are building is a comfortable one.

Let me steel-man the comfortable manager, because he is not a villain and he is often me. He has read the studies. He knows that turnover in ministry is brutal, that pastors burn out, that a church split can take a decade to heal. So he protects the calm. He smooths the friction. He tells himself that a peaceful staff is a faithful staff, that unity is a fruit of the Spirit, that he is shepherding tender people through a hard calling. And he is right about the tenderness. He is right that people in ministry carry wounds that the corporate world does not see. The instinct to protect them is a good instinct that has been pointed in the wrong direction.

## Trust Is What Makes Conflict Survivable

Here is what he gets wrong. He has confused the absence of conflict with the presence of trust, and Patrick Lencioni spent a career showing that these are nearly opposites (Lencioni 2002). Trust is what makes conflict survivable. Where trust is high, people fight about ideas in the open and walk out of the room intact. Where trust is low, people agree in the room and undermine in the hallway. The peaceful staff the comfortable manager is so proud of is not a sign of trust. It is a sign that trust is so thin nobody dares to test it. He is not protecting his people. He is starving them of the one thing that would let them tell him the truth.

## The Towel and the Settled Soul

The spine text here is the night before the cross, and it is stranger than we let it be. 'Jesus, knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he had come from God and was going to God, rose from supper. He laid aside his outer garments, and taking a towel, tied it around his waist' (John 13:3-4 ESV). Read the logic of that sentence. John tells us first that Jesus knew the full extent of his authority, that everything had been placed in his hands, and then, because of that knowledge and not in spite of it, he got on the floor and washed twelve sets of dirty feet. The security came first. The service came out of the security. He could kneel because there was nothing left to prove.

> The security came first. The service came out of the security. He could kneel because there was nothing left to prove.

That is the engine of a healthy staff culture, and almost nobody runs on it. The leader who knows where his standing comes from can take the lowest seat in the room without losing himself. The leader who does not know is the one who needs the meeting to affirm him, who cannot let a critique pass without correcting the record, who washes nobody's feet because he is still waiting for someone to wash his. A church staff inherits the anxiety of the person at the top. If the senior leader is grasping, the whole staff grasps. If he can kneel, he gives them permission to stop performing.

## How We Imported the Org Chart

We did not get here by accident, and the history matters. The modern church staff is a young invention. For most of Christian history the local congregation had a pastor and perhaps a sexton, and the idea of an executive pastor, a creative director, a connections coordinator, and a discipleship pastor reporting through an org chart would have been unintelligible. The multi-staff church as we know it is largely a product of the church growth movement that took shape in the nineteen seventies and eighties, when Donald McGavran's work at Fuller and the rise of the seeker-sensitive megachurch taught a generation to think of the congregation as an organization to be scaled. We imported the org chart from the corporation, and with it we imported the corporation's diseases, the politics, the performance reviews, the quiet anxiety of people whose livelihood depends on a manager's mood.

None of that is wrong in itself. Structure is a gift. But we adopted the structure without adopting the safeguard, and the safeguard is theological, not managerial. Edwin Friedman, a rabbi who spent his life studying leadership in congregations and families, named the missing piece. He called it self-differentiation, the capacity of a leader to stay connected to people while staying clear about his own convictions, to hold steady in the presence of someone else's anxiety without absorbing it or reacting to it (Friedman 2007). The differentiated leader does not need the staff to be calm in order to be calm himself. That is what lets him invite the hard conversation. He can hear it without coming apart.

## The Daily Work of Lowering the Temperature

So what do you actually do. Begin with the thing you can control, which is your own face in the room. Watch what happens to the temperature when someone disagrees with you, and learn to keep it level on purpose, because the staff is reading you for permission and you are giving it whether you mean to or not. Then make the disagreement count for something. When a junior staff member tells you a plan will not work and she is right, change the plan in front of everyone and say out loud who changed your mind. Nothing teaches a staff that candor is safe faster than watching candor win once. Bonhoeffer warned that the leader who arrives with a vision of the perfect community and demands that the real people conform to it will end up destroying both the vision and the people (Bonhoeffer 1954). Lead the staff you have, not the one in your head.

And ration your reactions. Henri Nouwen wrote that the great temptation of Christian leadership is the temptation to be relevant, to be spectacular, to be powerful, and that the antidote is a leadership that descends rather than climbs, that is willing to be led where it would rather not go (Nouwen 1989). A healthy staff culture is built by a leader who has made peace with not being the most impressive person in the room. He does not need the win in every exchange. He has already washed feet. He has nothing to prove and therefore everything to give.

## The Test at the Bottom of the Room

There is a test for whether the culture is actually changing, and it is a quiet one. Listen for what your newest and least powerful staff member is willing to say in front of the room. The intern, the part-time administrator, the young hire who joined six months ago and has the most to lose by speaking. When that person disagrees with you out loud, without lowering her voice and without checking the faces of the older staff first, you have built something real. Until that day, every assurance that your people feel free to speak is a thing you are telling yourself, not a thing they have shown you. The health of a staff is measured at its bottom, not its top, the same way Jesus measured greatness on the night he reached for the towel.

The culture you have is the one your people describe in the parking lot. You will not change it with a values document or a ropes course or a verse on the wall. You will change it the first time someone says the dangerous true thing in the meeting and watches you receive it without flinching. That is the whole work, and it never ends. The towel does not stay tied. You pick it up again every Tuesday.

> A healthy staff is not a quiet one. It is a brave one.
