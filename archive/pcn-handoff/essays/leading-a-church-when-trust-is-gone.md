I followed a man who had lied to the congregation for years. Not about money, which would have been simpler, but about who he was, a pattern of deception that came apart all at once and left a church of good people unable to believe anything said from the pulpit, including the things that were true. I arrived eight months after he left. The wound was not in the budget. It was in the eyes. People listened to me preach with their arms folded, not because they disliked me but because the last man had stood in the same spot and sounded sincere, and look what that was worth. You cannot lead people who have learned that listening is dangerous. And they had learned it from a master.

## The Wound, Not the Obstacle

The instinct in that situation is to rebuild trust by being trustworthy, by being competent and consistent and clearly not the last guy, and that instinct is not wrong but it is not nearly enough. The real question when trust is gone is not how the leader proves himself. The real question is whether the leader understands that broken trust is not primarily a problem to be solved but a wound to be grieved, and that the people are not being difficult, they are being faithful to an injury they did not ask for. A pastor who treats lost trust as an obstacle to his leadership has already revealed that he does not understand it. The lost trust is not in his way. It is the thing he is there to serve.

## When Trust Language Becomes a Weapon

> The lost trust is not in his way. It is the thing he is there to serve.

Here is the strongest case against my whole approach, and it comes from sharp people who have watched too many churches paper over real harm. They will say that talk of rebuilding trust is often a tool of institutional self-protection, that when leaders call for unity and healing they usually mean be quiet and let us move on, and that the demand for renewed trust frequently falls hardest on the very people who were hurt, asking the wounded to extend something to the institution that wounded them. This is not cynicism. It is observation, and it is frequently correct. The history of the church's handling of abuse and deception is a history of premature calls for reconciliation that served the powerful and silenced the harmed. I have to grant this in full, because to wave it away would be to commit the exact sin it names. So the honest version of the objection is this. Trust language is often a weapon against the wounded. And the only answer that is not itself a dodge is to insist that real trust can never be rebuilt by asking the hurt to pretend. It can only be rebuilt by leaders willing to stand inside the indictment rather than above it.

## Restoration by the Charcoal Fire

The text I carry into these churches is the most uncomfortable restoration scene in the New Testament. Peter had denied Christ three times, publicly, by a fire, when it cost something to be loyal. And the risen Jesus does not restore him with a warm word and a clean slate. By a charcoal fire, the same kind of fire Peter stood at when he lied, Jesus asks him three times, once for each denial, do you love me (John 21:15 ESV). It is not gentle. The text says Peter was grieved because he asked him the third time (John 21:17 ESV). Jesus rebuilds trust by walking Peter straight back into the smell of his own failure and refusing to let him skip past it. This is the pattern, and it is the opposite of how we usually do it. We rebuild trust by changing the subject. Christ rebuilds it by returning to the wound, naming it fully, and only then speaking the future. Feed my sheep. The commission comes after the fire, never instead of it.

## How We Taught the World to Distrust Us

> Christ rebuilds it by returning to the wound, naming it fully, and only then speaking the future.

The history of this in the church is long and not flattering, and naming it is part of the repair. The crises of trust that have hollowed out congregations across the last several decades, the financial scandals, the abuse coverups, the celebrity pastors whose private lives contradicted every public word, did not erode trust by accident. They eroded it through a consistent institutional reflex to protect the name over the truth, exactly the Sardis reflex, reputation kept alive after integrity had died. Gallup has measured public confidence in American institutions since the early nineteen seventies, and the long line for nearly every institution, the church among them, bends downward across the decades that follow, and the church accelerated its own fall every time it chose the cover story over confession (Gallup 2022). We were not merely victims of a cynical age. We taught a watching world to distrust us, one cover-up at a time, and the young especially have learned the lesson well.

## The Man Who Stops Trying to Be Trusted

Now the reversal, the thing that took me three years in that wounded church to learn. The leader cannot rebuild trust by being trusted. He rebuilds it by being trustworthy in a way that does not depend on whether anyone trusts him yet, and that means his faithfulness has to be aimed past the congregation's approval entirely. The moment I needed their trust in order to lead, I made my leadership a transaction, and they could smell the need, and the need is exactly what the last man had. He needed their belief and so he managed his image to secure it. The only way I could be different was to stop needing their trust and start simply telling the truth, including the truths that cost me, including the times I was wrong in front of them, with no anxious eye on whether it was working. Trust, it turns out, is never built by the man who is trying to build it. It is the slow accidental fruit of a man who has stopped trying and started confessing. Eugene Peterson said that the pastor's integrity is in the unseen disciplines, the angles no congregation watches, and that a ministry built for the visible approval rots at the unseen root (Peterson 1987, 2).

## The Rescuer Fantasy

So the law comes, and it comes for the leader before it comes for the people who broke trust. The law says that a pastor inheriting a wounded church will be tempted to use the wound, to position himself as the trustworthy rescuer against the villain who left, and that this self-positioning is its own quiet lie. I have done it. I have let a congregation's relief at my arrival become a thing I enjoyed too much, have let myself be the hero of a story whose real hero is Christ and whose real victims deserved more lament than I gave them. The law indicts the rescuer fantasy. It says that the man who rebuilds trust by being the not-villain is still making the church about himself, just in a more flattering pose. I cannot stand outside this. I am exactly the kind of leader who would rather be admired for honesty than actually pay its cost.

And then grace, which is the only ground a wounded church can finally stand on. The grace is that the trust the congregation most needs to recover is not trust in their leaders, who will fail them again because all leaders are men, but trust in the Christ who restored Peter at the fire and did not flinch from the wound. A church whose trust in its pastors has been destroyed has, painfully, been freed from an idol it should never have had. It was never safe to put ultimate trust in a man. The collapse of that trust, brutal as it is, can become the doorway to a deeper and unbreakable trust in the only One who has never once managed His image, who told the truth about us at the cost of His life, and who returns to our fires not to shame us but to commission us. Feed my sheep is spoken to a man who failed and was not pretended past it. That is the only kind of leader a broken church can follow. One who knows the fire.

> One who knows the fire.

Trust does not come back when the leader proves he is worthy. It comes back, slowly, when the leader stops needing it and starts telling the truth, especially the truth that costs him. There is no shortcut. There was none for Peter. There is none for me.

The fire is where it starts. We would rather it started anywhere else.
