There is a particular kind of hunger that only afflicts people who work with food all day. The cook surrounded by meals he never sits down to eat. The pastor surrounded by Scripture he only ever prepares for someone else. I have stood in my study with an open Bible at ten on a Tuesday morning and realized, with a cold clarity, that I had not read a word of it for my own soul in weeks. I had read it for the sermon. I had read it for the funeral. I had not read it for the bread it is. That is a confession, and it is more common among us than we admit.

> The interior life is the part of a pastor that no one sees and everything depends on.

The interior life is the part of a pastor that no one sees and everything depends on. It is the man as he is when the building is empty, when no one is watching the prayer, when there is no sermon to harvest from the text. And it is precisely because no one sees it that it is the first thing to go. We are paid for the public man. We are evaluated on the public man. The interior man pays no bills and fills no pews, and so he gets fed last, which means in a busy season he does not get fed at all.

## The Objection of the Therapeutic Age

Let me grant the strongest objection before I go further, because it has weight. Some will say this whole emphasis on the pastor's inner world is a modern indulgence, a kind of spiritual navel-gazing that the apostles would not have recognized. Paul did not write about his interior life. He planted churches, took beatings, ran hard, and trusted God to sustain him in the running. The fixation on inner experience, the objection goes, is a symptom of a therapeutic age that has turned even prayer into a project of self-improvement, and a pastor would do better to obey God and stop monitoring his own soul like a man taking his temperature every hour.

There is truth there, and I want to honor it. A spirituality that is forever examining itself can curdle into a new kind of self-absorption, and the cure for an anxious pastor is not always more introspection. But the objection misreads its own apostle. Paul wrote that though our outer self is wasting away, our inner self is being renewed day by day (2 Corinthians 4:16 ESV). He had an interior life, and he tended it, and the running he did was fueled by a renewal that happened in the hidden man. The objection also forgets the Lord Himself, who, in the press of a ministry more urgent than any of ours, rose while it was still dark and went out to a desolate place and prayed (Mark 1:35). The most needed man in Israel withdrew from the need to be with His Father. If He required it, the notion that we have outgrown it is not maturity. It is presumption.

## The Desolate Place Before Dawn

So I will carry that text, Mark 1, through this. The scene is exact and it indicts me. Jesus has healed all evening, the whole town gathered at the door, and the next morning, very early, while it is still dark, He gets up and goes off alone to pray. Then the disciples find Him, almost scandalized, and say everyone is looking for you (Mark 1:37). There is the pressure every pastor knows. Everyone is looking for you. The work is waiting. The needs are stacking up at the door. And the Son of God had, the night before, chosen the desolate place over the waiting crowd, not because the crowd did not matter but because He could not be for the crowd what He needed to be without first being alone with the Father. He drew His public ministry out of His hidden communion. We try to run a public ministry on an empty interior, and then wonder at the hollowness in our own preaching.

I come at this as a man who began with no interior life at all that he would have called by that name. I was an atheist. The inner world I had was a closed room with no window toward heaven, and the great surprise of conversion was the window opening. But here is the danger I did not anticipate. A man can be converted and then slowly board that window back up, not with unbelief but with busyness, until the room is once again sealed, and the strange agony of a sealed-up Christian is that he remembers the light and cannot find it. I was raised without a father, and prayer, the practice of being a beloved child before a present Father, was the very muscle I had never developed and most needed. For years I prayed the way a stranger speaks to a landlord. It took a long time to learn to pray the way a son speaks to a father who is glad he came in.

## From Feeding on the Word to Processing It

The history of how pastors lost the interior life is partly a history of good intentions. The Protestant recovery of the Word rightly put preaching at the center, but over centuries the pastor's relationship to Scripture quietly shifted from the man who feeds on it to the man who processes it. Ruth Haley Barton describes the way leaders come to relate to spiritual things functionally, using prayer and Scripture as tools for ministry rather than as means of their own communion with God, until they are, in her phrase, leading on empty (Barton 2008). Dallas Willard spent his life arguing that the missing piece in evangelical ministry was a real apprenticeship to Jesus in the actual practices of the inner life, that we had reduced the gospel to a transaction about the afterlife and lost the present reality of life with God (Willard 1998). We trained men to explain the bread. We did not always teach them to eat it.

> The hidden life is not preparation for the real work. The hidden life is the real work, and the public ministry is its overflow.

Here is the reversal. The hidden life is not preparation for the real work. The hidden life is the real work, and the public ministry is its overflow. Eugene Peterson said the three pastoral acts of praying, reading Scripture, and giving spiritual direction are so unobtrusive that they are easy to skip, and yet they are the angles, the unseen lines, that determine whether the visible shape of the ministry is true or distorted (Peterson 1987). Skip the angles, and the visible structure looks fine for a while, then slowly goes crooked, and everyone can see the crookedness but no one can name the cause, because the cause is invisible. The cause is a man who stopped meeting God in private and is now performing a God he no longer knows.

There is a practical objection I should answer before I reach the cross, because busy men will raise it. When. When does a pastor whose week is already full of urgent need find the hours for a hidden life that produces nothing the church can see. I have asked it myself, usually in the tone of a man looking for permission to skip the thing he knows he needs. But notice that the question assumes the interior life is one more task to be fit into a full schedule, and that assumption is the disease. The interior life is not an item on the list. It is the soil out of which everything else on the list either grows healthy or grows sick. A farmer does not ask when he will find time to have soil. The soil is the precondition of the harvest, and the man who neglects it to spend more hours planting is not more diligent, he is farming dust. Jesus rose before dawn not because His calendar had a gap but because His public life was unsustainable without His hidden one, and so is yours, and so is mine.

## The Room Where the Text Reads Us

There is also a quieter reason we avoid the interior life, and it is worth naming because naming it is half the cure. The hidden room is where God deals with us about ourselves, and many of us would rather stay busy than be that exposed. As long as I am preparing a sermon I am safe, I am the one with the answers, I am the handler of the text and not its subject. The moment I sit in the dark before dawn with nothing to prepare and no one to instruct, the text turns and begins to read me, and what it finds is not always flattering. Avoidance of the interior life is often avoidance of self-knowledge before God, and a pastor can hide from his own soul behind the very Scriptures he expounds. I have done it. The flight into busyness can be, underneath, a flight from the God who wants to tell me the truth about myself, and that truth is the doorway to every mercy I most need.

## The Torn Veil and the Empty Hands

The cross, law first. The law here is sharp. To handle the holy things professionally while not communing with the Holy One personally is the sin of the man who serves at the altar and despises the One the altar is for. It is possible to preach Christ and not know Christ, to be, in the end, a tour guide pointing at a country he has never entered. I have stood closer to that man than I want to say. The grace second. The cross opened the way into the very presence of God, tore the veil from top to bottom, made the holiest place accessible to a man with bloody hands and a divided heart (Matthew 27:51). The invitation to commune is not earned by the quality of your interior life. It is purchased by Christ and offered to you in your emptiness. You do not have to assemble a worthy inner life before you come in. You come in empty, and the coming in is how it fills.

The pastor's most important sermon is the one no one will ever hear, preached in a dark room before dawn, to an audience of One.

Feed the hidden man. The visible one is starving in front of you.
