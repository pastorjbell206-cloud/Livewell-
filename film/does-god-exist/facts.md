## Claims, chapter by chapter

**A fair question**
- The case against is stronger than many believers admit; nothing here forces anyone to agree; reasons each open to inspection. Old film, frame "question" (older); ledger line 311; the Answer, opening paragraph and "The case against, at its strongest".

**Something rather than nothing**
- In 1714 Leibniz set down what he took to be the first question we have a right to ask. Old film, frame "something"; the Answer, "First: why is there anything at all?"
- Quotation "Why does something exist rather than nothing? For 'nothing' is simpler and easier than 'something.'": the page, "The evidence" (The Principles of Nature and of Grace, sections 7 and 8, Robert Latta's translation of 1898).
- The universe, all of space, time, matter and energy, could have been different, or not been at all: old film, frame "something"; the Answer's second premise; the page, argument step 2.

**A ball in the woods**
- Richard Taylor, Metaphysics (1963), a large translucent ball in the woods; no one would think it simply exists for no reason; making it the size of the universe "doesn't make the question go away. It makes it bigger." The Answer, "First: why is there anything at all?", third paragraph; ledger line 313.

**No beginning needed**
- The argument doesn't need a beginning: old film, frame "more-chain"; the Answer ("because it doesn't need one").
- Aquinas held that reason alone can't show the world began in time: the page, "The evidence"; the Answer.
- Alexander Pruss's endless line of hens and eggs, each hen explained by an egg and each egg by a hen, leaving untouched why there is such a line rather than none: the page, "The evidence", second paragraph.
- "A longer chain is still a chain that needs explaining": old film, frame "more-chain" (younger caption), and ledger line 313 (from the Who made God? film's older frame 3).

**The argument, in three steps**
- Condensed from the page, "The argument, laid out" (steps 1 to 3 and conclusion), and the Answer's two premises and conclusion. The film folds steps 2 and 3 into a short "two" and states the conclusion as "three".

**The case against**
- David Hume, Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, published in 1779 after his death; one speaker objects, "why may not the material universe be the necessarily existent Being": the page, "The objection at its strongest" (the page names the speaker as Cleanthes; the film says only "one speaker").
- 1948, BBC radio debate with the Jesuit Frederick Copleston; Russell: "I should say that the universe is just there, and that's all.": the page, same section (the Answer and old film quote the shorter "just there, and that's all").
- The fallacy of composition, like arguing a wall must be small because each brick is: the page, same section.

**What is true in it**
- "Much of it"; the principle that whatever exists has an explanation can't be proved from anything more certain than itself: the page, "What is true in it".
- Lawrence Krauss, A Universe from Nothing (2012), argued physical law can account for a universe coming to be without a creator: the page, "The objection at its strongest", and its "claims" list.
- If the argument claimed everything has a cause, Russell's reply that God would need a cause too would end it; that question has its own page (here, its own film, the sibling who-made-god): the page, "What is true in it", third paragraph.

**Weigh the replies**
- If every constituent of the universe might not have existed, it is hard to see what would make their sum incapable of not existing (turned into a question for the viewer): the page, "The evidence", second paragraph.
- Not every inference from parts to whole commits the fallacy; a wall made entirely of brick is a brick wall: the page, same paragraph.
- The "nothing" in the universe-from-nothing proposals is a state described by quantum laws, as the philosopher David Albert objected in reviewing Krauss's book (New York Times, March 2012); such a universe is still a universe that might not have been: the page, "The evidence", last paragraph.

**Where it stops**
- The strongest reply is parity; every account of reality stops somewhere; the atheist may stop at the universe or its most basic laws and is no more irrational for doing so: the page, "The strongest reply, and an answer".
- The answer: the demand for a reason applies to what might not have been; whether there can be a being whose existence is not received from anything is the real crux, and philosophers divide over it: the page, same section, second paragraph.
- "Where do you think explanations stop, and why?": the old film's "think" prompt (older).

**Not yet Christianity**
- If sound, the argument shows a necessary being that explains the universe: the page, same section, third paragraph.
- A large part of what believers in one God mean by the word: old film, frame "what-god-means"; the Answer's conclusion.
- Not a proof in the mathematician's sense; not yet Christianity; does not show God is three persons or that he became a man: old film, frame "shows"; the Answer, "What kind of case this is" and "What this does not prove"; ledger line 316.
- Paul in Athens began with Acts 17:24 and went straight to the resurrection, a historical question: the Answer, "What this does not prove"; the page, last paragraph of "The strongest reply".

**Weigh it for yourself**
- Christians differ on how much these arguments can carry; some, following Aquinas, hold reason can show God exists; others hold belief can be warranted without such arguments: the page, "Where Christians differ".
- Russell's step is where an honest person can disagree; you can weigh all of it and still say no; what you can't honestly say is that there's nothing to weigh: old film, frame "weigh"; the Answer; ledger line 317.
- Title card, "The universe is still there, asking why.": adapted from the Answer's last sentence ("the universe will still be there, asking why").
