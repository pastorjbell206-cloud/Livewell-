Why would anyone walk away from everything to live in a desert?

Many early Christians did. Their movement changed the church as surely as any council.

Around 270, in a village church in Egypt, a young farmer named Antony heard the Gospel read, the story of a rich young man.

Jesus told him, “If you want to be perfect, go, sell your possessions and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. Then come, follow Me.”

Antony took the words as spoken to him. He sold his land, provided for his sister, and walked past the edge of the fields.

He went while the faith was still illegal, a generation before Constantine.

But after 313, when believing stopped costing anything, the desert filled with people looking for the cost themselves.

Not every motive was pure. Debtors and men dodging taxes or the army went too, as the monks' own sources admit.

Antony shut himself inside an abandoned fort near the Nile for twenty years. Friends passed him bread over the wall.

Around 305 they broke down the door, expecting a wreck, and found a man sane, gentle, and strangely whole.

Crowds tracked him down for counsel until he died in 356.

Athanasius, who knew him, wrote his life. Later, in a garden in Milan, its story helped push a professor named Augustine into faith.

But a man alone with God is also alone with himself, and some went strange.

Around 320 an ex-soldier named Pachomius gathered monks into walled communities along the Nile, with common meals, work, prayer and a written rule.

Basil of Caesarea carried it into the Greek church and turned it toward the neighbor. A monk who flees everyone, he asked, whose feet will he wash?

The desert had its mothers too, like Syncletica.

There were excesses. In Syria, Simeon lived some thirty-six years on a pillar, and people asked where devotion ended and performance began.

At worst, mobs of monks became muscle in church politics.

Yet the monks were their own sharpest critics.

Called to judge an erring brother, Abba Moses, once a bandit chief, came with a basket that leaked sand. His own sins, he said, ran out behind him unseen, and he had come to judge another man.

The enemy was never the body. It was pride.

The desert's wisdom reached the West as the West came apart.

Around 529, at Monte Cassino, Benedict of Nursia wrote a rule for monks short enough to read in an evening.

He hoped to set down nothing harsh, nothing burdensome. The day was a braid of psalms, work and reading.

Guests were received as Christ. It was a rule for beginners, and that's why it lasted.

When the Western order failed, monasteries were among the few places left that could read.

They didn't save civilization single-handed. What they kept alive was rarer, proof that the commands of Jesus can be a daily schedule.

Christians still weigh the monks differently.

Orthodox Christians honor their monasteries as houses of prayer and counsel for all, and their monks keep rules that carry Basil's stamp.

Catholics honor the vowed life as a calling God gives some for the good of all, and many of their houses keep Benedict's Rule.

Protestants, following the Reformers, hold that every calling is holy, the farmer's and the mother's as much as the monk's.

Most of us will never keep vigils or a rule of silence, and the monks don't ask it.

But they still stand at the edge of a comfortable church, asking the question Antony heard.

What would you sell?
