How did Christianity move south and east in a single century?

In 1900, better than four of every five Christians lived in Europe or North America.

No one at the great mission conference of 1910 saw it coming.

First, American Protestants split down the middle over whether the faith could survive modern knowledge.

The modernists believed it would die among educated people unless it made peace with Darwin and the historians.

The fundamentalists answered that a faith adjusted to fit each new consensus was not being saved but dissolved.

Each side accused the other of losing the actual faith. The argument never ended.

Then came the dictators, demanding the worship owed to Christ.

When Hitler took power in 1933, most German Protestants welcomed him.

In May of 1934, a minority met at Barmen and confessed Jesus Christ as the one Word of God.

It was brave. It said nothing directly about the persecution of the Jews.

In 1945 the church's surviving leaders confessed they hadn't been courageous enough. It came too late for six million people.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote that cheap grace is the deadly enemy of the church, grace without discipleship, grace without the cross.

In 1939 he was safe in New York, and sailed home within the month.

He was arrested in 1943 and executed at Flossenburg in April of 1945, weeks before the war's end.

He was the German church's exception, and so its accusation.

What Germany did to the church in a decade, the Soviet Union attempted for seventy years.

Of the tens of thousands of parishes open before the revolution, only a few hundred still functioned legally by 1939.

When China's churches reopened after 1979, the church hadn't died underground. It had multiplied, into the tens of millions by every serious estimate.

By many reckonings, more Christians died for their faith in that century than in any before it.

Back in 1906, a revival broke out in a former stable on Azusa Street in Los Angeles, under William Seymour, the son of freed slaves.

Black, white and immigrant believers worshiped together. From that room, Pentecostalism spread around the world.

The South was stirring too: Korea from 1907, the Congo from 1921, East Africa from the 1930s.

After the Second Vatican Council put worship into the world's languages, Catholicism's heart moved south as well.

At Lausanne in 1974, Latin American theologians told Western evangelicals that discipleship could not be separated from justice.

Christians still weigh that call differently.

Some hold that evangelism and social action are two sides of one witness, not tasks to be ranked.

Others hold that evangelism is primary, and that works of mercy flow from the gospel and serve it.

Sometime around 1980 the balance tipped. For the first time since the Middle Ages, most Christians were not Europeans or North Americans.

Perhaps ten million Africans were Christians in 1900. By the century's end, the standard estimates said more than three hundred million.

The southern churches aren't the church's future perfected. They are simply the church.

The historian Andrew Walls saw that Christianity has no permanent capital. Each center in turn becomes a mission field.

The Western church isn't the story's owner. It is one more province of a kingdom that keeps its capital elsewhere.

In Revelation, John writes what he saw.

After this I looked and saw a multitude too large to count, from every nation and tribe and people and tongue, standing before the throne and before the Lamb. They were wearing white robes and holding palm branches in their hands.
