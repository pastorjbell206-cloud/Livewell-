After Luther, what did the Catholic Church do? And how did Christians end up at war with each other?

The answer holds a real renewal and terrible wars, and they belong to one story.

The Catholic Church answered at the Council of Trent, which met from 1545 to 1563 on the Alpine edge of Italy.

It taught that justification comes by grace but not by faith alone, and that Scripture and the church's unwritten traditions are received with equal reverence.

At last, both sides knew exactly where they differed.

Trent also cleaned house. Bishops had to live in their dioceses and preach, and every diocese was to build a seminary to train its priests.

The pardon sellers were abolished. It took decades, but the church came out more disciplined, more educated and more serious.

The renewal had faces. Ignatius of Loyola, a Basque soldier wounded at Pamplona in 1521, founded the Society of Jesus, approved in 1540.

His companion Francis Xavier reached India and Japan, and died in 1552 within sight of China.

In Spain, Teresa of Avila and John of the Cross reformed the Carmelites and wrote some of the deepest Christian books on prayer ever written.

In Milan, Charles Borromeo served his plague-struck city in person. It was holiness, and any tradition should be glad to claim it.

The same renewal had a fist. The Roman Inquisition and the Index of Forbidden Books silenced honest questions.

Behind both stood one conviction, held on every side: that the truth of God should be secured by the power of the state.

Catholics believed it, and so did Lutherans and the Reformed. Almost no one doubted it but the hunted Anabaptists.

On Saint Bartholomew's Day in 1572, the leaders of France's Reformed minority, the Huguenots, were murdered in Paris, and thousands died.

In the Low Countries, Spain's Council of Troubles executed more than a thousand people. Protestant England executed Catholic priests as traitors.

The parties differed over grace and the sacraments. Over the sword they hardly differed at all.

The Thirty Years War began in Prague in 1618, when Protestant nobles threw two imperial officials out of a castle window. The men survived.

In 1631 imperial troops stormed the Protestant city of Magdeburg, and most of its people died.

In its later years, Catholic France was paying for Lutheran Sweden's war against the Catholic emperor.

With plague and famine, the German lands lost about a fifth of their people, by one historian's estimate.

The Peace of Westphalia came in 1648, and nobody had won. Each ruler would still set his land's religion.

It was not toleration, but a truce between exhausted certainties.

Christendom, the dream of Europe as one Christian house, did not survive.

Christians still read this century differently.

Catholics see a church renewing itself from within, teaching that grace comes first and that no one can earn it.

Protestants see Trent rejecting what the reformers had recovered: grace received by faith alone, and Scripture as the final word.

Both look back on the same record.

The wars were fought by baptized men, sure God was with them. And modern European unbelief was born, in no small part, from watching them.

When a skeptic says Christianity means violence, this is the century they remember.

The answer has never been denial. It is grief, and the long labor of being a different kind of church.

Jesus had said it long before the wars began.

Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called sons of God.
