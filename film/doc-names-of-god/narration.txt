Read the Old Testament and you meet God under many names. God Most High. God Almighty. The LORD.

So which one is His real name? And why would a name matter at all?

In the Bible, a name is more than a label. It says who someone is, and gives others a way to call on them.

The Hebrew word is shem. It means name, and it can also mean reputation.

So when God reveals His names, He's making Himself known. And He's making Himself available.

Genesis first calls God Elohim, the Creator. It's the ordinary word for God.

Then come titles, often tied to a place or an event. Melchizedek blesses Abram by God Most High.

Hagar, a runaway servant in the wilderness, names Him the God who sees me. To Abram, He's God Almighty, El Shaddai.

These aren't a menu of separate powers, one for provision and another for healing. Each names the one God.

Then comes the burning bush. Moses asks what name to give the Israelites.

God answers, I AM WHO I AM. Then He names Himself the LORD, and says, This is My name forever.

This is God's personal name, used nearly sixty-eight hundred times. Most English Bibles print it as LORD, in small capitals.

The name is tied to the Hebrew verb to be. God's answer echoes His promise to Moses, I will surely be with you.

At Sinai, the LORD proclaims His name and then describes it. Compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion and faithfulness.

God's name is filled with His character. To speak of His name is often to speak of God Himself.

Out of reverence, Jewish practice in time stopped speaking that name aloud.

Scribes marked it so readers would say Lord instead. Later Jewish usage calls it HaShem, the Name.

The Greek Old Testament wrote kyrios, Lord. The New Testament followed it, and the church inherited the practice.

The New Testament opens by naming a child. Jesus is the Greek form of the Hebrew Yeshua, which means the LORD saves.

The first Christians confessed, Jesus is Lord. Their Greek Bible used Lord for God's name, so the words carried its sound.

Paul backs it with the prophet Joel. Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved.

In Joel, that Lord is God's personal name. In Paul's letter, it's Jesus.

Back at the bush, the name spoke of God's self-existence and His faithful presence. Christians weigh the two differently.

Some hear self-existence first. God simply is and depends on nothing, and His presence with His people follows. Many in the classical tradition read it this way.

Others hear a promise first, I will be who I will be. His self-existence is implied, but presence is the point. Many Old Testament scholars read it this way.

Because God has a name, He can be called on. Prayer isn't sent to an unknown force. He has introduced Himself.

Jesus taught His disciples to begin by asking that God's name be honored, before bread or forgiveness. And the third commandment guards it.

You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain, for the LORD will not leave anyone unpunished who takes His name in vain.

That's about far more than careless speech. It forbids using God's name to back a false oath, a cruelty, a party, or a nation's cause.

The Bible ends with that name on God's people.

They will see His face, and His name will be on their foreheads.

To bear His name is to belong to Him.
