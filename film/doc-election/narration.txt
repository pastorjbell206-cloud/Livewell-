The Bible keeps saying God chose. Abraham, Israel, David, the apostles.

So people ask. Why them? What does it mean for me?

The church calls it election, God's free choosing.

He chooses people for tasks, a people for Himself, and above all the Servant He calls His Chosen One.

It rests on God's love, not the merit of the chosen. And it's always for something.

It starts with Abraham. God promises that through him all the families of the earth will be blessed.

The one is chosen for the sake of the many.

God keeps choosing the overlooked, like David, the youngest, out with the sheep.

Why them? Moses gives a plain answer.

The LORD did not set His affection on you and choose you because you were more numerous than the other peoples, for you were the fewest of all peoples.

It was because the Lord loved them.

In the New Testament, the Chosen One is Jesus. At the transfiguration the Father calls Him His chosen Son.

Jesus chose the Twelve to go and bear fruit.

Paul says believers were chosen in Him before the foundation of the world, to be holy.

How God's choosing meets human faith became one of the church's longest debates.

Many Greek fathers explained it by God's foreknowledge of how people would respond.

Augustine taught that faith itself is God's gift, so election can't rest on it.

Five readings stand side by side.

Augustinian and Reformed Christians hold that God chose particular people for salvation out of sheer grace, not foreseen faith, and gives them faith.

Otherwise, they argue, the final difference between saved and lost would lie in us, and grace would no longer be grace.

Arminian and Wesleyan Christians hold that God elects people as He foreknows their faith, which His grace makes possible for all but never forces.

Unconditional election, they argue, can't be squared with God's love for everyone, or the responsibility of those who refuse Him.

Others teach corporate election. God first chose a people, as with Israel, and individuals share in it by union with Christ through faith.

They argue this follows the Bible's own categories more closely than debates over individual decrees, and keeps election tied to mission.

Lutherans hold that God's election in Christ is the sole cause of salvation, but that He has predestined no one to damnation.

Grace alone saves and human guilt alone condemns, they argue, and any system explaining the difference goes beyond what God has revealed.

Those who follow Karl Barth hold that Jesus Christ is both the electing God and the elected human being.

In Him God chose humanity for fellowship, and chose for Himself the rejection sinners deserve. That, they argue, turns election from fear into good news.

Beneath the differences lies common ground. Every tradition confesses that salvation begins with grace.

The church rejects any teaching that God predestines people to sin, and any fatalism that makes preaching pointless.

All of them offer the gospel freely and call everyone to repent.

Election tells believers their standing began in God's love, not their achievement, so it can't be ended by their failure to earn it.

It leaves no room for boasting. The chosen were often the youngest, the weakest, the least likely.

Paul's most debated chapters on election end not with a solution, but with praise.

O, the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are His judgments, and untraceable His ways!

Christians who disagree about how election works can agree on this. The chosen are chosen for the sake of the world.
