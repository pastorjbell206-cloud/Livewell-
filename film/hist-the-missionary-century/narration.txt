Were the missionaries of the 1800s heroes, or agents of empire?

In 1790 the Christian map was mostly Europe, with old and honorable exceptions.

But Protestants, born claiming the Bible for everyone, had sent almost no one.

In 1792 a Baptist shoemaker, William Carey, argued that Christians must use means, not just wait on God.

Ministers at Kettering formed a missionary society, and Carey sailed for India, never to come home.

He lost a child and waited seven years for a first convert. By his death in 1834, his mission had printed Scripture in over forty languages.

In Burma, Ann Judson kept her husband Adoniram alive in prison so he could finish the Burmese Bible.

She died in 1826, one of thousands of missionary women whose graves mark the century.

In 1865 Hudson Taylor founded the China Inland Mission. Its people wore Chinese dress and went far up-country.

David Livingstone preached Christianity, commerce and civilization as one package. The last two would cost the first dearly.

Treaties ending the Opium Wars forced China open, and the faith arrived beside a drug most missionaries despised.

Many shared the era's racial contempt, and treated European customs as Christian requirements.

In 1900 the Boxer Rising killed thousands of Chinese Christians, and scores of missionary families, as agents of foreign power.

Yet missionaries like William Knibb of Jamaica helped end slavery across the British Empire.

And they translated. Scripture in a people's own tongue said God spoke their language and had no favorite race.

Converts read Exodus, where God spoke these words.

“I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery.

The empires shipped a book that condemned them.

The word missionary calls up a white face. Yet local believers did most of the evangelizing.

African catechists walked bush paths, and Chinese Bible women went house to house. Pacific islander teachers went island to island.

The histories made them scenery. They were the engine.

In India, Pandita Ramabai rescued widows and orphans and translated Scripture into Marathi.

Samuel Ajayi Crowther, a Yoruba boy seized by slave raiders in 1821, was freed at sea.

He led the translation of the Yoruba Bible, and in 1864 became the first African Anglican bishop.

Race-proud younger Englishmen dismantled his work and humiliated him until he died in 1891. His successor was white.

It took most of a century to unlearn the lesson that Africans could be converts but never leaders.

In 1910 more than twelve hundred delegates met at Edinburgh's World Missionary Conference, full of confidence.

Fewer than twenty came from Asia's younger churches, and almost none from Africa.

V. S. Azariah of India thanked the missionaries for their sacrifices, then asked for what they'd rarely given.

Not money, not workers. Friends.

Within sixty years the empires were gone, and the young churches outgrew the ones that sent them.

The missions preached, taught and healed. Christians still differ on how word and deed fit together.

Catholic teaching joins proclaiming Christ to works of charity, and says true evangelization must proclaim Jesus.

Orthodox churches see the liturgy as the heart of witness, meant to overflow into the world.

Many Protestants put preaching first, mercy flowing from it. Others call word and deed one witness.

So hold two truths together. The gospel really reached the whole world, at great cost, sped by empires it didn't control.

The peoples who received it sorted the treasure from the packaging better than the senders did.

God used the ships without blessing the cargo. He'd done it before, with Roman roads.
