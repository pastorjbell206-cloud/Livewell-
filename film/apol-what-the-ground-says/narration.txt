Does archaeology prove the Bible? Some say every dig confirms it.

Others say the spade has come up empty. No trace of the exodus, a Jericho without walls, little sign of Solomon's great kingdom.

The honest answer is neither.

Start with what didn't hold up.

For years, a shaft in Jerusalem was linked to the way David's men took the city. Later digging suggests it wasn't reachable in David's time.

Gates once credited to Solomon are now dated by some archaeologists to the century after him.

And for the exodus and the conquest, the ground is mostly silent.

But from the ninth century before Christ, Israel's neighbors and enemies start writing things down.

In 1993, at Tel Dan, diggers found pieces of an Aramaic victory stone. A king of Aram boasts of killing a king of the house of David.

Most scholars take it as the earliest known mention of David's dynasty by name, more than a century after him.

In 1868, in Moab, a black stone was found. King Mesha records how Omri, king of Israel, had oppressed his land, and how he broke free.

Second Kings tells it from Israel's side.

But after the death of Ahab, the king of Moab rebelled against the king of Israel.

The two accounts don't fit together in every detail. They agree on the main fact.

Then Assyria. Sennacherib invaded Judah, and the Bible says Hezekiah paid him thirty talents of gold.

Sennacherib's own annals boast that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem like a bird in a cage. They give the same thirty talents of gold. The silver differs.

And notice what the annals never claim. They never say the city was taken.

Hezekiah also cut a tunnel to bring water into the city.

It still runs under Jerusalem. An inscription found inside it in 1880 tells how the diggers, working toward each other, struck pick against pick, and the water flowed.

That's what independent sources look like. They agree on the main facts, differ in the details, and each tells the story from its own side.

Where the writers of Kings can be checked, they behave like historians of a kind.

But a stone can't show that God spoke to Moses. Inscriptions confirm names and events, not meanings.

And silence cuts both ways. It can't prove a great kingdom under Solomon, and it can't prove a small one.

Claims of proof and claims of disproof both say more than archaeology can.

The faith doesn't rest on a spade. It rests on what God did in history, above all the resurrection.

Archaeology shows the Old Testament's world was real. It was never going to be the ground of faith, and it hasn't taken it away.
