Here's another objection. The Gospels were written decades after Jesus, by people who never met Him, under names someone added later.

If that's true, why trust what they say?

Again, start with what's true.

Jesus was crucified around the year thirty. Most scholars date Mark around seventy, Matthew and Luke later, and John in the nineties.

That's forty to sixty-five years. Some able scholars argue for earlier dates, but that's a minority view, and it has to be argued, not assumed.

And none of the four Gospels names its author in its own text.

But think about what a gap like that is. It sits inside a single lifetime.

People who had known Jesus were still alive. Many more had known them.

And we have writing from inside the gap. Paul's letters, from the fifties.

In one of them, Paul passes on a summary he says he himself received.

For what I received I passed on to you as of first importance: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that He was buried, that He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures,

Most scholars, including many with no stake in Christianity, think that summary took shape within a few years of the cross.

And Paul says he stayed fifteen days with Peter in Jerusalem, and later met James and John.

Historians of the ancient world work with far bigger gaps than this.

One of our main accounts of Alexander the Great's campaigns, by Arrian, was written more than four centuries after them. Historians still use it, carefully.

So the question isn't only how long the gap was. It's what stood inside it.

Now the names. It's true the titles, According to Matthew and the rest, were almost certainly added by others.

But anonymous isn't the same as unknown. Every early copy that keeps a title gives the same four names. No early copy gives another.

If churches across the Roman world had guessed, you'd expect a Gospel of Andrew in one place and of Philip in another. We find none.

And look at two of the names. Mark and Luke weren't among the Twelve. Mark is remembered as the man who turned back partway through Paul's first mission.

When people did write gospels under famous names, they chose apostles, like Peter. Mark and Luke look less like a grab for authority than a memory nobody was free to improve.

Early in the second century, a writer named Papias passed on what an older teacher told him: that Mark had been Peter's interpreter, and wrote down what Peter remembered.

And Luke never claims to have seen Jesus. He says he carefully investigated what the eyewitnesses handed down. That's just what the church always said of him.

A skeptic can answer that witnesses alive somewhere don't help unless the writers used them. And memory is fragile.

That's fair. Memory bends, and a witness interprets as well as remembers.

But this story was told in churches where Peter, James and John had preached, among people who had heard them. It wasn't free to drift wherever anyone wished.

Some things stay open. Whether the apostle Matthew wrote the Gospel that bears his name, or stands behind it. Who the beloved disciple was. Exactly when Mark wrote.

Faithful Christians differ on those, and honest scholars live with them.

What the church confesses doesn't hang on a decade. It hangs on whether these books tell the truth about a man crucified under Pontius Pilate and raised on the third day.

And the earliest witness to that claim is older than any of the four.
