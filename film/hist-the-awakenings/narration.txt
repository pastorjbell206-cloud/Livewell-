Where did evangelicals come from?

Much of the answer lies in about sixty years, beginning in the 1730s.

When the religious wars burned out, much of Protestant Europe settled into a faith that was correct and cold.

The educated preferred a distant God who built the clock, wound it, and would never demand anything so indecent as a new birth.

But Jesus had demanded exactly that.

Jesus replied, “Truly, truly, I tell you, no one can see the kingdom of God unless he is born again.”

Later believers called that age a sleep. The waking began in Germany with Pietism, whose small groups read Scripture, prayed, and held one another to holiness.

At Herrnhut, Moravian refugees kept a prayer watch around the clock for decades, and from 1732 they sent missionaries.

In 1735 John Wesley, a young Anglican priest, sailed for Georgia with some of them.

In a storm, as the English passengers screamed, the Moravians sang psalms. Wesley found he was afraid to die.

In 1738, on Aldersgate Street, his heart was strangely warmed, as his journal puts it.

Across the ocean, in the winter of 1734, Jonathan Edwards's town of Northampton, Massachusetts, began to be surprised.

Conversions multiplied until the revival was the only subject in the streets.

Those it touched called it an outpouring of the Spirit. Its critics called it enthusiasm.

George Whitefield could be heard. In 1739 he preached in the open air to Kingswood's coal miners.

When he toured New England in 1740, the scattered fires became the Great Awakening. The skeptic Benjamin Franklin reckoned thirty thousand people could hear him at once.

Wesley had order. He gathered the awakened into societies and weekly classes, and his brother Charles wrote hymns by the thousand.

Edwards tested it all. True religion, he wrote in 1746, is a changed heart that shows itself in a changed life.

Whitefield the Calvinist and Wesley the Arminian split over predestination.

Calvinists hold that God freely chose particular people for salvation, out of sheer grace rather than foreseen faith, and gives them faith.

Arminians hold that God chose to save all who believe, and His grace makes faith possible for everyone without forcing it.

The two men patched their friendship, and Wesley preached Whitefield's memorial sermon.

The awakeners preached the new birth in a world built on slavery, and most of them made their peace with it.

Whitefield campaigned to make slavery legal in Georgia, then bought human beings and worked them. He died owning around fifty people.

Edwards owned enslaved people too, and defended the practice in writing, though he condemned the ocean trade.

The revival's clearest sight came with a blind spot the size of a person.

But the gospel escaped its preachers. The enslaved heard of the new birth and took it for themselves, and the African American church began.

George Liele, ordained in 1775, planted churches among the enslaved around Savannah and carried the gospel to Jamaica.

Olaudah Equiano, once enslaved, published his story in 1789, and it helped turn a nation against the trade.

Wesley attacked slavery in 1774, and his last letter urged William Wilberforce to press on against the trade.

Converted at sea in 1748, John Newton went on captaining slave ships for six more years.

As a pastor he wrote the hymn about amazing grace, and in 1788 he confessed his part in the trade.

That's the awakening in one man. Real grace, really received, beside a monstrous blindness, and then, slowly, sight.

What was born in those years was alive. But alive isn't the same as whole, and the church is still learning the difference.
