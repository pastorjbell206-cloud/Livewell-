## For James

**What the film leaves out on purpose.** From the definition: the Sabbath and Jubilee years by name, the land as Israel's rest, and the Greek and Hebrew word study beyond shabbat (nuach, menuchah, shabbaton, sabbaton, katapausis, sabbatismos). From the Old Testament: the Babylonian shapattu theory, the sign of Exodus 31, the death penalty and the man stoned for gathering wood (Numbers 15), the tabernacle framing, the thirty-nine kinds of labor, the land's Sabbaths and the exile, the prophets (Amos, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Nehemiah), the Maccabees and Qumran. From the New Testament: Nazareth and the Jubilee, the grain fields and David's bread, the healings in Mark 3 and John 5, the women resting "according to the commandment", Troas, 1 Corinthians 16:2, Revelation 1:10, and Hebrews 4 by name. Colossians 2, Romans 14 and Galatians 4 are kept out of the story chapters because the views read them differently. Romans 14:5 appears only inside the fulfillment view, as its holders' reason. From history: the Didache, Justin, Barnabas, Constantine's law of 321, Laodicea, Augustine by name, Aquinas, the Reformers one by one, Bownd, the Book of Sports, Mumford and Newport, Rachel Oakes Preston and Joseph Bates, Heschel by name, Bacchiocchi, Carson, Dies Domini, Dawn, Brueggemann and the four-views book. Also left out: the Adventist teaching that the Sabbath is a sign of loyalty in the last days, the Christian Sabbath's rest from recreation, the fulfillment view's "regular rest and worship remain wise and good gifts", and the Orthodox Sunday Liturgy. Each would have broken the equal length of the four views.

**"Fourth commandment."** The file says "the fourth commandment". The film says "it entered the Ten Commandments" and the tablets carry no numbers, because Catholic and Lutheran numbering count the Sabbath command as the third. Saying "fourth" would have used one side's count. Change it back if you prefer the file's wording.

**Views named without choosing.** The film names four views in the file's order: the seventh-day Sabbath (Seventh Day Baptists, Seventh-day Adventists), the Christian Sabbath (the Westminster Confession and the Baptist confession of 1689), the Sabbath fulfilled in Christ (Lutherans and many evangelicals), and the Lord's Day as the Sabbath's fulfillment (Catholic). Each gets one spoken line of exactly 30 words, framed the same way: who holds it, then their own reasons. Each gets an identical panel: the same frame, book, seven dots, type sizes and ink. No panel uses mustard. Each panel is full while it is described and all four end equal. The film ends the section on what all four share: one cross above the panels and the line that every tradition rejects keeping a day to earn standing before God. The life section opens with "Whatever view a Christian holds". Judaism is spoken of three times, each with respect. Jewish reckoning gives the day its boundaries, Jesus kept the day "as a faithful Jew", and "Jewish teachers also held that saving life overrides the Sabbath".

**The two verses.** Exodus 20:8 is quoted as the command Israel received, a history all four views share. Matthew 11:28 is the file's own `life` line about rest for the weary. It is also one of the texts the fulfillment view cites. The film uses it as rest offered to tired people, which all four views confess, and puts the closing line on the seventh day with no evening, the hope the `life` section gives every tradition. Neither verse appears in any earlier film.

**In the picture.** God and Christ are never drawn. A crown over the seventh day, an empty tomb at dawn, a cross and a light stand for them. People are faceless stick figures. The woman's eighteen years are shown only as a bent figure who stands straight. Mustard is kept to the opening question mark, the two small sunsets, the glow and ribbon on the seventh day, the dawn sun and the closing light.

**For you to check.** "Some just mean a day off" is the film's framing, not a sentence from the file. "Not a place. A day." rests on the file's "first thing in Scripture called holy" and Heschel's sanctuary in time, without naming Heschel. The closing card, "The work can wait. God is still God.", restates "the work is left unfinished" in gentler words. The narration runs 598 words (3,349 characters), at the top of the 480 to 600 range.

**Names to listen for.** shabbat (shah-BAHT), Pharaoh, Sinai (SY-nye), Deuteronomy, Ignatius of Antioch (ig-NAY-shus, AN-tee-ock), Seventh Day Baptists, Seventh-day Adventists, Westminster, "1689" (sixteen eighty-nine), Lutherans, evangelicals, Eucharist (YOO-kuh-rist).
