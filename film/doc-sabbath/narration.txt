People talk about keeping a Sabbath. Some mean Saturday. Some mean Sunday. Some just mean a day off.

So what is the Sabbath? And what does it ask of Christians now?

The Sabbath is the seventh day, which God set apart as holy and gave to Israel as a day of rest.

In Jewish reckoning it runs from sunset Friday to sunset Saturday.

The Hebrew word, shabbat, is related to a verb meaning to stop, more than to relax.

On the seventh day, God ceased from His work, then blessed the day and made it holy.

It's the first thing in Scripture called holy. Not a place. A day.

And it's the only day of creation without an evening and morning.

Israel received the Sabbath as a gift before it was a law.

In the wilderness, God sent twice the manna on the sixth day, and none on the seventh.

People who had made bricks without rest for Pharaoh learned to stop, because God provides.

At Sinai it entered the Ten Commandments, the longest of the ten.

Remember the Sabbath day by keeping it holy.

Exodus grounds it in creation. Deuteronomy grounds it in the rescue from slavery, so servants could rest as their masters did.

The rest reached sons and daughters, foreigners, even the animals.

Jesus kept the Sabbath as a faithful Jew. Yet the Gospels record more arguments over it than over any other part of the law.

He freed a woman bent over for eighteen years, and treated the Sabbath as the most fitting day for release.

Jewish teachers also held that saving life overrides the Sabbath.

What set Jesus apart was His authority. He called Himself Lord even of the Sabbath.

Jesus lay in the tomb over the Sabbath, and rose very early on the first day of the week.

Around the year 110, Ignatius of Antioch wrote of believers living by the Lord's day, the day on which life rose.

For centuries, some Christians, especially in the East, kept honoring Saturday alongside Sunday.

Christians agree that the Sabbath points to Christ. They differ on how the commandment binds them now.

Seventh Day Baptists and Seventh-day Adventists keep the seventh day. It was made at creation for everyone, it's in the Ten Commandments, and no text moves it to another day.

The Westminster Confession and the Baptist confession of 1689 teach a Christian Sabbath. One day in seven is lasting moral law, and the resurrection moved it to the first day.

Lutherans and many evangelicals hold the Sabbath is fulfilled in Christ. It was a sign of the Sinai covenant, Christ is the believer's rest, and days are left to conscience.

Catholic teaching holds that Sunday, the resurrection day and eighth day of new creation, fulfills the Sabbath. The faithful gather for the Eucharist and rest from work that hinders worship.

Every one of these traditions rejects keeping a day to earn standing before God.

Whatever view a Christian holds, the Sabbath tells tired people something plain. You're not a machine. The world doesn't depend on your work.

Stopping is an act of trust. The work is left unfinished, and God is still God.

It's also an act of justice. It asks how your week treats those who work for you, sell to you and serve you.

The deepest rest is the one Jesus offers to the weary.

Come to Me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest.

The church has long read that seventh day with no evening as a promise of rest in God without end.
