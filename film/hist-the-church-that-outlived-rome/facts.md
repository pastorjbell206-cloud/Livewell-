## For James

**Left out on purpose.** The Goths as Arian Christians who spared the churches; Jerome's line that the city which took the world had been taken; Augustine's thirteen years on the City of God (cut for length; easy to restore as one line after Jerome); Patrick's "prayer upon prayer in the sleet" and his Confession calling him "the least of the faithful"; Aidan, Lindisfarne and Northumbria in 635; Clotilda's name (the film says "his Christian queen") and the battlefield bargain; Gregory of Tours by name ("a chronicler"); Kent's king Ethelbert and his Frankish queen; Bede's "not Angles but angels" story; the lesson's balance on Gregory's letter, that it holds "both a deep pastoral patience and the seed of every later compromise" (cut for length; the kings chapter keeps "shallow, and it deepened", but you may want this line back); Boniface's death scene (named only, "was killed"); the envoys in Hagia Sophia; Bulgaria and the Cyrillic alphabet by name; Denmark and Norway by name (they light on the map only); "we do not get to place that history outside the family"; "including ours" and "we are slow students". The lesson's sources (Gonzalez, Fletcher, Brown, MacCulloch, Shelley) aren't named.

**Views named without choosing.**
- *How each tradition carries the work these missions began.* This lesson gives no Catholic, Orthodox or Protestant reading of the era. I looked through the other history lessons (the-long-middle, the-medieval-west, the-missionary-century, the-east-west-schism and the rest) and found no fair line for each tradition about these missions, so I said less than they could and took all three lines from one LiveWell page, the doctrine study of mission, which sets out how Christians differ. The Catholic and Orthodox lines are that page's own "Roman Catholic and Orthodox" view, near word for word; the Orthodox line is the only one that names this era (Cyril and Methodius). The Protestant line is that page's "evangelism as the church's primary task" view, which the page ties to evangelicals at Lausanne; the film says "many evangelical Protestants" because the same page shows other Protestants holding other views. The three lines are 24, 24 and 23 words. In the picture the three churches are the same drawing, the same size and the same ink; each is darkened only while its own line is spoken, and all three end dark together. If you'd rather keep strictly to the history lessons, cut the chapter "Carrying it on"; the film falls to about 516 words.
- *How deep the conversions went.* The lesson says historians argue and both sides hold evidence; the film keeps its verdict, "shallow, and it deepened". In the picture most of the warriors cross as hollow rings, a few filled, and the rows fill in over generations.
- *East and West treated alike.* Clovis gets "loyalty and politics" and Vladimir gets "the politics were real". The lesson says it was "Frankish clergy" who objected to Slavonic worship and that the brothers "did what the western missions rarely did"; the film says "some objected" and drops the comparison, so no line sets Western against Eastern Christians. Charlemagne's forced baptisms are told plainly, and the film keeps the lesson's own answer from inside: his own scholar Alcuin protested, and "the hands that forced the Saxons were baptized hands".

**Check these.**
- The lesson quotes no verse. Zechariah 4:6 stands beside the lesson's verdict that the faith traveled best with its least armed carriers and worst with power. The line before it says "the prophet Zechariah heard this word"; in the chapter an angel speaks to Zechariah, and the word is the LORD's to Zerubbabel. The BSB text opens a quotation that closes in verse 7, so the caption shows an opening quote mark alone. Cut or swap it if you'd rather quote only what a lesson quotes.
- "By the year 1000 Europe was baptized, but not converted" could be heard as a comment on infant baptism. The lesson means baptism by command of kings, and the film follows it at once with "No king can order that", Alcuin's point, which LiveWell's mission study also makes in its "errors" section, naming Charlemagne's forced baptisms. Worth a listen with that ear.
- The map is a drawing, not data. The coastlines, the dashed Rhine–Danube frontier and Hadrian's Wall line (the "old imperial fence"), and the dots of ink that spread around each named place are symbolic. Ink spreads only around places the lesson names; Sweden, Finland, the Baltic lands and the far north-east stay pale because the lesson names no conversion there. Inside the old empire the church's dots are there from the start, except in Britain, where Kent fills in after 597.
- "Within a century, the converted were the converters, sailing to Iona and the continent" puts Columba (563) and Columbanus (around 590) about a century after Patrick, as the lesson does.
- 597 words, near the top of the 480 to 600 range.
