Rome ruled the Mediterranean for half a thousand years, then fell. Why didn't the church, tangled up in that empire, fall with it?

In 410 a Gothic army under Alaric sacked Rome. In Bethlehem, the old scholar Jerome wrote that his voice stuck in his throat.

In 476 the last western emperor, a boy, was quietly deposed, and no one bothered to appoint another. The church crossed the wreck intact.

Irish raiders took Patrick from Roman Britain at about sixteen. In six years of slavery, his family's faith became his own.

He escaped. Then, around the middle of the fifth century, he went back to his captors, carrying the gospel.

His Confession is one of the oldest writings from any victim of the slave trade, and it forgives.

Ireland had never been Roman. Within a century, the converted were the converters, sailing to Iona and the continent.

On the continent, faith came from the top. Around 496 Clovis, king of the Franks, was baptized, urged on by his Christian queen.

A chronicler says three thousand warriors followed him into the water. That's loyalty and politics, not three thousand changed hearts.

Paganism lingered for generations, yet their descendants became saints and scholars. Europe's conversion was shallow, and it deepened.

In 590 a reluctant monk, Gregory, became pope. He fed starving Rome and still looked outward.

In 597 he sent another reluctant monk, named Augustine, to Kent.

He advised sparing the temples and removing only the idols, because a stubborn people climbs to God by steps, not leaps.

Within a century the English were sending missionaries back. Boniface felled the Germans' sacred oak, and was killed in 754.

In the East, the gift was an alphabet. In 863 the brothers Cyril and Methodius gave the Slavs Scripture and worship in their own tongue.

Some objected that God could be worshiped properly only in Hebrew, Greek and Latin. Cyril answered that the rain of God falls on all alike.

In 988 Vladimir of Kyiv chose the faith of Constantinople, and the politics were real. By about 1000, even Iceland had chosen baptism.

Each tradition still carries this work, with its own emphasis.

Catholic teaching holds that the church is missionary by its nature, proclaiming Christ, planting the church, and expressing the faith in each culture's forms.

Orthodox churches point to the practice, from Cyril and Methodius onward, of putting Scripture and worship into local languages and raising up local clergy.

Many evangelical Protestants hold that the church's first task is to preach Christ crucified and risen, and call people to repentance and faith.

This era carries shame too. Charlemagne fought the pagan Saxons for three decades, and his law made refusing baptism a capital crime.

At Verden in 782, thousands of Saxon prisoners were put to death.

His scholar Alcuin protested that a man can be dragged to the font but not to belief. He was right, and he was ignored.

The hands that forced the Saxons were baptized hands.

So what kind of victory was it? The faith outlived the empire, which suggests its life never came from empire.

It traveled best with its least armed carriers, slaves and monks and queens, and worst wherever it rode with power.

No king can order that. It has to be preached, person by person.
