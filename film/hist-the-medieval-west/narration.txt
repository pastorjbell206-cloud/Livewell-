Was the medieval church a dark age, or an age of faith?

Some picture a thousand years of superstition and corrupt priests. Others, a Europe where everyone believed.

Neither survives the evidence. From 1054 to 1517, the church had its noon and its sickness at once.

This church baptized every child, married every couple and buried every body.

You could live a whole life without one important day the church didn't touch.

In 910 a duke founded a monastery at Cluny that no king or lord could control.

That mattered, because priesthoods were bought and sold. The church called it simony, after the magician in Acts who tried to buy the Spirit's power.

But Peter replied, “May your silver perish with you, because you thought you could buy the gift of God with money!

Then reform reached the papacy. Gregory the Seventh took on simony, and kings who appointed bishops.

Defied by King Henry the Fourth, Gregory excommunicated him. In 1077 Henry stood three days in the cold at Canossa, waiting to be absolved.

Reform had freed the church from kings. It had also taught it to stand above them.

By the thirteenth century, the church stood higher than any church before or since. Pope Innocent the Third made kings yield.

Gothic cathedrals rose, the first universities grew, and Thomas Aquinas taught that faith and reason are friends.

Francis of Assisi gave up everything to live with the poor, and to its credit, that church made room for him.

The same church held the sword. In 1095 Pope Urban the Second called Europe's knights to recover Jerusalem.

In 1096 crusaders massacred the Jewish communities of Worms and Mainz, and in 1099 the people of Jerusalem, Muslim and Jew alike.

In 1204 a crusade sacked Christian Constantinople. From 1231 inquisitors hunted heresy, and in 1252 a papal decree allowed torture in church courts.

How did the people of a crucified Lord come to this?

Mostly not through cynicism, but through certainty joined to power. We aren't better than they were.

The height gave way. From 1309 the popes lived at Avignon, in the French crown's shadow, for nearly seventy years.

Catherine of Siena, a dyer's daughter, went there to tell the pope to go home to Rome, and in 1377 he went.

Then rival popes reigned at Rome and Avignon, until the Council of Constance ended the schism in 1417.

That council put Jan Hus to death, though he came under a promise of safe conduct to plead for reform.

Why did the Reformation come in 1517, and not sooner? Not for lack of reformers.

Waldo and Wycliffe were condemned. Hus and Savonarola were put to death.

Reform kept arriving, and the structure kept absorbing it, or killing it.

By the 1510s, indulgences were raising money to rebuild Saint Peter's in Rome.

After Gutenberg's press, the next Hus couldn't be silenced by silencing one man.

Catholics see in Peter's successor a center that held the church together, and a church that grew its own reformers, from Cluny to Francis.

Orthodox Christians read these centuries from the East, which honored Rome as first, but as a brother, not a sovereign. The East didn't forget 1204.

Protestants see in Wycliffe and Hus a reform the church refused, and in the Reformation, God's Word reaching the people in their own tongue.

The church that did all this is the church we come from. Its height and its corruption are our inheritance, not someone else's.

And the sickness isn't medieval. Any church that confuses its power with Christ's kingdom can catch it, ours included.
