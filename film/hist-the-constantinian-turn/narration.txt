When Rome became Christian, was it the church's great victory, or the moment something went wrong?

The honest answer is both. And the question it raised has never closed.

In 312, two rivals for the western empire met at the Milvian Bridge outside Rome. Constantine won.

Lactantius says a dream told him to mark his soldiers' shields with a sign of Christ.

Eusebius, writing later, says he and his army saw a cross of light in the sky, with the words, conquer by this.

The accounts don't quite match. But he fought under the sign of a crucified man, and won.

The next year at Milan, Constantine and his eastern colleague Licinius granted Christians full legal freedom.

A decade earlier, under Diocletian, believers saw their Scriptures seized and their friends killed. Now an emperor built them churches.

We may greet that moment with suspicion. Those who lived it greeted it with tears.

It felt like resurrection.

The gains were real. The church got its buildings back, and its clergy were freed from civic duties.

Bishops became judges the state honored, often fairer to the poor. In 321, Sunday became a public day of rest.

Only an emperor could gather bishops from across the world. Nicaea met in 325 because Constantine wanted it, paid for it, and sat in it.

But there was a price tag no one read. The first cost was the emperor himself, inside the church's own business.

Constantine enforced Nicaea's creed with exile. Later emperors exiled the other side.

The second cost was the crowd. As the faith became legal, then favored, then official in 380, people poured in, some in truth, some for careers.

The third cost has a name. At Trier in the 380s, a Spanish ascetic, Priscillian, was accused of heresy and sorcery.

Urged on by fellow bishops, the imperial authorities put him to death. Christians had begun executing Christians over doctrine.

Martin of Tours protested and broke communion with the bishops responsible. The church that had suffered under the sword had picked it up.

Was Constantine sincere? The answer is disputed.

He kept the pagan title of chief priest, and the Unconquered Sun on his coins. He had his own son executed, and was baptized only on his deathbed.

Yet he endowed churches where favor bought him nothing. And deathbed baptism was common in his century.

Most historians now see a real believer, his faith and ambition tangled all the way down.

The capture was never total. In 361 the emperor Julian tried to restore the old gods, and reigned under two years.

In 390, after a riot in Thessalonica, the emperor Theodosius ordered a reprisal that killed thousands.

Ambrose, the bishop of Milan, barred him from the Lord's table until he did public penance. And he did.

For once, the church judged the state's violence instead of blessing it. Such moments were rare.

When Rome fell to Alaric in 410, pagans blamed the abandoned gods, and Augustine spent thirteen years answering.

Rome's fortunes, he argued, were never the measure of the kingdom of God. No city of man is the church's destiny.

Hebrews says the same.

For here we do not have a permanent city, but we are looking for the city that is to come.

Every generation since has met its own 312, some power offering protection in exchange for blessing.

Nothing in the fourth century says favor must corrupt. Everything in it says favor will try.

The martyrs were asked whether they'd die for their Lord. Constantine's age asked something harder.

Could the church hold power without worshiping it?
