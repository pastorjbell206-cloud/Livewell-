Many picture the early church under Rome as one long manhunt, legions combing the provinces for believers.

That picture is wrong. The real question is harder. Why would anyone die rather than say one word?

From Nero in 64 until 313, persecution was mostly local and occasional, the work of mobs and governors.

Around 112 a governor named Pliny wrote to the emperor Trajan. Trajan answered, don't seek Christians out, but punish any who are accused and won't recant.

So the church lived legal enough to grow, and illegal enough to die.

Rome didn't want agreement. It wanted a gesture. A pinch of incense, a word calling Caesar lord, and the accused walked free.

Rome had room for one more god. It couldn't tolerate refusal, and Christians would worship Jesus and no one else.

Around 165 the philosopher Justin was put to death in Rome. Asked to sacrifice, he said no right-thinking person deserts truth for falsehood.

In 177, in Lyons, a slave girl named Blandina, whom her friends thought too frail to hold out, died still confessing Christ.

In 203, in Carthage, a twenty-two-year-old mother named Perpetua was arrested with a pregnant slave, Felicity.

In her prison diary, her father begs her, weeping, to think of her nursing son and say the word.

She points to a water jug. Can it be called anything but what it is? Neither can she. She is a Christian.

She and Felicity died together. A philosopher, a slave, a young mother, and not one would say the word.

Then plague, invasion and inflation shook the empire. Emperors looking for a cause found the people who wouldn't sacrifice.

In 250 Decius ordered everyone to sacrifice and carry a certificate proving it. Many died. Many more complied.

In 258 Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, was executed. He met the sentence with the words, thanks be to God.

In 303 Diocletian ordered churches torn down, Scriptures burned, and at last everyone made to sacrifice.

In 311 a dying Galerius granted grudging toleration. Two years later Constantine made the faith legal.

Rome hadn't grown kind. It had given up.

Most Christians weren't heroes. Later the lapsed, who had given in, came back ashamed, asking to return.

A Roman priest named Novatian said they could never be restored. His case was serious. The martyrs had died rather than say the word.

Cyprian and the larger church chose a slower, harder way. The lapsed could return through real repentance, case by case.

After all, the church's own Lord had been abandoned by every man He trained.

Then everyone deserted Him and fled.

And afterward, on a beach, He fed them breakfast.

Jesus came and took the bread and gave it to them, and He did the same with the fish.

The body of Christ isn't a hall of the undefeated. It's a company of the forgiven.

Around 197 Tertullian told Rome the blood of Christians is seed. True, but deaths alone argued no one into faith.

Christians stayed in plague cities and nursed the dying, pagans too. They took in abandoned babies and seated slave beside senator.

The church refused to make dying a strategy. Courage was demanded when the hour came, never as a performance.

Within a century, Christians held the empire's power and began to reach for the sword. The people of the catacombs became the people of the courtroom.

What held the martyrs up was a settled conviction that Jesus was Lord and Caesar was not.

Most of us have never been asked their question. The era asks for more than admiration. It asks whom we worship.
