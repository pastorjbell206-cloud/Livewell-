# Whose kingdom?: each line beside its source

- **There is a moment in the life of nearly every American church.**
  - Source: There is a moment in the life of nearly every American church that reveals, with almost surgical precision, how deeply national identity has been woven into the fabric of Christian worship. It usually happens on the Sunday nearest the Fourth of July, or on the Sunday before Memorial Day, or sometimes on the Sunday after a national tragedy. The sanctuary is decorated with flags, and the worship set includes a patriotic hymn. Someone reads a passage about God's faithfulness to "this nation." A veteran is honored, and a prayer is offered for "our troops" and "our leaders" and "our way of life."

- **And in that moment, something happens that most people in the room do not notice.**
  - Source: And in that moment, something happens that most people in the room do not notice. The line between worship of God and celebration of nation becomes invisible. The emotional register of the room, the reverence, the gratitude, the sense of being in the presence of something sacred, is identical whether the congregation is singing "Holy, Holy, Holy" or "God Bless America." The tears that fall during a patriotic tribute feel the same as the tears that fall during communion. The lump in the throat when the flag is presented feels the same as the lump in the throat when the cross is lifted.

- **It has a name. Sociologists call it civil religion.**
  - Source: That emotional fusion, that unbroken blending of national feeling and spiritual devotion, is not an accident. It is the product of something that has been growing in American culture for centuries. And it has a name. Sociologists call it civil religion.

- **"God Bless America" quietly replaced "Thy Kingdom Come" as the functional prayer of the church.**
  - Source: And if you want to understand how patriotism became a practical savior for millions of American Christians, if you want to understand how "God Bless America" quietly replaced "Thy Kingdom Come" as the functional prayer of the church, you have to understand what civil religion is, where it came from, and why it is so spiritually lethal precisely because it feels so spiritually natural.

- **That is the invitation: not to stop caring about America, but to stop treating the American project as though it were the Kingdom of God.**
  - Source: That is the invitation: not to stop caring about America, not to abandon civic responsibility, not to retreat into apolitical passivity, but to stop treating the American project as though it were the Kingdom of God, to stop wrapping the cross in the flag, to stop borrowing sacred language for national purposes, and to trust that the God who has sustained His church through the fall of Rome, the splintering of Christendom, the rise and fall of empires, and the upheaval of every civilization in history is more than capable of sustaining it through whatever happens to the United States of America.

- **A church that knows it belongs to a Kingdom that cannot be shaken can face any political outcome with sorrow, yes, with concern, yes, but never with despair.**
  - Source: It gains the freedom to endure any political outcome without despair. This is perhaps the most important freedom of all. A church that has confused its identity with a nation's fortunes is a church that will be destroyed by that nation's decline. But a church that knows it belongs to a Kingdom that cannot be shaken, a Kingdom secured not by elections but by resurrection, can face any political outcome with sorrow, yes, with concern, yes, but never with despair. Because despair is only possible when the thing you have lost is ultimate. And for the church of Jesus Christ, no nation is ultimate.
