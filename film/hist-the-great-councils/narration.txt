Why would Christians fight over one word? It sounds like the smallest argument there is.

But underneath the great councils sat the largest question anyone can ask. Who is Jesus, and what does it mean to call Him God?

The church had worshiped Jesus from the beginning. But it had no agreed words yet for what it had always believed.

The whole gospel hung on it. If Jesus isn't truly God, the cross is a noble death and nothing more.

If He isn't truly human, our flesh was never touched by salvation.

The first crisis carried the name of Arius, a priest in Alexandria.

He taught, with real intelligence and piety, that the Son was the highest creature, but still a creature.

It sounded reverent. But a created savior can't bridge the gap between God and man.

The argument split the eastern churches. Crowds chanted slogans in the streets.

In 325 the emperor Constantine, wanting unity in his empire, called bishops from across the world to Nicaea.

It was the first time the scattered church had gathered like this.

They settled it. The Son is of the same substance as the Father, true God from true God, begotten, not made.

They borrowed a word that isn't in Scripture to protect what Scripture means.

In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.

Nicaea didn't end the fight. For most of the next fifty-six years, emperors backed the Arian side.

Athanasius of Alexandria, who defended Nicaea most stubbornly, was exiled five times.

The truth didn't win because it was powerful. It won slowly, against power.

By 381 a new question had surfaced. If the Son is fully God, what about the Holy Spirit?

The council at Constantinople, under the emperor Theodosius, confessed the Spirit as Lord and giver of life, worshiped and glorified with the Father and the Son.

Three teachers called the Cappadocians showed how the one God is one substance and three persons. Not three gods, and not one God wearing masks.

Then the church had to ask how one Christ could be both God and man at once.

The councils weren't pure. Rivalry between Alexandria and Antioch and imperial politics turned theology into a contest for power.

At Ephesus in 431 the dispute was whether Mary could be called the bearer of God, and so whether her child was truly God from the first moment.

Nestorius, the bishop of Constantinople, was condemned, partly for what he taught and partly for the politics that crushed him.

At Chalcedon in 451 the church reached its great settlement. Jesus Christ is one person in two natures, fully God and fully man.

The natures are united without confusion, never blended into some third thing, and without separation, never split into two sons.

Chalcedon itself didn't unite the church. Ancient churches in Egypt, Syria and the East did not accept it, and that break has never healed.

Christians still read that council differently, and that wound is real.

Yet the definitions hold. The man on the cross was God, so the love shown there is God's own.

And He was truly human, so our flesh and death were carried into God. Take either away and the gospel falls apart.

The Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us.

When you say Jesus is fully God and fully man, you stand on ground that quarreling bishops marked out under fire.

We didn't earn it. The least we owe is to know what it cost, and to hold it as carefully as they did.
