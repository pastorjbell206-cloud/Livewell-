Why are there Catholics and Protestants at all?

Much of the answer lies in one century, from 1517 to 1648.

It began with Martin Luther, a German monk who could not quiet his conscience.

He confessed, fasted and prayed, and found no rest. His answer came from Paul's letter to the Romans.

For the gospel reveals the righteousness of God that comes by faith from start to finish, just as it is written: “The righteous will live by faith.”

Luther came to believe that the righteousness God requires is the righteousness God gives, received by faith. He said it felt like walking through open gates into paradise.

In 1517 he posted ninety-five theses against the sale of indulgences, reduced time in purgatory sold to pay for church building.

The new printing press spread his words faster than anyone could answer.

Ordered to recant, he refused, saying his conscience was captive to the Word of God. He was declared an outlaw.

German princes protected him, partly from conviction, partly to resist Rome and the emperor. Faith and politics were tangled from the start.

In Zurich, Ulrich Zwingli led a second reform, and his split with Luther over the Lord's Supper never healed.

In Geneva, a French lawyer named John Calvin gave the Reformed tradition its lasting shape. It spread to France, the Netherlands and Scotland.

Geneva was a city of serious faith. It was also where a man named Servetus was put to death for heresy, a stain not to be explained away.

The Radicals said the reformers hadn't gone far enough. They baptized only those who could profess faith themselves.

Those adults had been baptized as babies, so their enemies called them Anabaptists, rebaptizers.

A church of willing believers threatened the social order, so Catholics and Protestants alike hunted them.

Many were put to death across Europe, with a courage their persecutors could not deny.

In Munster a violent minority seized the city, and it ended in disaster. That was used to justify killing the peaceful majority.

In England it began with a king. When the pope wouldn't annul his marriage, Henry the Eighth made himself head of the English church.

His son pressed reform forward. His daughter Mary forced England back toward Rome, and Protestants were put to death.

Elizabeth the First settled on a middle way, Protestant in doctrine but keeping much of the old worship. In time it produced the Anglican tradition.

The Catholic Church answered with the Council of Trent, which met across the middle of the century.

It reaffirmed the authority of tradition alongside Scripture, and the place of the sacraments and good works in salvation.

It corrected real abuses and reformed the training of clergy. A new order, the Jesuits, carried the faith to Asia and the Americas.

No side's story is clean. Catholics killed Protestants, Protestants killed Catholics, and both killed the Radicals.

Wars of religion ravaged France and the Low Countries. The Thirty Years War devastated Germany, until exhaustion forced peace in 1648.

Western Christianity was torn into pieces never put back together.

Many Protestants see here a recovery of what had been buried: free grace, the authority of Scripture, and God's Word in the people's own tongue.

Catholics see at Trent the church renewing itself from within, holding Scripture and tradition together, and teaching that grace comes first and no one can earn it.

And free churches trace believers' baptism and the refusal to force faith back to the Radicals.

Whatever church you call home, you're an heir of this century.

The church needed renewal, and it should mourn the violence of men who were sure they served God.

We are heirs of both.
